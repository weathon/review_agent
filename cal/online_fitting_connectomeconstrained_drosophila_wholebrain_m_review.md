# Final Consolidated Review
## Summary
This paper presents an online gradient-based training framework for fitting a synapse-resolved, connectome-constrained whole-brain *Drosophila* rate model to resting-state calcium imaging data. The central technical contribution is the use of D-RTRL-style online updates to make training feasible for a 138k-neuron network on a single GPU, and the paper further argues that the trained model reproduces held-out neuropil dynamics while exhibiting emergent heavy-tailed weights and near-critical dynamics.

## Strengths
- **The paper tackles a genuinely hard scaling bottleneck with a concrete systems-level solution.** The core technical contribution is not just “using online learning,” but demonstrating it in a connectome-constrained recurrent model at fly whole-brain scale: 138,639 neurons and 15,091,982 synaptic connections. The memory-scaling result in Fig. 2A directly supports the claim that the online method removes dependence on sequence length in a way that matters in practice for this target setting.
- **The anatomical readout is a meaningful modeling choice rather than a generic decoder.** Instead of learning an unconstrained output head by default, the paper maps single-neuron activity to neuropil activity using synapse-weighted anatomical contributions within each neuropil (Section 3.6). The comparison against an unconstrained linear readout in Fig. 2C is a specific and useful result showing that this anatomical prior improves optimization behavior.
- **The model does show nontrivial autoregressive behavior beyond immediate teacher-forced fitting.** In Section 4.2 and Fig. 3A, the model is trained on the first 500 steps and then simulated beyond that window, and the authors report that the held-out segment retains structured oscillatory dynamics and nontrivial FC agreement. While the extent of generalization should be characterized more carefully, this is stronger than a purely one-step prediction result.
- **The paper is ambitious in integrating structure, dynamics, and optimization into one framework.** The combination of FlyWire connectivity, neuron-level recurrent dynamics, neuropil-level experimental targets, and online gradient fitting is more integrated than standard macroscopic whole-brain models or unconstrained RNN baselines. The novelty is therefore substantial at the level of system construction and computational methodology.

## Weaknesses

###: Fatal
None.

### Major:
- **The criticality claim is substantially under-supported by the current analysis.** The paper’s strongest biological claim is that optimization “drives the network toward” critical resting-state dynamics (Abstract, Section 4.4). However, the evidence relies on log-log linear fits of avalanche duration distributions from relatively short sequences and reports only fitted exponents and \(R^2\) values (Fig. 5A-B; Appendix J.1-J.2). The analysis does **not** include standard statistical validation steps needed to distinguish a power law from plausible alternatives (e.g., log-normal or exponential tails), nor does it report sensitivity to thresholding choices, cutoff selection, or preprocessing. Since this claim is central to the paper’s biological interpretation, the current evidence is not strong enough to support it at the level stated.
- **The mechanistic interpretation via spectral radius is too strong for the model actually studied.** Section 4.4 argues that as training progresses, the spectral radius of the recurrent matrix approaches 1, and interprets this as evidence that the system is moving “toward critical dynamics.” But the actual model is not just an autonomous linear recurrent system: it includes a learned, nonlinear, dense background/input pathway \(I_i^{[\mathrm{enc}]}\) driven by neuropil-level activity through Eq. (3). In this setting, the spectral radius of the recurrent connectivity alone is at best a partial heuristic, not a sufficient mechanistic explanation for the operating regime. This does not make the spectral-radius observation useless, but the paper overstates what it establishes.
- **The claimed alignment between learned weights and connectome statistics is suggestive but not yet conceptually convincing.** Section 4.3 compares learned continuous effective weights \(w_{ij}\) against anatomical synapse-count distributions from FlyWire, after rescaling the model weights to the same numerical range before Q-Q analysis. This does show that training produces a heavier-tailed distribution than initialization, but the stronger conclusion—that optimization spontaneously recovers empirical connectome statistics—is not fully justified. Anatomical synapse counts and learned effective couplings are different quantities, and the post-hoc rescaling weakens the force of the distributional match as evidence for a deep structure-function principle. This result should be framed more cautiously as a distributional resemblance, not a validated recovery of anatomical statistics.
- **The role of the learned neuropil feedback / background-input term is insufficiently disentangled from the role of the connectome.** Equation (3) introduces a learned mapping from previous-step neuropil activity to per-neuron input, with one weight per neuropil-neuron pair and a ReLU nonlinearity. This is a powerful global feedback pathway layered on top of the sparse connectome. Yet the paper does not ablate this component, vary its parameterization, or test whether the reported emergent properties and held-out performance depend primarily on this dense feedback rather than on the connectome-constrained recurrent structure. Because the scientific pitch emphasizes connectome-constrained whole-brain modeling, this missing disentanglement is a notable gap.
- **The empirical support for online-vs-BPTT optimization quality is narrower than the paper’s framing suggests.** The paper does convincingly show the memory advantage of online training on the target scale, but the optimization-quality comparison to BPTT in Fig. 2B is done only on a synthetic low-rank recurrent surrogate (Appendix C), not on the full connectome-constrained model. Given that the full model is exactly where the paper wants to claim practical superiority, the current evidence supports the scaling advantage more strongly than it supports comparable optimization behavior on the true target architecture.

### Minor
- **Generalization evidence is limited in scope.** The main demonstration trains on the first 500 time steps and evaluates on the next 500 within the same recording. That is a useful first test, but the paper mentions 18 brains in Section 3.2 and does not show cross-brain validation or broader robustness across animals/preparations. As written, the significance claim would be stronger if the learned dynamics were shown to generalize across biological variability, not just across a contiguous held-out segment.
- **The reported held-out FC result is promising but somewhat overstated in the text.** A test FC correlation of 0.556 is nontrivial, but calling this “strongly aligned” is stronger than the evidence supports, especially for a headline whole-brain modeling result. The paper would benefit from more granular analysis of where the model succeeds or fails across neuropils, and from additional baselines beyond the small GRU.
- **The baseline suite is informative but not fully calibrated.** The GRU baseline in Appendix F is much less biologically structured and likely not capacity- or inductive-bias-matched to the proposed model. Its failure to maintain realistic dynamics is suggestive, but it does not isolate which ingredient matters most: connectome topology, anatomical readout, online training, or simply scale and parameterization.
- **The coarse temporal resolution of the data is a real limitation for the dynamical interpretation.** The experimental recordings are sampled at 1.2 Hz (Section 3.2), and then deconvolved to produce rate targets. This is acceptable for the paper’s stated fitting task, but it limits how strongly one should interpret learned cellular time constants or avalanche timing as biologically precise dynamical quantities. The paper could acknowledge this more explicitly.

### Trivial
- **The paper would benefit from clearer reporting of the training/evaluation protocol around teacher forcing vs. autonomous rollout.** Appendix L suggests a two-stage training pipeline with warm-up and prediction phases, and Appendix F uses a 250-step warm-up before autonomous prediction for baseline evaluation. Making the exact protocol more explicit in the main text would make the generalization claims easier to assess.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Add an ablation removing or simplifying Eq. (3) to isolate how much of the performance and emergent behavior comes from the dense neuropil feedback term versus the sparse connectome.
- Evaluate BPTT on a large anatomically faithful FlyWire subgraph rather than only on the low-rank surrogate, to better connect the optimization comparison to the intended application.
- Strengthen the criticality section with proper statistical tests: MLE-based fitting, goodness-of-fit testing, likelihood-ratio comparisons against alternatives, and threshold/preprocessing sensitivity analysis.
- Show cross-animal validation across the 18 brains, or at least multiple train/test splits across animals, to support claims of broader significance.
- Provide direct plots of predicted vs. ground-truth neuropil trajectories on held-out windows for diverse regions, not just FC summaries.
- Reframe the weight-distribution result around rank/order or shape similarity without relying on post-hoc range matching, and if possible relate learned effective weights to anatomy in a more direct edgewise or groupwise manner.

## Novel Insights
The most important synthesis across the reviews is that this paper is strongest as a **computational-methods paper with an ambitious neuroscience application**, and weaker as a paper claiming robust biological discovery about criticality and recovered anatomical statistics. The online optimization contribution appears real and significant, and the connectome-constrained construction is unusually substantive. But the same modeling choice that makes fitting possible—the learned neuropil-to-neuron background pathway—also muddies the biological attribution story, because it introduces a powerful dense feedback mechanism that is not cleanly separated from the recurrent connectome. As a result, the paper’s central scientific tension is not whether it can fit the data at scale, but whether the observed emergent phenomena can be attributed to connectome-constrained optimization rather than to flexible global feedback plus generic recurrent training dynamics.

## Suggestions
- Reposition the paper’s main claim around **scalable online fitting of a connectome-constrained whole-brain model**, which is already a strong contribution, and temper the biological claims about “spontaneous criticality” and “recovered connectome statistics” unless substantially stronger evidence is added.
- Add a focused ablation of Eq. (3): remove it, sparsify it, randomize it, or replace it with lower-capacity alternatives, and report the effect on held-out dynamics, FC, spectral radius, and weight-tail emergence.
- Upgrade the criticality analysis with statistically defensible methodology and robustness checks; otherwise present it as preliminary evidence suggestive of critical-like dynamics rather than a validated conclusion.
- Replace or supplement the weight-distribution comparison with analyses that do not depend on range rescaling, and clarify that the learned \(w_{ij}\) are effective couplings rather than direct anatomical synapse counts.
- Include stronger validation of generalization, ideally across multiple flies, and broaden the baseline/ablation suite to better isolate which ingredients are actually essential.
- Clarify in the main text exactly when training/evaluation uses ground-truth previous-step neuropil activity versus autonomous model predictions, since this affects how readers interpret the generalization claims.
