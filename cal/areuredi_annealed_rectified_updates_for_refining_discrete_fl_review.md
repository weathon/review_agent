# Final Consolidated Review
## Summary
AReUReDi extends Rectified Discrete Flows (ReDi) to multi-objective optimization by integrating annealed Tchebycheff scalarization, locally balanced proposals, and Metropolis-Hastings updates, providing theoretical guarantees of invariance and convergence to the Pareto front. The framework is demonstrated on peptide and SMILES sequence design, simultaneously optimizing up to five therapeutic properties and outperforming evolutionary and diffusion-based baselines.

## Strengths
- **Novel integration of discrete flow rectification with principled multi-objective guidance.** The combination of Tchebycheff scalarization (which can reach non-convex Pareto points), locally balanced proposals (preserving reversibility w.r.t. a target distribution), and MH acceptance is a coherent and well-motivated algorithmic design. The proof that Barker's balancing function yields acceptance probability 1 (Appendix A) is a clean theoretical result that directly connects the proposal mechanism to sampling efficiency.
- **Comprehensive ablation studies validating each component.** Tables 7–10 and Tables 13–16 systematically ablate individual guidance objectives, rectification rounds, annealing schedules, weight vectors, and the role of the learned prior. The ablation showing that removing any single guidance signal causes collapse in the corresponding property (Table 7–8) is particularly convincing evidence that AReUReDi is steering toward genuine trade-offs rather than trivially maximizing one objective.
- **Coverage of diverse target types and sequence modalities.** The evaluation spans structured proteins with/without known binders, intrinsically disordered targets, wild-type amino acid sequences, and chemically-modified peptide SMILES—demonstrating generality beyond a narrow task setting.

## Weaknesses
- **The monotonicity constraint used in all experiments breaks the theoretical guarantees.** Section 4 states that a "monotonicity constraint that accepts only token updates that increase the weighted sum of the current objective scores" was used in all experiments. This constraint replaces the MH acceptance probability with a greedy filter, violating detailed balance and invalidating the invariance and convergence theorems of Appendix A. The paper claims this constraint does not alter "the underlying optimization objectives," but altering the stationary distribution is precisely the concern—not the objectives themselves. No theoretical or empirical characterization of what the monotonicity constraint does to the stationary distribution is provided. This creates a fundamental disconnect: the algorithm proven correct is not the algorithm evaluated. This matters because the greedy constraint may sacrifice Pareto front *coverage* (diversity of trade-off solutions) for faster convergence to a single trade-off point, which would undermine the core "full coverage" claim.
- **No standard multi-objective evaluation metrics or Pareto front visualization.** The paper claims "superior trade-off navigation" and "full coverage of the Pareto front," but reports only mean property scores across 100 generated sequences. Established MOO metrics—hypervolume indicator, generational distance, spread, inverted generational distance—are absent. Without hypervolume, it is impossible to assess whether AReUReDi's Pareto front dominates alternatives. Furthermore, there are no 2D/3D Pareto front scatter plots showing trade-off curves against baselines. For example, in Table 2 (target 1B8Q), SPEA2 achieves affinity of 7.3240 vs. AReUReDi's 5.7130; without seeing the front, one cannot tell whether AReUReDi's solutions are Pareto-dominated on this objective or represent a deliberate trade-off. Mean scores alone conflate front quality with individual property maximization.
- **The coverage guarantee requires randomizing ω, but experiments use fixed weights.** The Coverage Guarantee (Theorem 4, Appendix A.2) explicitly requires ω to be sampled from a distribution with full support on the simplex. Yet Section F states that "the same weight for each objective" was used for all wild-type tasks, and fixed unequal weights for SMILES tasks. With a single fixed ω, Tchebycheff scalarization converges to a *single* point on the Pareto front (the point maximizing min_n ω_n s̃_n(x)), not the full front. This directly contradicts the "full coverage" claim and means the theoretical coverage guarantee was never empirically tested.
- **The half-life score model is trained on only 105 sequences.** Appendix E.3 reports that the half-life dataset (after deduplication) contains only 105 entries. A model fine-tuned on this few samples—however well pre-trained on stability data—risks severe overfitting, and optimizing against it risks "proxy hacking": finding sequences that exploit the model's noise rather than reflecting genuine half-life improvement. Since half-life is one of five core objectives and its scores are prominently reported (e.g., 42–64 hour half-lives in Table 1), the reliability of this proxy is a material concern.
- **Missing comparison against the closest methodological baselines.** The paper compares against classical MOO algorithms (NSGA-III, SPEA2, etc.) and PepTune, but not against guided discrete flow methods (e.g., Nisonoff et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025a) adapted to multi-objective via scalarization. These are the closest methodological relatives—single-objective guided discrete flows with scalarization would isolate the contribution of AReUReDi's specific components (rectification, locally balanced proposals, MH) from the generic idea of using guided discrete flows for MOO.
- **Comparison with PepTune uses an adapted backbone.** Table 2 compares against "PepTune + DPLM," where PepTune's backbone is replaced with DPLM. The paper notes this adaptation was necessary for wild-type peptide generation, but this means the comparison is not against PepTune as published and designed. The large performance gap (e.g., non-fouling 0.31 vs. 0.87) may partially reflect the backbone substitution rather than a pure algorithmic advantage. While the matched wall-clock comparison (Table 11) is a laudable effort, comparing top-2 of 4 AReUReDi samples against top-2 of 100 PepTune samples remains statistically fragile given the small sample size on the AReUReDi side.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Wet-lab or high-fidelity simulation validation of generated peptides. While in silico optimization is the stated scope of this ML paper, biological validation would substantially strengthen the practical impact claims.
- Analysis of how uncertainty in proxy score models propagates to final sequence quality, and whether the method is robust to miscalibrated oracles.
- Detailed breakdown of computational latency sources (MCMC steps vs. flow inference vs. score model evaluation) and potential optimizations (e.g., parallel coordinate updates, batch score evaluations).
- Comparison with continuous-space flow methods (e.g., ParetoFlow) even via approximate latent-space projection, to contextualize the discrete vs. continuous trade-off.

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.*
- **"No biological/wet-lab validation" as a core weakness.** This is scope creep: the paper is an ML methods contribution at ICLR, not a wet-lab biology study. All property scores are clearly identified as proxy model outputs. This is standard for the venue and field. (Moved to Nice-to-Have.)
- **"Self-contained benchmark is circular."** The paper explicitly states no public benchmark exists and creates one. This is unavoidable in a nascent area; the authors use independently published datasets (PepNN, BioLip2, PPIRef) for training and validated score models (Appendix E). While not ideal, calling it "circular" overstates the concern.
- **"Statistical significance testing on benchmark comparisons."** While confidence intervals or significance tests would be welcome, the paper reports averages over 100 samples and shows standard deviations in Figure 1E. For ICLR-scale experiments in generative modeling for biology, this is within community norms.
- **"η→∞ in theory vs. η_max=20 in practice."** The paper already acknowledges that "these guarantees hold only in the limit of an infinitely long Markov chain." This is the standard caveat for all annealed MCMC methods; it does not represent a novel oversight.

## Novel Insights
The most interesting structural observation across the reviews is that AReUReDi occupies a middle ground between two established paradigms—MCMC-based Pareto sampling and gradient-guided hill climbing—without fully committing to either. The theoretical framework is MCMC (preserving detailed balance for distributional guarantees), but the practical implementation (monotonicity constraint + annealed guidance) behaves more like annealed hill-climbing with a learned prior. This hybrid status is arguably the method's real strength: the ReDi prior provides a strong generative inductive bias that makes even greedy local search effective in high-dimensional discrete spaces, while the Tchebycheff scalarization provides balanced multi-objective pressure. The paper's value may lie less in the MCMC guarantees and more in demonstrating that rectified discrete flows produce priors good enough that even simple greedy guidance suffices for strong multi-objective trade-offs—a finding that the current presentation obscures by anchoring the narrative to theoretical guarantees that the experiments don't actually test.

## Suggestions
- **Run the MH-acceptance variant experimentally and compare with the monotonicity constraint.** Report both Pareto front quality (hypervolume) and coverage diversity. This would clarify whether the monotonicity constraint is a harmless efficiency trick or whether it materially damages front coverage, and would either validate or revise the theoretical framing.
- **Add hypervolume and Pareto front scatter plots.** At minimum, plot pairwise objective trade-offs (e.g., affinity vs. half-life) for AReUReDi and baselines, and compute hypervolume relative to a reference point. This is the standard evaluation in the MOO community and is essential for the claimed contribution.
- **Run experiments with randomized ω to test the coverage guarantee.** Sample multiple weight vectors from the simplex, generate sequences for each, and assess whether the union of solutions spans the Pareto front. This would empirically validate or refute the "full coverage" claim.
- **Report acceptance rates and mixing diagnostics.** Even with the monotonicity constraint, reporting how often proposals are accepted and how the chain explores would help readers assess whether the sampler is well-behaved or largely stuck.
- **Reframe the narrative around the monotonicity constraint.** Either (a) prove that the monotonicity constraint preserves a modified stationary distribution with analogous convergence properties, or (b) honestly present the MCMC framework as the theoretical ideal and the monotonicity constraint as a practical heuristic that works well empirically but sacrifices the formal guarantees. Option (b) would be more honest and still leaves a strong paper.

**Evaluation on key axes:**
- **Novelty:** High. The integration of rectified discrete flows with Tchebycheff scalarization and locally balanced MCMC proposals for multi-objective discrete optimization is genuinely new and well-motivated.
- **Technical soundness:** Moderate. The theoretical results are correct for the MCMC variant, but the gap between the proven algorithm and the evaluated algorithm (monotonicity constraint breaking detailed balance) is a significant concern that is not adequately addressed.
- **Empirical support:** Moderate-to-high for demonstrating that the method works in practice; moderate for validating the specific theoretical claims (coverage, convergence). Missing standard MOO metrics and Pareto front visualization weakens the empirical case for "superior trade-off navigation."
- **Significance:** High for computational biology and therapeutic design. Multi-objective discrete sequence optimization with flow-based priors addresses a real bottleneck, and the empirical improvements over baselines are substantial.
- **Clarity:** Good. The paper is well-organized with clear algorithmic description, though the inconsistency between Algorithm 1 (MH acceptance) and the experimental section (monotonicity constraint) creates confusion about what was actually run.
