# Harsh Critic Review
## Section-by-Section Critical Review

**Title & Abstract**
The title clearly and accurately reflects the paper's core contribution: investigating the consistency of answer sets generated by LLMs for enumeration questions. The abstract succinctly summarizes the problem, approach, key findings (pervasive inconsistency, mitigation via prompting), and contributions (benchmark, metrics, analysis). All claims made in the abstract are substantiated in the paper.

**Introduction & Motivation**
The problem is exceptionally well-motivated with a clear, concrete example (Table 1) that illustrates the phenomenon of answer-set inconsistency. The connection to established database concepts (query containment/equivalence) provides a strong theoretical foundation. The five research questions (RQs) are clearly enumerated and guide the entire paper. The four stated contributions are substantial and accurately reflect the work presented.

**Method / Approach**
*Definitions (Sec. 3.1):* The formal definitions of answer-set consistency and contradiction are precise and form a solid basis for the evaluation.
*Dataset (ASCB) Construction (Sec. 3.2 & App. B):* The process of creating the 600-quadruple benchmark is described in detail. The use of existing KGQA datasets and LLM-assisted generation followed by extensive manual curation is a reasonable approach. A major concern is the potential for bias or artifacts introduced by using LLMs (GPT-4.1) in the generation and filtering pipeline, even with manual revision. The authors acknowledge that questions were "heavily modified," but the degree of influence from the generating LLM's own biases on the final dataset is unclear and a limitation.
*Evaluation Tasks & Metrics (Sec. 3.3 & 3.4):* The three tasks (Base, CtE, Oracle) are well-designed to isolate different capabilities and test mitigation strategies. The metrics (classification accuracy, consistency rate, Jaccard similarity) are appropriate. The inclusion of a control relation (\(E_{1,*}\)) to estimate the impact of pure stochasticity is a sophisticated and valuable design choice. The use of the McNemar test for significance testing is suitable for the paired, binary outcomes.
*Potential Gaps/Questions:* 1) The prompt for the Enumeration task (App. A.1) instructs the model to "return an exhaustive list." However, the evaluation does not penalize incompleteness unless it causes an inconsistency. The paper would be strengthened by discussing how answer completeness (a traditional QA metric) interacts with the new consistency metric. 2) For the CtE task, the conversational context links classification and enumeration, but the exact prompt transition is not shown. It is crucial to know if the model's own classification output is fed back as part of the context for enumeration, or if it's just prompted to "think about" the relation.

**Experiments & Results**
*Scope:* The evaluation of 18 models across multiple families is comprehensive and appropriate for a top-tier conference.
*Results (Sec. 4 & Tables 3, 5):* The key findings are clearly presented: high rates of inconsistency across all models and relations, with ternary relations (\(E_{4,1\backslash 3}\)) being most difficult. The classification results (App. D) show that top models can recognize relations with high accuracy (~90%), creating a striking contrast with their poor consistency rates—this is a central and compelling result.
*Analysis:* The separation of factors via the control relation (\(E_{1,*}\)) is insightful, showing that semantic misunderstanding is a key driver for complex relations beyond stochasticity. The hypothesis testing (Sec. 4.3, App. E) robustly supports \(H_1\) (CtE/Oracle improve consistency).
*Concerns & Missing Analysis:*
1.  **Interpretation of High %IDK:** For some models/strategies (e.g., GPT-5 Base with 32% IDK, CtE often higher than Base), the improved consistency rate could be partially an artifact of the model refusing to answer ("idk") rather than generating consistent answers. The paper notes this but should analyze it more deeply. A model that always says "idk" would have perfect consistency but zero utility. The trade-off between consistency and coverage/completeness should be discussed as a key limitation of the mitigation strategy.
2.  **CtE vs. Oracle Performance:** In several cases (noted in Sec. 4.2 and visible in Fig. 1c), CtE outperforms Oracle. The authors offer a plausible explanation (the act of classifying may force useful reasoning), but this surprising result deserves more rigorous investigation. Is it because the Oracle prompt is less effective? Or does the act of prediction genuinely improve subsequent generation? An ablation study on the CtE prompt structure could be informative.
3.  **Error Analysis (App. H):** The provided categories (terminology, completeness, logic, uncertainty) are useful but somewhat superficial. A quantitative breakdown of error types across models/relations would significantly strengthen the causal analysis.
4.  **Baselines:** While the paper compares many models, it lacks a simple, non-LLM baseline (e.g., a rule-based system or a retriever over a known KB for the factual questions) to contextualize how far LLMs are from a system designed for consistency. This would help calibrate the "far from perfect" claim.

**Writing & Clarity**
The paper is generally well-written and logically structured. The definitions are clear, and the flow from motivation to method to results is smooth. Some sections in the appendices (e.g., the multi-agent pipeline description in B.2) are dense but necessary for reproducibility. The formatting artifacts from PDF extraction are noticeable (e.g., misaligned tables, broken references like `~~1~~`) but do not impede understanding, as instructed.

**Limitations & Broader Impact**
The limitations section (Sec. 5) appropriately notes the English-only, static, single-turn scope of the dataset and the need for more advanced mitigation strategies. It could be strengthened by explicitly mentioning the potential dataset bias from LLM-assisted generation and the coverage-consistency trade-off inherent in the "idk" strategy. Broader impact is briefly alluded to (users should exercise caution), but a fuller discussion on the societal implications of unreliable enumeration from widely deployed AI systems would be valuable. The positive impact of providing a benchmark for improving reliability is clear.

### Overall Assessment
This is a strong, well-executed paper that makes a clear contribution suitable for ICLR. It identifies and rigorously formalizes an important, underexplored problem (LLM inconsistency for set-valued answers), provides a valuable new benchmark (ASCB), and conducts a thorough empirical analysis with insightful findings (e.g., the disconnect between relation recognition and answer consistency). The proposed prompting mitigation is simple but effective and analytically justified. The main weaknesses are the potential confounds in the dataset creation process and the need for a more nuanced analysis of the "idk" results and the surprising CtE vs. Oracle performance. These issues do not undermine the core contribution but should be addressed to strengthen the paper. The work provides both a practical diagnostic tool and a direction for improving LLM reliability.

# Neutral Reviewer
## Balanced Review

### Summary
This paper introduces and formalizes the problem of "answer-set inconsistency" in Large Language Models (LLMs) for factual enumeration questions, where models generate answers that violate expected set-theoretic relations (e.g., equivalence, containment) between related questions. The authors create a handcrafted benchmark dataset (ASCB) of 600 question quadruples, propose metrics to quantify inconsistency, and conduct an extensive evaluation of 18 state-of-the-art LLMs. Results show pervasive inconsistency across models, even when they can correctly identify the underlying relations. The paper also proposes and evaluates prompting-based mitigation strategies (classification-then-enumeration and oracle correction), which significantly improve consistency.

### Strengths
1. **Well-defined problem and formalization**: The paper clearly defines "answer-set consistency" and "answer-set contradiction" for enumeration questions, grounding the issue in set-theoretic relations. This formalization provides a solid foundation for analysis and benchmarking.
2. **Novel, high-quality benchmark dataset**: The creation of the ASCB dataset (600 quadruples, 2400 questions) is a significant contribution. The dataset is carefully handcrafted from multiple sources, ensuring crisp, objective questions with well-defined relations. The inclusion of diverse relations (equivalence, containment, disjointness, ternary set difference) allows for comprehensive evaluation.
3. **Extensive and rigorous evaluation**: The paper evaluates 18 diverse LLMs across three tasks (base, classification-then-enumeration, oracle) and multiple metrics (consistency rate, Jaccard similarity, classification accuracy). Statistical significance testing (McNemar) is appropriately used to validate improvements from mitigation strategies. The analysis includes a control relation (E1,*) to separate stochastic from semantic causes of inconsistency.
4. **Effective mitigation strategies**: The proposed prompting strategies, particularly "classification-then-enumeration" (CtE), demonstrate statistically significant improvements in consistency across most models. This provides a practical, immediately applicable method to enhance LLM reliability for enumeration QA.
5. **Insightful analysis and discussion**: The paper answers its five research questions clearly, identifying that (1) inconsistency is widespread, (2) large models can recognize relations well, (3) containment and n-ary relations are hardest, (4) stochasticity and semantic misunderstanding are key causes, and (5) prompting mitigates inconsistency. The discussion of limitations and future work is thoughtful.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited depth in error analysis**: While the paper identifies high-level causes (stochasticity, semantic misunderstanding), a more granular categorization and analysis of specific error types (e.g., terminological variation, partial enumeration, logical misreasoning) is lacking. Appendix H lists some patterns but could be expanded with quantitative breakdowns.
2. **Dataset construction relies heavily on manual effort**: The ASCB dataset is handcrafted, which ensures quality but limits scale and may introduce bias. The paper mentions automated generation but notes heavy manual revision was required. This raises questions about the feasibility of scaling the benchmark or adapting it to other domains/languages.
3. **No comparison to non-LLM baselines**: The evaluation focuses solely on LLMs. Including a comparison to a traditional retrieval-based system (e.g., executing SPARQL queries on a knowledge graph) would help contextualize LLM performance and highlight the unique challenges of generative models.
4. **Incomplete exploration of mitigation strategies**: The proposed strategies are prompting-based. The paper mentions but does not explore more advanced methods like structured representation parsing, neuro-symbolic integration, or fine-tuning for consistency, which are noted as future work but could have been preliminarily tested.
5. **Potential dataset artifacts**: Although the dataset is handcrafted, there may be unintended biases in question selection or relation construction (e.g., the noted greater difficulty of N4,1 over N3,1). A more detailed analysis of dataset characteristics (e.g., domain distribution, lexical complexity) would strengthen the benchmark.

### Novelty & Significance
**Novelty**: The paper addresses an underexplored aspect of LLM inconsistency—consistency across enumeration questions with set-theoretic relations—which differs from prior work on single-answer factual consistency, propositional logic, or preference transitivity. The formalization, benchmark, and relation-specific analysis are novel contributions.
**Significance**: The work is highly relevant for the ICLR community, as it tackles a critical reliability issue in LLMs used for knowledge-intensive tasks. The findings reveal fundamental limitations in current models' ability to maintain logical coherence, which is essential for trustworthy AI. The benchmark provides a valuable tool for future research, and the mitigation strategies offer practical insights. The paper aligns with ICLR's focus on understanding and improving foundational model capabilities.

### Suggestions for Improvement
1. **Deepen error analysis**: Include a quantitative breakdown of inconsistency types (e.g., terminological mismatch, missing elements, extra elements) across relations and models. This would provide clearer diagnostic insights for model improvement.
2. **Explore automated dataset expansion**: Propose or implement a semi-automated pipeline to scale the ASCB to more languages, domains, or complex relations (e.g., cardinality constraints), reducing manual effort while maintaining quality.
3. **Add non-LLM baseline**: Compare LLM performance against a retrieval-based system (e.g., using Wikidata SPARQL queries) on the same questions to benchmark how far LLMs are from "perfect" consistency achievable with structured methods.
4. **Experiment with advanced mitigation techniques**: Extend the evaluation to include preliminary results on mitigation via fine-tuning (e.g., on consistency-aware data) or neuro-symbolic approaches, as suggested in the future work.
5. **Enhance dataset analysis**: Provide more detailed statistics on the ASCB dataset (domain distribution, answer set size ranges, lexical/syntactic diversity) to help users understand its scope and potential biases.

# Spark Finder Review
## How to Improve This Paper

### Missing Experiments (top 3-5 only)
1. **Ablation/comparison with established consistency methods (e.g., self-consistency, chain-of-thought).** Without this, it's unclear if the improvements from CtE are due to the specific relational reasoning or simply the effect of additional computation/decoding steps, undermining the claim about the strategy's effectiveness.
2. **Evaluation on out-of-distribution or more complex logical relations (e.g., transitive containment, cardinality constraints).** The benchmark is handcrafted and may not generalize. Testing on adversarial or compositional relations is needed to assess the robustness of the findings and mitigation strategies.
3. **Comparison to a non-LLM baseline (e.g., a rule-based or retrieval system).** This would establish a sensible performance floor/ceiling and contextualize how "hard" the task is, making the LLMs' inconsistency levels more interpretable.
4. **Breakdown of consistency by answer set size.** The dataset includes sets of 2-100 answers, but no analysis shows if inconsistency scales with set size. This is critical for practical implications.

### Deeper Analysis Needed (top 3-5 only)
1. **Detailed error analysis categorizing failure modes per relation type.** The paper attributes inconsistency to stochasticity and semantic misunderstanding but does not qualitatively analyze *how* models fail (e.g., due to entity ambiguity, knowledge gaps, misinterpretation of negation). This is essential for understanding the root causes.
2. **Correlation analysis between relation classification accuracy and answer-set consistency.** The paper reports both metrics but does not investigate if models that are better at recognizing relations are also more consistent in their answers. This directly addresses RQ4 on causal factors.
3. **Systematic analysis of how model size, architecture, and training data correlate with inconsistency.** The observation that newer/bigger models don't always outperform is not explored. Factors like parameter count, MoE vs. dense, or pre-training data mixture should be analyzed to explain performance variation.
4. **Discussion of benchmark limitations and potential biases.** The handcrafted dataset may have domain skews or simplified relations. A critical discussion of its coverage and how that might affect the generalizability of results is needed for the benchmark to be trusted.

### Visualizations & Case Studies
1. **Concrete, annotated examples of answer-set contradictions.** Showing specific question pairs where a model's enumerated answers violate its own predicted relation (or the gold relation) would make the abstract problem tangible and illustrate common failure patterns.
2. **Visualizations of answer set overlaps (e.g., Venn diagrams) for a sample of cases.** For containment and disjointness relations, seeing the actual generated sets would instantly reveal the nature and severity of inconsistencies beyond aggregate metrics.
3. **Case studies of where the CtE mitigation fails.** The paper notes CtE sometimes underperforms Base. Presenting specific instances where classification fails or leads to worse enumeration would clarify the limitations of the proposed strategy.

### Obvious Next Steps
1. **Compare to a self-consistency baseline (majority voting over multiple samples).** This is a standard baseline for improving consistency in LLMs. Its omission makes it difficult to judge the novelty and effectiveness of the proposed prompting strategies.
2. **Pilot experiments using structured representations (e.g., LLM-generated SPARQL queries).** The paper mentions this as future work, but a preliminary result showing that converting questions to a structured form (and possibly using a deterministic reasoner) improves consistency would significantly strengthen the narrative.
3. **Test prompt variations for the mitigation strategies.** The prompts are fixed. Experimenting with different phrasings (e.g., more explicit instructions to enforce consistency) would help determine the sensitivity and optimize the approach.
4. **Expand model coverage to include more recent open-source models (e.g., Qwen2.5, Phi-3, Command R).** The model list is good but not exhaustive. Including a broader set, especially strong open-source models, would make the survey more comprehensive and authoritative.

# Final Consolidated Review
## Summary
This paper formalizes "answer-set inconsistency" in LLMs for factual enumeration questions, where models generate answers that violate expected set-theoretic relations (e.g., equivalence, containment). The authors create a handcrafted benchmark (ASCB) of 600 question quadruples, propose metrics to quantify inconsistency, and evaluate 18 state-of-the-art LLMs. Results show pervasive inconsistency, even when models correctly recognize relations, and demonstrate that prompting strategies (classification-then-enumeration and oracle correction) significantly improve consistency.

## Strengths
- **Clear formalization and problem definition:** The paper provides precise definitions of answer-set consistency and contradiction, grounding the issue in set-theoretic relations and connecting it to database concepts (query containment/equivalence).
- **High-quality benchmark dataset:** The ASCB dataset (600 quadruples, 2400 questions) is carefully constructed from multiple sources with manual curation, ensuring objective, crisp enumeration questions covering equivalence, containment, disjointness, and ternary relations.
- **Rigorous and extensive evaluation:** The evaluation of 18 LLMs across three tasks (Base, Classification-then-Enumeration, Oracle) with multiple metrics (consistency rate, Jaccard similarity, classification accuracy) is comprehensive. The use of a control relation (\(E_{1,*}\)) to separate stochastic from semantic causes is insightful, and statistical testing (McNemar) robustly validates improvements.
- **Effective and analyzable mitigation strategies:** The proposed prompting strategies, especially Classification-then-Enumeration (CtE), yield statistically significant consistency gains across most models. The analysis reveals that inconsistency stems from both stochasticity and semantic misunderstanding, with the latter dominating for complex relations.

## Weaknesses
- **Potential dataset bias from LLM-assisted generation:** The dataset construction uses LLMs (GPT-4.1) for initial generation and filtering, followed by manual revision. While manual curation ensures quality, the influence of the generating LLM's biases on the final dataset is not analyzed, which could affect benchmark generalizability.
- **Incomplete analysis of the consistency-coverage trade-off:** For some models/strategies (e.g., GPT-5 Base with 32% "idk", CtE often higher "idk" rates), improved consistency may partly stem from the model refusing to answer rather than generating consistent answers. The trade-off between consistency and answer coverage/completeness is noted but not quantitatively explored, leaving the practical utility of mitigation strategies unclear.
- **Lack of non-LLM baseline for context:** The evaluation focuses solely on LLMs without comparing to a non-LLM baseline (e.g., a rule-based retriever executing SPARQL queries on a knowledge graph). Such a baseline would contextualize how far LLMs are from systems designed for consistency and calibrate the "far from perfect" claim.
- **Superficial error analysis:** While the paper attributes inconsistency to high-level causes (stochasticity, semantic misunderstanding) and Appendix H lists error patterns (terminology, completeness, etc.), a quantitative breakdown of error types across relations and models is missing. This limits diagnostic insights for model improvement.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Comparison to standard consistency-enhancing methods like self-consistency (majority voting) to better position the proposed prompting strategies.
- Analysis of how consistency correlates with answer set size (the dataset includes sets of 2-100 answers) to assess scalability.
- Pilot experiments using structured representations (e.g., LLM-generated SPARQL queries with a deterministic reasoner) for mitigation, as mentioned in future work.
- Expanded model coverage to include more recent open-source models (e.g., Qwen2.5, Phi-3) for a more comprehensive survey.

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.*
- **"The exact prompt transition for CtE is not shown":** The paper describes the CtE process in Section 3.3 ("the model is first asked to identify the relationship... and is then prompted to enumerate"), and prompts are provided in Appendix A, making this criticism unfounded.
- **"Missing ablation with established consistency methods (e.g., chain-of-thought)":** The paper does not claim to exhaustively compare all consistency methods; this is scope creep rather than a flaw in the presented work.
- **"Evaluation on out-of-distribution or more complex logical relations is needed":** The paper's scope is the benchmark it created; demanding OOD evaluation is a suggestion for future work, not a weakness of the current contribution.
- **"Formatting artifacts from PDF extraction impede understanding":** As per instructions, these are parser issues and not paper problems; they do not affect content evaluation.

## Novel Insights
The paper provides several novel insights beyond its core contributions: it demonstrates a striking disconnect between LLMs' ability to recognize set-theoretic relations (high accuracy for top models) and their ability to generate answers consistent with those relations (poor consistency rates). The control relation (\(E_{1,*}\)) reveals that stochasticity is a major factor for equivalence inconsistencies, while semantic misunderstanding dominates for containment and ternary relations. Surprisingly, the Classification-then-Enumeration strategy sometimes outperforms the Oracle, suggesting that the act of relation prediction itself may induce beneficial reasoning.

## Suggestions
- Conduct a quantitative analysis of error types (e.g., terminological mismatch, missing elements, logical misreasoning) to provide deeper diagnostic insights.
- Include a non-LLM baseline (e.g., executing SPARQL queries on Wikidata) to benchmark LLM performance against a system designed for consistency.
- Explore the consistency-coverage trade-off more explicitly, perhaps by reporting consistency rates conditional on non-"idk" responses or by incentivizing completeness in prompts.
