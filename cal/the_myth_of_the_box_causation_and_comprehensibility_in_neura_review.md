# Harsh Critic Review
## Section-by-Section Critical Review

**Title & Abstract**
The title is provocative and clearly signals a philosophical/conceptual critique. The abstract accurately summarizes the core thesis: the "black box" characterization relies on a false assumption that causal continuity requires correlative continuity (i.e., identifiable intermediary features). The claim is bold and, if substantiated, would have significant implications for XAI. The abstract is clear but highly conceptual; for an ICLR audience, the lack of any mention of empirical validation, formal definitions, or technical implications might be a red flag, suggesting the paper may be more philosophical than computational.

**Introduction & Motivation (Section 1)**
The problem is well-motivated within the discourse on AI explainability and opacity. The literature review is adequate, citing key works from both technical and philosophical perspectives. The introduction successfully frames the "black box" problem and introduces the core fallacy to be challenged. The "Secret Owls" example (Cloud et al., 2025) is an excellent and timely case study that concretely illustrates the puzzle of inexplicable feature transmission. A minor weakness is that the introduction does not explicitly state the paper's specific *contributions* in a bulleted list, which is common in ICLR papers. More importantly, it does not clearly delineate its scope as a *conceptual* argument, which may lead to mismatched expectations from readers seeking technical novelties or empirical results.

**Method / Approach (Section 2)**
This is the core argumentative section. The author attempts to construct a philosophical counterexample to the principle that causal continuity implies correlative continuity. The "wobbling clay" thought experiment is creative and serves its illustrative purpose. The argument is carefully structured, outlining desiderata for a counterexample (complex, nonlinear, low-level, unequivocal causality) and then proposing the clay system as an instance.

**Major Concerns:**
1.  **The analogy's applicability to neural networks is asserted, not demonstrated.** The paper claims neural networks are a candidate for such discontinuous correlation but does not provide a rigorous argument for why the dynamics of a high-dimensional, discrete-parameter, trained neural network are analogous to the continuous, homogeneous, physical dynamics of a clay lump. The clay's state is described as having no individuated features finer than "the whole form." Is this true for a neural network's weight configuration or intermediate activations? One could argue that every single weight and activation *is* an individuated feature. The author needs to engage with this disanalogy more deeply. The argument risks being dismissed as "neural networks are like clay, clay has this property, therefore neural networks might too" without establishing the relevant similarity.
2.  **The treatment of "feature" and "correlation" is informal.** For a computational audience, the lack of formal definitions for "feature," "correlative continuity," and even "causation" within the context of neural networks is a significant gap. The argument hinges on these notions. A more rigorous treatment, perhaps connecting to concepts in information theory (e.g., mutual information between layers) or causal abstraction theory, would greatly strengthen the paper's credibility for ICLR.
3.  **The "god's eye view" claim is strong but undefended.** The assertion that even an omniscient being could not find a correlative feature at t₂ is a powerful ontological claim. However, it is presented as an intuition based on the thought experiment. A more compelling argument would involve reasoning about the mathematical structure of the system (e.g., arguing that the mapping from clay state to wobble frequency is so holistically dependent on the entire state that no function isolating a proper subset of state variables constitutes a "correlate"). This level of formalization is missing.

**Experiments & Results**
There are none. This is a purely conceptual/philosophical paper. For ICLR, this is a major deviation from the norm. The acceptance of such papers is rare and hinges on the conceptual contribution being exceptionally clear, rigorous, and impactful for the field's foundations. The paper applies its framework to the "Secret Owls" case study (Section 3.1), but this is an interpretive discussion, not an experiment. It does not provide new analysis of the Cloud et al. data or model internals to *demonstrate* the absence of correlative features; it simply offers the discontinuous-correlation explanation as a plausible alternative. This section feels more like an extended discussion than a result.

**Writing & Clarity**
The writing is generally excellent—clear, engaging, and philosophically nuanced. The structure is logical, and the clay analogy is well-explained. However, some passages are dense and might be challenging for an audience primarily trained in STEM. The paper could benefit from a concise summary of the logical argument in plain language, perhaps with a simple diagram illustrating causal vs. correlative continuity.

**Limitations & Broader Impact**
The paper implicitly acknowledges a key limitation: the degree of correlative discontinuity is feature-dependent and system-dependent, and not all explanations in neural networks will fit this model. However, this should be an explicit, highlighted limitation section. The author should explicitly state that this is a conceptual argument whose applicability to any given neural network case is not proven and requires careful, case-by-case evaluation. The broader impact discussion (Sections 3.2 and 3.3) is thoughtful, correctly noting that dissolving the "black box" myth may not automatically solve trust issues but will reshape the discourse. A missed opportunity is a discussion of the impact on XAI research methodology: if the goal shifts from finding hidden correlates to characterizing holistic causal histories, what would that research program look like?

### Overall Assessment
This paper presents a thoughtful, well-articulated philosophical challenge to a foundational assumption in the XAI discourse. Its core insight—that we may be searching for intermediary correlates that do not exist—is provocative and potentially important. However, for ICLR, the paper faces significant hurdles. The argument relies heavily on an analogy whose relevance to neural networks is not rigorously established, lacks formal definitions or frameworks that would allow the ML community to operationalize its claims, and provides no empirical or computational validation. As a result, while the contribution is intellectually interesting, it currently reads more as a philosophy of science paper than a machine learning conference paper. Its acceptance would depend on reviewers valuing foundational conceptual critique highly enough to overlook the absence of technical novelty or empirical evidence. Major revisions to more formally bridge the philosophical argument to the mathematical structure of neural networks would be required to meet typical ICLR standards.

# Neutral Reviewer
## Balanced Review

### Summary
This paper challenges the foundational assumption that neural networks are "black boxes" due to inherent opacity in tracing output features to specific input causes. The author argues that the supposed opacity arises from a false philosophical assumption: that causal continuity across a system necessarily implies correlative continuity (i.e., the existence of identifiable intermediary features that correlate with both input and output features). Using physical analogies (e.g., a clay wobble persisting across time without a correlative intermediary feature) and referencing AI studies (e.g., the "Secret Owls" subliminal learning experiment), the paper contends that in many cases, no such hidden intermediary features exist; thus, the "black box" is a myth. The consequences are explored for explainable AI, trust in AI systems, and the language used in the field.

### Strengths
1. **Clear and Engaging Philosophical Argument**: The paper is well-written and logically structured. It clearly defines its target assumption (causal continuity implies correlative continuity), provides a plausible counterexample (the clay wobble), and systematically draws implications for AI.
2. **Interdisciplinary Engagement**: The paper effectively synthesizes concepts from philosophy of causation, cognitive science, and AI explainability literature (e.g., citing Zerilli, Chesterman, Zednik, and the Cloud et al. "owls" study). This bridges conceptual and applied discussions.
3. **Provocative Core Thesis**: The central claim—that the "black box" problem may be a conceptual fallacy rather than an epistemic limitation—is novel and thought-provoking. It directly challenges a dominant narrative in XAI and could reframe debates about transparency and trust.

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of Technical or Formal Grounding for ML**: For an ICLR submission, the paper is almost entirely philosophical and conceptual. It does not provide mathematical formulations, algorithmic insights, empirical validations on real neural networks, or a formal framework to distinguish when correlative continuity fails in ML systems. The clay analogy, while illustrative, is not a neural network.
2. **Limited Engagement with Contemporary ML Explainability Techniques**: The paper critiques the general assumption of opacity but does not deeply engage with state-of-the-art interpretability methods (e.g., mechanistic interpretability, causal mediation analysis, feature visualization) that attempt to *find* intermediary correlates. It does not address whether these methods' successes or failures support or refute its thesis.
3. **Underdeveloped Connection to the "Secret Owls" Example**: The Cloud et al. study is presented as a key example, but the paper only superficially applies its argument to this case. It does not provide a detailed analysis of the model's internals, training dynamics, or potential mechanistic explanations that might exist, leaving the application of the thesis feeling speculative.
4. **Vague Implications for Practice**: The discussion of consequences (trust, language) remains at a high conceptual level. It does not translate into concrete recommendations for AI practitioners, model auditors, or policymakers on how to assess explanations or build trust if the "black box" is a myth.

### Novelty & Significance
- **Novelty**: The paper's core philosophical argument—denying the necessity of correlative continuity in causal explanations for complex systems—is not new in philosophy of science, but its application to the foundational discourse of neural network "opacity" is novel for the AI community. It offers a distinct conceptual critique of the XAI paradigm.
- **Significance**: If accepted, the argument could significantly shift how researchers frame the explainability challenge, moving from "uncovering hidden features" to "accepting holistically mediated causation." However, for ICLR, the **significance is limited by its lack of technical contribution**. The paper does not advance machine learning methodology, theory, or empirical understanding. It is more suitable for a philosophy or interdisciplinary journal (e.g., *Philosophy & Technology*, *Minds and Machines*) than a top-tier ML conference focused on technical advances.

### Suggestions for Improvement
1. **Ground the Argument in ML Theory or Experiments**: To be suitable for ICLR, the author should formalize the argument in terms of information theory, dynamical systems, or learning theory. Alternatively, design simple neural network experiments (e.g., analyzing feature representations in a toy model) to empirically demonstrate cases where output features have causal antecedents but no linearly identifiable intermediary correlates.
2. **Engage Deeply with Mechanistic Interpretability Literature**: Discuss specific studies (e.g., from Anthropic, OpenAI, or academic labs) that claim to find "circuits" or "features" in models. Analyze whether these discovered correlates truly satisfy the correlative continuity assumption or if they are post-hoc interpretations, strengthening the paper's critique.
3. **Develop a Positive Framework for Explanation**: Instead of only deconstructing the "black box," propose an alternative, actionable model for explanation in AI that accommodates "correlative discontinuity." What would satisfactory explanations look like? How should we evaluate them? This would increase the paper's utility for the XAI community.
4. **Sharpen the AI Relevance Throughout**: The clay example, while helpful, dominates the argument. Tailor the discussion more consistently to neural network architectures—e.g., by analyzing specific properties (nonlinearities, high dimensionality, distributed representations) that might make correlative discontinuity more likely than in simple systems.

# Spark Finder Review
## How to Improve This Paper

**ICLR Context:** This is a philosophical/conceptual paper making a strong claim about the foundations of explainability. ICLR reviewers expect rigorous engagement with the technical ML literature and empirical phenomena, even for theoretical work. The paper currently relies heavily on abstract argument and a single external study (Cloud et al.). To be convincing at ICLR, it must directly bridge its philosophical thesis to concrete, observable properties of modern neural networks through analysis and evidence.

### Missing Experiments (top 3-5 only)
1.  **Empirical validation of the core thesis on a standard model/task.** The claim that intermediary correlative features "do not exist" in some cases is a strong, falsifiable claim about neural networks. The paper must demonstrate this with a simple, controlled experiment (e.g., on a small transformer or MLP) where a clear input feature causes an output feature, but an exhaustive search (e.g., via causal mediation analysis, probing classifiers across all layers) fails to find a linearly or non-linearly decodable intermediary representation that correlates with the output. Without this, the claim remains a speculative philosophical position.
2.  **Ablation on the "Secret Owls" study.** The paper's primary empirical anchor is the Cloud et al. study. To bolster its argument, the authors should attempt to replicate a key aspect of this finding and perform their own analysis. For instance, they could train a small model on the described numerical data and rigorously probe/intervene on its internal representations to *attempt* to find the "owl" correlate, documenting the failure. This turns the cited study into direct evidence for their thesis.
3.  **Comparison to a case where correlative continuity *does* hold.** To show the distinction is meaningful, the paper should contrast its "clay wobble" scenario with a well-understood case in interpretability where intermediary features *are* cleanly identifiable (e.g., curve detectors in CNNs, grammatical structure in LSTMs). This would ground the discussion, showing their thesis applies to a *class* of model behaviors, not all.

### Deeper Analysis Needed (top 3-5 only)
1.  **Engagement with the mechanistic interpretability literature.** The paper largely ignores a relevant subfield (e.g., work from Anthropic, OpenAI, Neel Nanda) that seeks precisely to find "circuits" and intermediary features corresponding to input-output relationships. The argument must directly engage with this literature's successes and claimed failures. Does the paper predict these efforts are fundamentally doomed for certain behaviors? This is a critical gap in the scholarly context.
2.  **Analysis of the *conditions* for correlative discontinuity.** The argument is presented as a binary possibility (features exist or don't). A more useful contribution would hypothesize *when* in a neural network (by architecture, training dynamics, task) we should expect this phenomenon. For example, is it linked to high degeneracy of representations, extreme non-linearity, or distributed computation? Without this, the thesis is too vague to guide research.
3.  **Clarification on the ontology of "features."** The argument hinges on "individuating" intermediary features. This needs a precise, operational definition aligned with ML practice (e.g., directions in activation space, concepts from sparse autoencoders, neurons). The clay analogy uses macroscopic physical features, which is a weak analogy for high-dimensional neural activations. The analysis must define what counts as a "feature" in a NN and what search would constitute proof of its absence.

### Visualizations & Case Studies
1.  **Visualizing the "search for a correlate" in a concrete model.** For a claimed instance of correlative discontinuity (perhaps inspired by the owls study), the authors should visualize the internal activations across layers for relevant inputs. The visualization should compellingly show that while the input and output are correlated, there is no layer or subspace where activations *systematically* vary with the target feature in a way that suggests a coherent intermediary representation.
2.  **A detailed case study of an unexplained but robust model behavior.** Beyond citing Cloud et al., the authors should pick a known, puzzling "emergent" behavior from the literature (e.g., certain in-context learning capabilities, grokking, or specific hallucination patterns) and walk through how their framework reinterprets the search for an explanation. This would demonstrate the practical utility of the conceptual shift.

### Obvious Next Steps
1.  **Formalize the claim as a testable conjecture or provide a proof-of-concept.** The paper should move from philosophical argument to a formal statement that can be investigated by the ML community. As a minimal step, provide a simple, constructed toy model (e.g., a specific non-linear function) that provably exhibits causal continuity without correlative continuity, and then argue neural networks can instantiate similar dynamics.
2.  **Connect to the literature on underspecification and simplicity biases.** The thesis likely relates to known phenomena where many internal configurations can lead to the same input-output map. The paper should explicitly bridge to this technical ML literature to show its roots in observable training dynamics, not just abstract causation.
3.  **Discuss implications for XAI method evaluation.** If some explanations are "complete" without intermediary correlates, how should we evaluate XAI methods (like saliency maps or concept vectors) that purport to find such correlates? The paper should outline what the new criteria for a "good" explanation would be under its framework, moving beyond critique to constructive guidance.

# Final Consolidated Review
## Summary
This paper challenges the foundational "black box" metaphor in explainable AI, arguing it rests on a false philosophical assumption: that causal continuity in a system necessarily implies correlative continuity (i.e., identifiable intermediary features that correlate with both input and output). Using a thought experiment (a clay's persistent wobble) and a contemporary AI study ("Secret Owls"), the author contends that for some neural network behaviors, no such hidden intermediary features exist; the explanation is complete with the holistic causal history, and the "box" is a myth.

## Strengths
- **Provocative and well-articulated conceptual critique:** The paper clearly identifies and challenges a deep-seated assumption (correlative continuity) in the XAI discourse, offering a novel philosophical lens that could reframe debates about opacity, explanation, and trust.
- **Effective interdisciplinary synthesis:** The argument skillfully bridges philosophical reasoning about causation with concrete AI literature, using the timely Cloud et al. (2025) "Secret Owls" study as a compelling anchor point to illustrate the conceptual puzzle.
- **Clear and engaging writing:** The core argument is logically structured and accessible, making a complex philosophical point digestible through a well-chosen physical analogy (the clay wobble).

## Weaknesses
- **The analogy to neural networks requires more substantive development.** The argument hinges on the clay system having no individuated intermediary features. The paper asserts neural networks can be similar but does not rigorously establish the relevant similarity. A deeper engagement with the properties of neural networks (e.g., distributed, high-dimensional representations) is needed to move from a plausible analogy to a convincing case for NNs. This leaves the core thesis feeling suggestive rather than demonstrated for its primary domain of application.
- **The practical implications for XAI research and evaluation remain vague.** While the paper successfully deconstructs the "black box" concept, it offers little concrete guidance on what constitutes a satisfactory explanation under its new framework. How should researchers and practitioners proceed if some explanations are "complete" without intermediary correlates? The discussion of trust and language (Sections 3.2, 3.3) stays at a high conceptual level without translating into actionable insights for model development, auditing, or evaluation.
- **Limited engagement with the technical interpretability literature.** The paper critiques a general assumption but does not deeply engage with state-of-the-art mechanistic interpretability work that explicitly seeks to find intermediary "circuits" or features. A discussion of whether this literature's successes or failures support the thesis would ground the philosophical argument in contemporary technical practice and strengthen its relevance for the ICLR audience.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A more formal characterization of the conditions under which correlative discontinuity might be expected in neural networks (e.g., linked to specific architectural properties or training dynamics).
- A brief discussion connecting the thesis to related ML concepts like underspecification or simplicity biases, showing its roots in observable training phenomena.
- A diagram illustrating the distinction between causal continuity and correlative continuity in the clay and neural network examples.

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution*
- **Weakness: "Lack of technical or formal grounding for ML / No experiments."** This criticism misapprehends the paper's stated scope and contribution. The paper is a conceptual argument, not an empirical systems paper. Demanding experiments or formal ML theory imposes a methodological standard outside its goals. The paper's contribution is philosophical, and it should be evaluated on the clarity, novelty, and implications of its argument.
- **Weakness: "The 'god's eye view' claim is strong but undefended."** The paper's ontological claim (that no intermediary feature exists, even for an omniscient observer) is integral to the thought experiment and is argued for through the properties of the clay system. Requesting a formal mathematical proof for this philosophical claim is not a standard expectation for this type of conceptual work.
- **Weakness: "Underdeveloped connection to the 'Secret Owls' example."** The paper uses the Cloud et al. study as an illustrative example to apply its framework, not as a case for novel empirical analysis. It successfully argues that the discontinuous-correlation view provides a plausible alternative explanation, which is sufficient for its conceptual purpose.
- **Nitpick: "Introduction does not explicitly state contributions in a bulleted list."** This is a stylistic preference, not a substantive flaw.

## Novel Insights
The paper's core insight is genuinely novel for the ML community: the pervasive sense of neural network "opacity" may stem not from an epistemic limitation (features are hidden) but from an ontological one (in some cases, no fine-grained intermediary features exist to be found). This reframes the explanatory challenge from "uncovering the hidden mechanism" to "characterizing a holistic causal history," a shift with profound implications for how we conceptualize the goals and successes of explainability research.

## Suggestions
- Strengthen the argument by more directly analyzing why the properties of neural networks (e.g., extreme non-linearity, distributed representations, high degeneracy) might make them candidates for correlative discontinuity, moving beyond analogy to a principled connection.
- Develop a more concrete, positive framework for what "explanation" entails when correlative continuity fails. Offer tentative criteria for when an explanation citing distal causes and holistic system states can be considered complete. This would transform the critique into a constructive guide for future work.
