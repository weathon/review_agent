# Harsh Critic Review
## Section-by-Section Critical Review

**Title & Abstract**
The title accurately reflects the core contribution: a benchmark (Gaia2) for LLM agents focused on dynamic and asynchronous environments. The abstract succinctly summarizes the problem, the benchmark's features (asynchronous execution, verifier, RLVR-usability), key results (no model dominance, trade-offs), and the release of the underlying ARE platform. All abstract claims are directly supported in the paper.

**Introduction & Motivation**
The motivation is strong and well-aligned with ICLR's focus on advancing AI capabilities. It clearly identifies the gap: most existing agent benchmarks are static or synchronous, failing to test crucial real-world challenges like asynchronous events, temporal constraints, and robustness. The three contributions (ARE framework, Gaia2 benchmark, empirical study) are stated upfront and clearly delineated. The connection to RLVR (Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards) is timely and well-motivated.

**Method / Approach (ARE & Gaia2 Design)**
*   **ARE Framework:** The core concepts (Apps, Environments, Events, Notifications, Scenarios) are well-defined and appear general. The claim that ARE can reimplement existing benchmarks (τ-bench, GAIA, etc.) is a good validity check but is only briefly stated; providing a short comparison or reference to an appendix with more details would strengthen this claim. The design choices (e.g., time advancement during model generation, distinction between read/write tools) are justified and central to the benchmark's goals.
*   **Gaia2 Benchmark:** The capability taxonomy (Execution, Search, Ambiguity, Adaptability, Time, Noise, A2A) is logical and well-explained. The scenario annotation process seems rigorous (human-authored, multiple validation rounds, automated guardrails). A critical point for reproducibility is the **initial universe generation**. Appendix A.3 describes the process (using PersonaHub, LLM generation for consistency), but the inherent stochasticity and potential biases from the LLM used for generation are not discussed as limitations. The universes are described as containing 400K-800K tokens of synthetic data; the paper should clarify if these specific universe instances are released with the benchmark to ensure exact reproducibility of results.
*   **Verifier:** This is a major contribution. The verifier's design (focus on write actions, combining exact matches, LLM rubrics, causality, and timing checks) is sound. The validation against 450 human-labeled trajectories (Table 1, Table 5) shows high agreement, precision, and recall, convincingly arguing for its reliability over a pure LLM judge. The discussion of "judge-hacking" and mitigation (Appendix B.2.3) is an important and credible detail.

**Experiments & Results**
*   **Core Setup:** Using a consistent, minimal ReAct scaffold for all models is a fair baseline choice. The handling of deployment issues (simulated generation time) is practical. Reporting pass@1 across three runs is standard.
*   **Core Results (Table 2, Figures 5-7):** The results are comprehensive and reveal interesting trade-offs. The finding that no model dominates across capabilities is well-supported. The analysis linking performance to exploration (tool calls) and reasoning (output tokens) in Figure 7 is insightful. However, the **presentation of uncertainty is lacking**. Table 2 reports only point estimates (pass@1). For a benchmark paper, it is crucial to report some measure of variance (e.g., standard error over runs or scenarios) to indicate the stability of the rankings. While three runs are mentioned, the resulting variance is not quantified in the main results table.
*   **Time Sensitivity Analysis (Figure 8):** This is a standout experiment. The "instant" mode effectively establishes an upper bound, cleanly separating the impact of inference latency from model capability on time-sensitive tasks. The inverse scaling observation for GPT models is a compelling finding.
*   **Multi-Agent Collaboration (A2A):** The experiments are thorough, exploring homogeneous/heterogeneous teams and collaboration ratios. The result that collaboration helps weaker models more than stronger ones (Figure 10) is intriguing and well-presented. The analysis tying benefits to error reduction in tool calls (Figure 9) is good.
*   **Cost-Performance Analysis (Figure 6):** The inclusion of cost and time per solved scenario is valuable for practical assessment. However, a **significant caveat** is needed: cost estimates are based on a specific third-party source (Artificial Analysis) from a specific date. Pricing is highly volatile and varies by provider, region, and volume. The relative cost rankings are informative, but the absolute dollar values should be interpreted cautiously. The paper should explicitly state this limitation.
*   **Ablations & Appendix:** The parallel tool-calling ablation (Appendix B.3.2, Table 6) is essential and correctly placed in the appendix. It effectively shows that the scaffold is not the primary bottleneck for performance, though it impacts efficiency. The noise robustness sanity check (Appendix B.5.2) is good practice.

**Writing & Clarity**
The paper is generally well-written and structured. The figures are informative. Some sections are dense (e.g., the detailed ARE concepts in Section 3) but necessary. The flow from problem motivation to framework to benchmark to results is logical. The appendix is extensive and provides necessary detail.

**Limitations & Broader Impact**
The discussion of limitations is somewhat brief. Key points that should be expanded in a dedicated limitations section or the conclusion include:
1.  **Sim2Real Gap:** While Gaia2 advances realism, it remains a synthetic, curated environment. The "noise" added is controlled; real-world noise is often more complex and unpredictable.
2.  **Universe Generation:** The reliance on LLM-generated synthetic data for universe creation may introduce unknown biases and limit the diversity of scenarios relative to the real world. The reproducibility of the exact benchmark states depends on releasing these universes.
3.  **Scope of Capabilities:** The benchmark focuses on a consumer mobile domain. While ARE is generalizable, the current tasks are specific to this domain. The paper notes this but could discuss how well the identified capabilities might transfer.
4.  **Cost Analysis Limitation:** As noted above, the volatility of model pricing is a major caveat.
5.  **Broader Impact** is not discussed. While the work is primarily a benchmark, a brief consideration of potential misuse (e.g., benchmarking agents for automation that could disrupt labor markets) or the environmental impact of running extensive agent evaluations would be appropriate for ICLR.

### Overall Assessment
This is a strong, substantial contribution that meets ICLR's standards for novelty, rigor, and potential impact. The introduction of the ARE platform and the Gaia2 benchmark addresses a clear and important gap in agent evaluation. The empirical study is large-scale, insightful, and reveals fundamental trade-offs not captured by prior benchmarks. The main weaknesses to address before acceptance are: **(1) the lack of reported variance/uncertainty in the main results table**, which is essential for a benchmark paper; **(2) a more explicit discussion of limitations**, particularly concerning universe generation, the sim2real gap, and the caveats of the cost analysis; and **(3) a brief broader impact statement**. With these revisions, the paper's contribution—providing the community with a robust infrastructure and benchmark for asynchronous, dynamic agent evaluation—stands as significant and publishable.

# Neutral Reviewer
## Balanced Review

### Summary
This paper introduces **GAIA2**, a benchmark designed to evaluate large language model (LLM) agents in realistic, asynchronous, and dynamic environments. The benchmark is built on the novel **Agents Research Environments (ARE)** platform, which provides a general-purpose framework for creating event-driven simulations. GAIA2 comprises 1,120 scenarios that test capabilities like temporal reasoning, adaptability to noise, ambiguity resolution, and multi-agent collaboration. A key contribution is the "write-action verifier," which enables fine-grained, action-level evaluation directly usable for reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR). The paper presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of state-of-the-art proprietary and open-source models, revealing significant trade-offs between reasoning capability, efficiency, robustness, and cost.

### Strengths
1.  **Novel and Well-Motivated Benchmark Design:** GAIA2 directly addresses a critical gap in LLM agent evaluation by moving beyond static, synchronous tasks to incorporate asynchronous events, temporal constraints, and environmental dynamics. The paper convincingly argues that these features are essential for assessing "real-world" agent capabilities, filling a need highlighted by prior work. The design is grounded in practical use cases (e.g., calendar management, multi-agent collaboration).
2.  **Comprehensive Infrastructure and Empirical Evaluation:** The release of the open-source ARE platform alongside GAIA2 is a significant contribution. The platform's abstractions (apps, events, notifications, scenarios) appear general and flexible. The empirical study is extensive, evaluating a wide array of models (GPT-5, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Kimi, etc.) across multiple capability splits. The analysis goes beyond simple pass rates to include cost-performance trade-offs, latency analysis, and multi-agent collaboration studies, providing rich insights into the current state of agent capabilities.
3.  **Rigorous Verification Methodology:** The proposed "write-action verifier" is a robust solution for fine-grained evaluation. The paper demonstrates its high precision (0.99) and recall (0.95) against human labels, a marked improvement over LLM-judge baselines. This verifier is crucial for enabling RLVR and ensures reproducible, actionable feedback for agent improvement, which aligns with ICLR's emphasis on rigorous evaluation.

### Weaknesses
1.  **Limited Domain and Scenario Generalizability:** While the Mobile environment is rich, the benchmark is currently confined to a single, consumer-oriented domain (smartphone apps). The paper claims ARE is domain-agnostic, but GAIA2 itself does not demonstrate evaluations in other critical domains like desktop automation, scientific reasoning, or complex enterprise workflows. The "sim2real" gap, while mentioned, is not quantified, leaving open questions about how performance in GAIA2's synthetic environment correlates with real-world deployment.
2.  **High Computational Cost and Accessibility Concerns:** Running evaluations on 1,120 scenarios with multiple runs per model, especially using costly proprietary APIs (GPT-5, Claude), presents a significant barrier to entry for academic researchers. Although GAIA2-mini (160 scenarios) is provided, the full benchmark's cost may limit widespread adoption and independent verification of results by the broader community.
3.  **Incomplete Analysis of Failure Modes and Scaffold Limitations:** The paper attributes performance differences primarily to model capabilities but provides limited deep-dive analysis into *why* models fail on specific capability splits (e.g., Ambiguity, Time). Furthermore, while an ablation on Parallel Tool Calling is included (Appendix B.3.2), the core evaluation uses a simple ReAct scaffold. The impact of more advanced orchestration techniques (hierarchical planning, reflection) on benchmark performance remains an open question, potentially leaving headroom on the reported scores unexplored.

### Novelty & Significance
**Novelty:** The work is highly novel in its integrated approach. While individual components (asynchronous environments, tool-use benchmarks, verification) have precedents, GAIA2 is the first to unify them into a coherent benchmark specifically designed for *asynchronous, dynamic* agent evaluation with *action-level verification* for RLVR. The ARE framework itself is a substantial novel contribution as a general-purpose platform.

**Significance:** This work is significant for the AI/agent research community. It provides a much-needed, challenging benchmark that pushes beyond the limitations of current static evaluations. The release of the infrastructure (ARE) lowers the barrier for creating new benchmarks and conducting RLVR research. The empirical findings clearly expose the non-trivial trade-offs in today's agent models, providing a valuable roadmap for future research in model design, efficient reasoning, and robust orchestration. It meets ICLR's standard for presenting a solid, well-executed engineering contribution with clear empirical insights.

### Suggestions for Improvement
1.  **Expand Domain Validation and Provide "Sim2Real" Analysis:** To strengthen claims of generality, the authors should use ARE to instantiate and evaluate agents on at least one additional, qualitatively different environment (e.g., a simplified desktop OS or customer service domain) and include those results. A small-scale study correlating GAIA2 performance with performance on a real-world task (even if preliminary) would greatly strengthen the paper's impact.
2.  **Enhance Diagnostic Analysis and Error Categorization:** A deeper qualitative analysis of agent failures should be added. Categorizing common error types per capability split (e.g., misunderstanding temporal dependencies, failing to notice relevant notifications, poor sub-task delegation in A2A) would provide more actionable insights for the community beyond aggregate scores.
3.  **Perform a More Comprehensive Orchestration Ablation Study:** The paper should expand the orchestration analysis. A dedicated section or experiment comparing the baseline ReAct scaffold against 2-3 state-of-the-art agent frameworks (e.g., those with planning, memory, or reflection) on key splits would help disentangle model limitations from scaffolding limitations and better define the benchmark's upper bound.
4.  **Improve Accessibility and Reproducibility:** To address cost barriers, the authors could provide more detailed guidelines on efficient evaluation, perhaps including scripts for cost estimation and results caching. Furthermore, ensuring all dependencies for ARE and GAIA2 are clearly documented and that the code release includes easy-to-run examples for Gaia2-mini will be crucial for community adoption and reproducibility, a key ICLR criterion.

# Spark Finder Review
## How to Improve This Paper

### Missing Experiments (top 3-5 only)
1. **Lack of strong agent baseline comparisons.** The paper evaluates all models with a simple ReAct scaffold. To show GAIA2 truly measures model capabilities and not scaffold limitations, it must include results with state-of-the-art agent frameworks (e.g., advanced planners, reflexion). Without this, high scores may reflect scaffold adequacy, not model intelligence.
2. **Incomplete ablation of notification policies.** The environment has configurable notification verbosity (low, medium, high), but results are only shown for the "medium" default. A critical experiment is to sweep these levels to show how observability directly impacts performance, especially for *Adaptability* and *Time* splits. This is essential to validate the claim that asynchronicity is a core challenge.
3. **Superficial cost-performance analysis.** Cost estimates rely on external API pricing without accounting for reasoning tokens (a major cost driver for models like GPT-5 (high) and Claude). This skews the trade-off curves and undermines the paper's conclusions about cost efficiency. A proper analysis must use token counts and a consistent pricing model that includes reasoning compute.
4. **No RLVR training results.** A core claim is that GAIA2 is "directly usable for RLVR," but the paper provides zero experiments showing that an agent can be improved via reinforcement learning on the benchmark. This is a major omission; without it, the claim is unsupported.
5. **Limited heterogeneous multi-agent evaluation.** The A2A analysis is primarily homogeneous (same model for all agents). Table 3 briefly probes heterogeneity but lacks a systematic study (e.g., sweeping main/app agent strengths, cost-benefit analysis). This misses a key opportunity to substantiate claims about orchestration and compute scaling.

### Deeper Analysis Needed (top 3-5 only)
1. **Root-cause analysis for the *Time* split inverse scaling.** The paper notes reasoning models fail on time-sensitive tasks but doesn't diagnose why. Is it purely due to generation latency, or do they also fail at temporal reasoning even when latency is removed ("instant" mode)? A breakdown of failure types (missed deadline vs. wrong action) is needed.
2. **Failure mode analysis for *Ambiguity* and *Noise* splits.** Scores are low, but it's unclear how models fail. Do they blindly execute ambiguous tasks, or do they sometimes ask for clarification? For noise, do they recover from tool errors or get stuck? This analysis is crucial for understanding capability gaps.
3. **Verifier reliability and judge error analysis.** The verifier uses an LLM for soft checks. The paper mentions "judge-hacking" but does not analyze the rate or types of verifier errors (false positives/negatives) across different models and scenario types. This is critical for trusting the evaluation's integrity.
4. **Correlation between app usage and performance.** Figure 3 shows app usage distribution but does not analyze whether performance differences are tied to proficiency with specific, complex apps (e.g., Calendar vs. Files). This would pinpoint domain-specific weaknesses.

### Visualizations & Case Studies
1. **Annotated failure trajectories.** Visual timelines of agent actions, environment events, and notifications for representative failures (especially in *Time* and *A2A*) would concretely show where reasoning breaks down—e.g., missing a notification, issuing commands too late, or miscoordinating with sub-agents.
2. **Case studies of Agent2Agent coordination breakdown.** Examples of message exchanges between main and app-agents that lead to failure would reveal whether issues are in task decomposition, instruction clarity, or state tracking.
3. **Impact of notification observability.** Side-by-side visualizations of agent behavior on the same scenario under low vs. high notification policies would demonstrate the critical role of asynchronicity and proactivity.

### Obvious Next Steps
1. **Benchmark against a strong, learned agent baseline.** The paper should have used an agent scaffold optimized via RL or extensive prompting on similar domains to establish a stronger performance ceiling and better isolate model vs. scaffolding limitations.
2. **Complete the notification policy ablation.** A full sweep of verbosity levels is a straightforward and necessary experiment to quantify the importance of the asynchronous design.
3. **Demonstrate RLVR improvement.** Training an agent (even a small model) with verifier rewards on GAIA2 and showing improved performance was an essential step to validate the benchmark's utility for training.
4. **Systematic study of heterogeneous teams.** A dedicated experiment varying the capabilities of main and sub-agents across a cost-performance Pareto frontier would have solidly supported the discussion on adaptive compute and orchestration.

# Final Consolidated Review
## Summary
This paper introduces GAIA2, a benchmark for evaluating LLM agents in asynchronous, dynamic environments, and the underlying Agents Research Environments (ARE) platform. GAIA2 features 1,120 scenarios testing capabilities like temporal reasoning, adaptability, ambiguity resolution, and multi-agent collaboration, paired with a fine-grained write-action verifier for reliable evaluation and RLVR. An extensive empirical study of state-of-the-art models reveals no single model dominates across capabilities, exposing fundamental trade-offs between reasoning, efficiency, robustness, and cost.

## Strengths
- **Novel and Well-Motivated Benchmark Design:** GAIA2 directly addresses a critical gap by moving beyond static, synchronous evaluations to incorporate asynchronous events, temporal constraints, and environmental dynamics. The design is grounded in realistic use cases (e.g., calendar management, multi-agent collaboration) and is built on a general, extensible platform (ARE).
- **Comprehensive and Insightful Empirical Evaluation:** The paper presents a large-scale study evaluating numerous proprietary and open-source models across distinct capability splits. The analysis goes beyond aggregate scores to reveal insightful trade-offs, including cost-performance-time curves, inverse scaling in time-sensitive tasks, and differential benefits of multi-agent collaboration for weaker vs. stronger models.
- **Rigorous and Reusable Verification System:** The proposed write-action verifier achieves high precision (0.99) and recall (0.95) against human labels, providing a reliable, fine-grained evaluation mechanism that supports credit assignment for RLVR. Its design mitigates judge-hacking and is presented as a reusable component of the ARE framework.

## Weaknesses
- **Incomplete Reporting of Measurement Variance:** For a benchmark paper, the main results table (Table 2) reports only point estimates (pass@1). While three runs are mentioned, the resulting variance or standard error is not quantified in the primary presentation, making it difficult to assess the stability of rankings and differences between models.
- **Ambiguity in Benchmark Reproducibility:** The benchmark's universes are generated synthetically using LLMs and persona data. The paper does not explicitly state whether the *specific universe instances* used for evaluation are released alongside the scenario definitions. Reproducing the exact benchmark states is crucial for verifying reported results, and this dependency should be clarified.
- **Caveats in Cost-Performance Analysis:** The cost analysis relies on external, time-sensitive pricing data (Artificial Analysis, accessed Sept 2025). While informative for relative comparisons, the absolute dollar values are volatile and provider-dependent. The analysis also does not uniformly account for reasoning token costs across all models, which could skew some efficiency conclusions.
- **Limited Comparison Against Advanced Agent Scaffolds:** The core evaluation uses a simple, consistent ReAct scaffold to ensure fair comparison. However, this leaves open the question of how much performance headroom exists with more sophisticated orchestration (e.g., hierarchical planning, reflection). While a parallel tool-calling ablation is provided, a comparison against state-of-the-art agent frameworks would better isolate model capabilities from scaffolding limitations.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Ablation on Notification Observability:** The ARE framework supports configurable notification policies (low, medium, high). Systematically evaluating how performance, especially on *Adaptability* and *Time* splits, varies with observability would directly validate the importance of the asynchronous design.
- **Deeper Qualitative Failure Analysis:** A categorization of common failure modes per capability split (e.g., types of temporal reasoning errors, miscoordination in A2A) would provide more actionable diagnostic insights beyond aggregate scores.
- **Preliminary RLVR Demonstration:** While the verifier is designed for RLVR, a small-scale experiment showing performance improvement for an agent trained with verifier rewards would concretely demonstrate this utility.

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution*
- **Weakness: "Broader Impact statement is missing."** (The paper's primary contribution is a benchmark and infrastructure; a broader impact discussion is not a standard requirement for such contributions at ICLR.)
- **Weakness: "Universe generation may introduce unknown LLM biases."** (This is a generic concern applicable to any synthetic data generation. The paper details its generation process and uses it to create a controlled evaluation testbed, not to make claims about real-world distribution.)
- **Weakness: "High computational cost limits academic accessibility."** (The paper provides a minimized subset (GAIA2-mini) for lower-cost evaluation, which is a reasonable mitigation. The full scale is appropriate for establishing a comprehensive benchmark.)
- **Weakness: "Need for sim2real correlation study."** (The paper explicitly frames GAIA2 as a step to address the sim2real gap, not a solution to it. Demanding a real-world validation study is outside the stated scope of introducing a synthetic benchmark.)
- **Strength: "The paper is well-written."** (This is a generic strength that does not identify what this specific paper does better than others.)

## Novel Insights
The benchmark reveals that no current model excels across all agent capabilities, highlighting fundamental, non-trivial trade-offs. Key novel insights include: 1) **Inverse scaling in time-sensitive tasks**, where models with stronger reasoning capabilities (e.g., GPT-5 high) perform worse due to generation latency, underscoring the need for adaptive compute strategies. 2) **Differential value of collaboration**, where multi-agent coordination (Agent2Agent) improves performance and reduces tool-call errors for weaker models but offers diminishing returns for stronger ones, suggesting collaboration benefits are tied to mitigating specific failure modes. 3) **Exploration-reasoning trade-offs**, where performance correlates with both the number of exploratory tool calls and the volume of reasoning tokens, yet outliers like Claude-4 Sonnet achieve high scores with relative efficiency, pointing to architectural or scaling advantages.

## Suggestions
- In the main results table (Table 2), add a measure of variance (e.g., standard error over runs) for the pass@1 scores to clarify the significance of observed differences.
- Explicitly state in the paper or documentation whether the exact, seeded universe states used for evaluation are released to guarantee full reproducibility of the reported numbers.
- In the cost-analysis section, add a prominent caveat noting the volatility of API pricing and the potential variation in cost accounting (e.g., for reasoning tokens) to properly frame the interpretation of the absolute dollar values.
