## Summary
This paper proposes two courtroom-inspired multi-agent architectures for LLM-as-judge evaluation: Multi-Advocate One-Round Evaluation (MORE), which deploys three advocates per candidate answer for a single judging round, and Single Advocate Multi-Round Evaluation (SAMRE), which iterates up to four rounds with a single advocate per answer and an optional three-member jury. Both are evaluated on the MT-Bench pairwise preference dataset against a single-judge baseline, with SAMRE-without-juries achieving the best accuracy across all six judge models tested.

---

## Strengths

- **Clear algorithmic specification**: Both MORE (Algorithm 1) and SAMRE (Algorithm 2) are presented with concrete pseudocode, including the stopping condition (two consecutive rounds with the same ordinal ranking), score aggregation rules, and jury voting, enabling a principled reading of the proposed methods — a level of specificity often absent in early-stage multi-agent evaluation papers.
- **Consistent empirical improvement over single-judge baseline**: Across all six judge model backbones tested (Llama-3-8B through GPT-4-turbo), the proposed architectures improve accuracy over the single-judge baseline, with SAMRE-without-juries reaching statistically significant gains (p < 0.05) for five of the six models. This broad consistency across model families is meaningful, not just for a single strong model.
- **Productive negative result on jury utility**: The finding that SAMRE *without* juries outperforms the full SAMRE architecture across all models is a concrete, replicable empirical result — albeit one the paper does not adequately explain. It is a genuine contribution to the design space that informs future work.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None that individually invalidate the entire paper, but the combination of theoretical inconsistencies and empirical gaps described below significantly undermines confidence in the claims.

### Major

- **Critical architectural inconsistency — 3 vs. 5 jurors**: Section 3.2 explicitly states "five diverse jurors," while Algorithm 2 initializes `{C₁, C₂, C₃}` (three jurors). This is not a stylistic issue — the number of jurors affects the voting outcome (majority changes with 3 vs. 5), the reproducibility of results, and the validity of any comparison between SAMRE variants. It is unclear which version was actually run, calling the experimental setup into question.

- **Theorem 2 is empirically contradicted by the paper's own results**: Theorem 2 claims that the multi-advocate (MORE) framework requires fewer iterations to reach a given level of score differentiation than the iterative debate (SAMRE) framework — i.e., MORE is more computationally efficient *and* should be preferred. Yet empirically, SAMRE-without-juries *consistently and substantially outperforms* MORE in accuracy across all six models (e.g., 0.89 vs. 0.85 for Llama, 0.95 vs. 0.90 for GPT-4-turbo). The paper never addresses this disconnect. If the theory predicts MORE should converge faster to equivalent quality, why does the iterative SAMRE achieve consistently higher accuracy? This gap between theory and experiment is unaddressed and undermines the theoretical framing.

- **Aggregation Property is essentially trivially true by construction**: The Aggregation Property claims $g(f_{i\text{-agg}}) \geq \max_j g(f_{ij})$. Under Assumption 2, $f_{i\text{-agg}}$ is selected by argmax over softmax-weighted internal scores, and under Assumption 1 ($g \approx g'$), this selects the argument with approximately the highest external score. The aggregated score therefore approximately *equals* $\max_j g(f_{ij})$, making the inequality hold with near-equality by definition — not as a non-trivial theoretical result. The claim of strict inequality in Theorem 1 then requires distributional assumptions about how individual advocate scores compare to a single-advocate score that are never stated explicitly in the main text.

- **Model roles in experiments are never clarified**: Table 1 lists Llama-3-8B, Qwen, Gemini, GPT-4-o, GPT-4-turbo, and GPT-3.5-turbo as "models." MT-Bench provides fixed answer pairs generated by specific models — so if these are the *judge* models, the paper should say so explicitly; if they are the *answer-generating* models, the experimental setup is entirely different from what is described. This ambiguity makes the results uninterpretable and prevents reproducibility.

- **No comparison to existing multi-agent evaluation frameworks**: The only baseline is a single judge (Algorithm 3). There is no comparison to ChatEval, Reconcile, PRD, or other multi-agent debate-based evaluation systems that are direct prior art. Without such baselines, it is impossible to know whether the gains over a single judge are due to the specific courtroom architecture or simply to using any multi-agent setup at all.

- **Jury underperformance is unexplained and architecturally damaging**: The jury is presented as a core, motivated component of SAMRE (drawing on adversarial jurisprudence and social choice theory). Yet it *hurts* performance in every tested model. The paper describes this finding as evidence that "iterative refinement and advocate roles are key drivers" but offers no explanation for *why* juries degrade accuracy. Do they introduce noise? Does the binary jury vote conflict with the judge's averaged score? This is the most important empirical result in the paper and is dismissed in a single sentence.

- **Suspiciously uniform result patterns — no variance or confidence intervals reported**: Table 1 shows perfectly monotonic ordering (Baseline < MORE < SAMRE < SAMRE w/o Juries) for every single model, with no variation or exceptions. The absolute gains in MORE are exactly 0.03–0.04 for all models; for SAMRE w/o Juries, exactly 0.07–0.09. No random seeds, temperature settings, or confidence intervals are reported. For a system with stochastic LLM outputs, this uniformity is implausible and warrants a transparency statement or additional experimental detail.

### Minor

- **Section 3.5 is a single sentence**: The probabilistic error-reduction model is described in the abstract as a key contribution and highlighted in the conclusion, yet Section 3.5 in its entirety reads: "We also provide detailed results of error reduction achieved by our iterative advocate framework in Appendix A." If this is a core contribution, it must appear in the main body, even in summary form.

- **Stopping criterion lacks justification**: The early stopping rule in SAMRE (break when two consecutive rounds agree on ordinal ranking) is reasonable intuitively, but no analysis is provided of how frequently it triggers across questions and models, what the distribution of rounds-to-termination looks like, or whether the trigger condition is sensitive to the threshold choice.

- **Statistical test unit of analysis is unclear**: Table 3 reports paired t-tests, but the unit of analysis is unspecified. If the test is over individual question pairs (n=3,300), the large sample makes nearly any effect reach p < 0.05, making p-values uninformative without accompanying effect sizes (e.g., Cohen's d).

- **"Optimal" in the title is unsupported**: No formal notion of optimality is defined, proved, or even conjectured. The paper compares two architectures against a single baseline, which does not support the title's claim.

### Tiny

- The conclusion discusses "scoring methods vs. ranking methods" as a finding ("scoring methods may offer more granular feedback"), but this is never directly tested in the reported experiments. This appears to be a claim inherited from Section 2.4 with no supporting experiment.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Computational cost breakdown**: A table or figure showing token usage, number of LLM calls, and latency per evaluation for Baseline, MORE, SAMRE, and SAMRE-without-juries would make the efficiency claims in Section 3.4 and Theorem 2 empirically grounded and allow practitioners to assess the accuracy/cost trade-off.
- **Breakdown of results by MT-Bench question category**: Reporting accuracy separately for coding, math, writing, etc. tasks would reveal whether gains are domain-specific or uniform, and would help future researchers know where the framework adds most value.
- **Prompt sensitivity analysis**: Given that multi-agent systems are known to be highly sensitive to prompt phrasing, a brief ablation over advocate/judge prompt variants would strengthen reliability claims.
- **Convergence case studies**: Short transcript examples showing SAMRE rounds where the verdict changed vs. where early stopping triggered would make the iterative mechanism tangible.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel finding in this paper — underappreciated by all three reviewers — is that the jury component, motivated by social choice theory and adversarial jurisprudence, actively *hurts* performance. This suggests a fundamental tension between the courtroom metaphor and the actual information dynamics of iterative LLM debate: adding a separate aggregation layer (jury votes) on top of an already-iterative judge may introduce a misalignment between the judge's accumulated context (which sees all rounds of argument) and the jury's aggregated binary vote (which collapses that context into a coarser signal). This is a substantive insight for the design of multi-agent evaluation systems — not just "juries didn't help" but potentially "late-stage aggregation that discards context is harmful." The paper does not pursue this interpretation, which is a missed opportunity.

## Suggestions

1. **Resolve the 3 vs. 5 juror inconsistency immediately** — confirm the exact experimental setup in both text and pseudocode, and consider reporting results for both jury sizes.
2. **Add an explicit model role table**: For each row in Table 1, clarify whether the listed model is acting as judge, advocate, or both, and specify which models generated the MT-Bench answers being compared.
3. **Investigate and explain the jury performance degradation**: At minimum, conduct an ablation over jury size (0, 3, 5), jury diversity, and jury voting rule; at best, provide a theoretical account of why majority-vote aggregation of binary jury decisions underperforms the judge's averaged continuous score.
4. **Add at least one multi-agent evaluation baseline** (e.g., a simple majority-vote ensemble of three independent judges) to establish whether the courtroom *structure* specifically drives the gains, or whether any multi-LLM aggregation would suffice.
5. **Report empirical cost metrics** (API calls per evaluation, approximate token count, wall-clock time) for all four conditions, even approximately. Theorem 2 is currently unsupported by any empirical evidence.
6. **Move the probabilistic error-reduction analysis from Appendix A into the main body** or at minimum provide a self-contained summary; this is advertised as a key contribution but completely absent from the main text.
7. **Report confidence intervals or standard deviation over multiple runs** (at least for a subset of models) to address the implausibly uniform result patterns in Table 1.
8. **Revise the title** to remove the word "optimal" unless a formal notion of optimality is introduced.

**Axis evaluations:**

- **Novelty**: Moderate-low. The courtroom metaphor for multi-agent LLM evaluation is a fresh framing, but the underlying mechanism (multiple LLMs debating) is well-explored. The specific combination of MORE and SAMRE architectures adds some originality.
- **Technical soundness**: Weak. The Aggregation Property is near-trivially true by construction; Theorem 2 is at odds with the paper's own empirical findings; the 3 vs. 5 juror inconsistency undermines reproducibility; and the theoretical proofs are entirely in appendices that cannot be verified.
- **Empirical support**: Weak. A single benchmark (MT-Bench), no multi-agent baselines, unexplained and suspicious uniformity in results, ambiguous model roles, and absence of cost metrics make the empirical claims difficult to evaluate or replicate.
- **Significance**: Conditionally moderate. If the results hold up under proper experimental conditions and the jury degradation finding is investigated, this paper could contribute meaningfully to the LLM evaluation design space. In its current form, the significance is limited.
- **Clarity**: Mixed. The algorithms are clearly presented; the narrative motivation is readable; but key details (juror count, model roles, Section 3.5, appendix-heavy theory) create substantial gaps in the main text.
