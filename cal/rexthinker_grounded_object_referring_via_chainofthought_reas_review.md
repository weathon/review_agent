# Harsh Critic Review
## Section-by-Section Critical Review

### Title & Abstract

The title accurately reflects the system's design. The abstract's core claims — SOTA on HumanRef, improved rejection, and strong generalization — are broadly supported by the experiments, but two qualifications need more transparency in the abstract: (1) "strong generalization" in out-of-domain settings is tested on a single dataset (RefCOCOg) and the zero-shot models underperform fine-tuned baselines by a non-trivial margin (~5–9 DF1 points); (2) the claim of "verifiable and interpretable" reasoning rests on post-hoc GPT-4o rationalizations as training data (detailed below) — a fundamental distinction the abstract should not obscure.

---

### Introduction & Motivation

The motivation is genuine and well-articulated. The two properties — verifiability and trustworthiness — are useful design criteria, and the analogy to human step-by-step reference resolution is apt. The paper correctly identifies that both coordinate-prediction and retrieval-based paradigms lack interpretability and struggle with rejection.

**One concern**: the paper positions its CoT approach as solving "hallucination," but hallucination in this context is operationalized only as over-detection (generating a box when none should exist). The claim that CoT reasoning causally reduces this is plausible but not carefully dissected. Is the gain in rejection score (53.5 → 68.2) from the reasoning structure itself, or simply from training on the rejection subset with answer-grounded supervision? This conflation of cause and effect persists throughout.

---

### Method / Approach

The two-stage architecture (Grounding DINO for proposals + MLLM for reasoning over candidates) is clean and well-motivated by Table 5's ablation showing neither component alone is sufficient.

**Critical issue — box hint dependency and oracle gap**: The pipeline's recall is fundamentally capped by Grounding DINO's recall. If a target object is missed by the detector, Rex-Thinker cannot recover it. The paper provides no oracle analysis: what would performance be if the box hints were perfect? This is important for understanding how much of the performance gap (against, say, coordinate-regression models) is architectural versus due to the detector bottleneck. Table 5 shows Grounding DINO alone has Avg. Recall of 87.3 on HumanRef — but this is with the category already specified. How sensitive is the pipeline to Grounding DINO's recall in harder out-of-domain scenes?

**Second issue — the category label requirement**: For out-of-domain RefCOCOg evaluation, the authors note they "use Grounding DINO and the original COCO category label" to generate box hints. In practical deployment, the category label might not be available or may be ambiguous. The paper doesn't discuss this constraint.

**GRPO reward design**: The accuracy reward uses exact IoU=1 matching (since box hints are provided as inputs, predicted boxes must identically match a hint). This is a reasonable simplification but effectively measures classification over a discrete set, not truly spatial reasoning. This design choice should be acknowledged as a constraint.

---

### Dataset Construction (HumanRef-CoT)

This section has the paper's most significant conceptual weakness.

**Post-hoc rationalization**: The GPT-4o annotation pipeline is designed as follows: GPT-4o is given the image, the referring expression, *and the ground-truth answer*, then instructed to generate reasoning that arrives at the known correct answer. The system prompt (shown in Figure 6/Appendix A.2.1) explicitly states: *"Note that I will tell you which objects are the ground truth that fit this description, you need to use the answer I give as a reference. But you can't refer to the answer I gave in your answer, pretend that you are thinking about it yourself."* 

This is not reasoning — it is **supervised backward rationalization**. GPT-4o is being asked to construct a plausible justification for a pre-specified answer, not to genuinely reason forward from the visual evidence. The resulting "reasoning traces" are trained into Rex-Thinker as if they were authentic reasoning chains. The paper's claims of learning "verifiable" and "trustworthy" reasoning are therefore overstated: the model is learning to *mimic the appearance* of reasoning, not to genuinely discover answers through deliberate inference.

This distinction matters considerably. If the reasoning traces were generated by GPT-4o actually solving the problem (without ground-truth answers), the training signal would be qualitatively different. The authors appear aware of this but frame it as quality control ("we provide GPT-4o with both the referring expression and its ground-truth answer") rather than as a fundamental limitation.

**Quality control scope**: The automated filtering removes 1.2–7.6% of samples. The human evaluation (600 samples, 5 Ph.D. students) finds zero high-level errors and only 1.2% low-level factual errors in intermediate steps. But "correctness" here is evaluated against the given ground-truth answer, not against independent visual assessment. This validates internal consistency, not genuine reasoning fidelity.

**Domain restriction**: HumanRef-CoT is entirely human-centric (people only). For a paper claiming a "grounded referring framework" applicable broadly, all supervised training is in a single, narrow domain. The zero-shot generalization to RefCOCOg (general objects) is interesting but one test case is insufficient to support broad generalization claims.

---

### Experiments & Results

**Table 3 — In-domain HumanRef evaluation**: The comparison is structured such that nearly all baselines are in zero-shot setting (not trained on HumanRef), while Rex-Thinker is trained on the HumanRef-CoT data. Only RexSeek-7B is trained in-domain. Rex-Thinker-GRPO outperforms RexSeek-7B by approximately +1.2 Avg. DF1 (83.5 vs. 82.3) and +14.1 rejection score (68.2 vs. 54.1). This is a reasonable but modest improvement in detection accuracy; the rejection score improvement is more notable.

The table presentation is misleading: comparing a trained model against zero-shot models on the training benchmark gives the appearance of larger gains than are warranted. The text says "most compared models were evaluated in a zero-shot setting" — this should be foregrounded in the main paper, not relegated to the appendix.

**Table 4 — Out-of-domain RefCOCOg**: This comparison has a serious confound. All baselines (RexSeek, Grounding DINO, Qwen2.5-VL, ChatRex) were trained on the RefCOCOg training set. Rex-Thinker-CoT and Rex-Thinker-GRPO are zero-shot. Despite this disadvantage, Rex-Thinker-GRPO (zero-shot: 83.2/83.3) still underperforms all baselines (84.0 to 90.0). Only Rex-Thinker-GRPO* (fine-tuned on RefCOCOg: 89.2/88.8) reaches competitive performance.

The abstract and introduction frame this as "strong zero-shot generalization," but 83.2 val versus 90.0 (ChatRex, trained on RefCOCOg) is a ~7-point gap. This should be characterized more carefully.

**Missing baselines on standard benchmarks**: The paper does not evaluate on RefCOCO/+/g as a fine-tuned system. All prior MLLM-based methods (ChatRex, Groma, Ferret-v2, etc.) report numbers on these benchmarks. Without in-domain RefCOCO/+/g results, it is impossible to know whether Rex-Thinker's gains on HumanRef come at the cost of general REC performance.

**Reasoning faithfulness not evaluated**: GRPO optimizes for final F1 reward only. The paper shows (qualitatively, Figure 4–5) that the reasoning traces remain structured after GRPO, but no quantitative measure of reasoning faithfulness or alignment between intermediate reasoning steps and final predictions is reported. It is possible that GRPO produces correct answers with increasingly shallow or incoherent reasoning.

**Ablation studies** (Section 5.4): The ablations justify the core design choices (box hints, CoT cold start) effectively. However, several natural ablations are absent:
- How does performance scale with dataset size for HumanRef-CoT?
- What is the sensitivity to Grounding DINO's quality (e.g., using a weaker detector)?
- Does the number of planning steps (simple vs. complex expressions) correlate with accuracy gain from CoT?

---

### Writing & Clarity

The paper is generally well-written. The description of the GRPO objective (Equations 1–5) is complete and clear. Figure 1 and Figure 4 effectively illustrate the reasoning process. The primary clarity issue is the underemphasis of the post-hoc rationalization problem in the data construction section.

---

### Limitations & Broader Impact

The paper's stated limitations section is brief and largely implicit. The following are not adequately addressed:

1. **The rationalization problem** in HumanRef-CoT is not acknowledged as a limitation — it is presented as a design choice. The distinction between genuine reasoning traces and answer-guided post-hoc rationalization should be discussed explicitly.

2. **Human-only domain**: The entire supervised training pipeline is human-centric. The paper does not discuss what happens when the referred category is not well-supported by human-centric training (e.g., objects with complex spatial relationships unlike those in HumanRef).

3. **Inference cost**: Rex-Thinker requires a two-step inference (detector + ~560-token MLLM generation). The latency implications compared to direct-prediction models are not discussed.

4. **Privacy and bias**: HumanRef-CoT involves celebrity recognition as a subset. The paper briefly mentions NSFW filtering but does not discuss concerns about face recognition or demographic bias in reasoning traces.

---

### Overall Assessment

Rex-Thinker presents a reasonable engineering contribution — a retrieval-augmented CoT framework for referring expression comprehension — with solid ablations and a publicly available codebase. The two-stage training approach (SFT cold start + GRPO) is principled, and the improvement in rejection/abstention behavior is a noteworthy practical advance. However, the paper's positioning overstates the degree to which it achieves "verifiable" reasoning: the HumanRef-CoT dataset is built from GPT-4o rationalization of known correct answers, not from genuine inference, and the model learns to mimic this structure rather than independently discover reasoning paths. The comparative experiments are partially confounded (most HumanRef baselines are zero-shot; RefCOCOg baselines are fully supervised), and the paper lacks evaluation on standard RefCOCO/+/g benchmarks. The zero-shot generalization gap (~7 points behind in-domain-trained baselines) is notably underplayed. For ICLR, these issues — particularly the data construction conceptual flaw and the limited breadth of evaluation — need to be addressed before the contribution can be assessed at full value.

# Neutral Reviewer
## Balanced Review

### Summary
Rex-Thinker proposes a novel framework for Object Referring Expression Comprehension (REC) that reformulates the task as a structured Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning process, specifically utilizing a planning–action–summarization paradigm to enhance interpretability and reduce hallucinations. The authors construct a large-scale CoT dataset (HumanRef-CoT) and employ a two-stage training pipeline combining supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to enforce grounded reasoning. Experimental results demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on the HumanRef benchmark, particularly in rejection scores, alongside strong zero-shot generalization to out-of-domain datasets like RefCOCOg.

### Strengths
1.  **Novel Reasoning Framework:** The explicit decomposition of object referring into **Planning, Action, and Summarization** stages provides a clear, human-like rationale for decisions. This structured approach addresses the "black box" nature of standard detectors by grounding each reasoning step in specific candidate boxes (Figure 1, Section 3.1).
2.  **Dataset Contribution:** The creation of **HumanRef-CoT** with over 90,000 CoT-style samples fills a critical gap in the literature regarding training data for reasoning-based REC. The rigorous two-stage filtering process (Section 3.2, Table 1) and human evaluation (Appendix A.2.2) validate the high quality of the annotations.
3.  **Improved Reliability and Trustworthiness:** The model significantly outperforms baselines in the **rejection score** (Table 3: 68.2 vs. 53.5 for RexSeek), demonstrating a concrete improvement in the ability to abstain from predictions when no matching object exists, a key requirement for deployment in safety-critical applications.
4.  **Effective Reinforcement Learning:** The integration of **GRPO** (Section 4.2) effectively refines the model’s reasoning capabilities beyond what SFT alone can achieve, with ablations in Appendix A.3.5 confirming the necessity of the cold-start SFT for successful RL optimization.

### Weaknesses
1.  **Inference Latency:** The CoT-based generation significantly increases inference time compared to direct prediction models. Appendix A.4.1 notes an average inference time of **6.68s** for Rex-Thinker-GRPO versus 1.13s for the Plain version, which raises concerns for real-time deployment.
2.  **Detector Dependency:** The pipeline relies on an open-vocabulary detector (Grounding DINO) to generate candidate boxes. This introduces a potential failure cascade where errors in initial detection cannot be corrected by the MLLM, as seen in the analysis of interaction cases in Appendix A.3.7.
3.  **Out-of-Domain Performance Gap:** While the zero-shot generalization on RefCOCOg is competitive, it still lags behind supervised SOTA baselines (Table 4: Zero-shot ~81% vs. Supervised ~90%), suggesting the learned reasoning does not fully transfer to significantly different object domains without further fine-tuning.
4.  **Reasoning-Answer Inconsistency:** Appendix A.4.2 explicitly acknowledges the model sometimes produces reasoning traces that do not perfectly align with the final answer (e.g., 9 candidates in reasoning, 8 in output), indicating a weakness in enforcing strict logical consistency during the GRPO phase.

### Novelty & Significance
**Novelty:** The paper introduces a significant methodological shift by treating REC not just as a detection task but as an explicit reasoning process supported by large-scale CoT data. The application of GRPO to refine grounding reasoning and the specific "Planning-Action-Summarization" structure are distinct contributions compared to existing direct prediction or retrieval-based baselines.
**Significance:** The focus on **trustworthiness** (rejection of hallucinated outputs) and **interpretable** reasoning is highly relevant to the current trajectory of AI safety and MLLM reliability. By providing concrete evidence of reduced hallucinations through CoT, this work offers a practical path toward deploying multimodal models in high-stakes environments.

### Suggestions for Improvement
1.  **Refine Reward Function for Consistency:** To address the reasoning-answer mismatch noted in Appendix A.4.2, introduce an explicit **consistency reward** in the GRPO phase that penalizes cases where the number of objects summarized in the reasoning trace differs from the final answer box count.
2.  **Optimize Inference Efficiency:** Investigate techniques to shorten the reasoning trace without sacrificing interpretability, such as distilling the reasoning process into a smaller model or limiting the depth of the "Planning" phase for simple queries to reduce the 6.68s latency.
3.  **Robustness Analysis of Detection Errors:** Provide a detailed failure mode analysis specifically focusing on scenarios where the initial detector proposes incorrect or missing candidates. Discuss how the MLLM attempts to handle (or fails to handle) these errors to give a complete picture of system robustness.
4.  **Ablation on Prompt Length:** Include an analysis on how the cost of reasoning (token generation) correlates with performance gains, perhaps identifying a "break-even" point where the overhead of CoT outweighs the benefit in accuracy.

# Spark Finder Review
## How to Improve This Paper

### Missing Experiments (top 3-5 only)
1. **Out-of-domain rejection evaluation is absent** — The paper claims improved trustworthiness via rejection capability, but rejection scores are only reported on in-domain HumanRef. Without out-of-domain rejection results, the claim that the model "drastically improves the ability to reject hallucinated outputs" in general settings is unsupported.

2. **RefCOCOg zero-shot results contradict generalization claims** — Table 4 shows Rex-Thinker-CoT/GRPO (81.2/80.3) performs significantly worse than baselines like ChatRex-7B (87.2) in zero-shot. This directly undermines the claim of "strong generalization in out-of-domain settings" and requires explanation or additional experiments.

3. **No comparison to other CoT-based referring methods** — The paper positions itself as a CoT reasoning approach but doesn't compare against LLaVA-CoT, LlamaV-o1, or other recent CoT MLLMs applied to grounding. Without this, it's unclear whether gains come from CoT specifically or other architectural choices.

4. **Detector quality ablation is missing** — The two-stage approach depends critically on the open-vocabulary detector's candidate quality. No experiment shows how performance degrades with weaker detectors or whether the MLLM can recover from poor candidate proposals.

### Deeper Analysis Needed (top 3-5 only)
1. **Quantitative interpretability Measure absent** — The paper claims improved "interpretability" and "verifiability" but provides no user study, automated metric, or quantitative evaluation of reasoning quality. These claims remain subjective without empirical validation.

2. **Reasoning-answer inconsistency not quantified** — Appendix A.4.2 acknowledges cases where reasoning traces don't match final predictions, yet this directly undermines the "verifiable" claim. The frequency and severity of these inconsistencies must be measured.

3. **Interaction category performance drop unexplained** — Table 14 shows recall decreases (-0.21%) for Interaction expressions with CoT. The paper hypothesizes occlusion issues but doesn't analyze whether the CoT structure itself causes conservative rejection or if this is a data limitation.

4. **Correlation between reasoning length and accuracy missing** — Appendix A.3.3 shows completion length stays stable (~560 tokens), but no analysis shows whether longer/more detailed reasoning correlates with higher accuracy or if the CoT is sometimes unnecessary overhead.

### Visualizations & Case Studies
1. **Side-by-side CoT vs. non-CoT on identical hard examples** — Current visualizations mostly show successful CoT cases. Direct comparison on the same challenging images would reveal whether CoT reasoning actually resolves ambiguities that direct prediction fails on.

2. **Reasoning-answer mismatch examples from Appendix A.4.2** — Figure 13 mentions inconsistencies but doesn't show concrete examples. Visualizing these failures would expose whether the reasoning trace is trustworthy or post-hoc rationalization.

3. **Failure cases where CoT reasoning is correct but prediction wrong** — This would reveal whether errors stem from reasoning flaws or from the final selection mechanism, directly testing the "grounded reasoning" claim.

### Obvious Next Steps
1. **Human evaluation of interpretability claims** — A user study measuring whether CoT traces actually help humans understand/verify predictions is essential for the interpretability contribution.

2. **Out-of-domain rejection benchmark** — Test rejection capability on RefCOCOg or similar datasets where some expressions have no matching objects. This is necessary to support trustworthiness claims.

3. **Ablation on CoT necessity per expression complexity** — Analyze whether complex expressions benefit more from CoT than simple ones. If CoT helps equally across all difficulty levels, the reasoning may not be adding genuine value.

# Final Consolidated Review
## Summary

Rex-Thinker reformulates object referring expression comprehension as an explicit Chain-of-Thought reasoning task, using a two-stage architecture where an open-vocabulary detector proposes candidate boxes and an MLLM performs structured "Planning–Action–Summarization" reasoning over these candidates. The authors construct HumanRef-CoT, a 90,824-sample dataset with GPT-4o-generated reasoning traces, and train the model via supervised fine-tuning followed by GRPO-based reinforcement learning. The approach achieves strong results on HumanRef, with particularly notable improvements in rejection capability (ability to abstain when no matching object exists), and demonstrates zero-shot generalization to RefCOCOg.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated structured reasoning framework**: The Planning–Action–Summarization decomposition provides an interpretable reasoning structure where each step is explicitly grounded in candidate boxes (Figure 1). This addresses a genuine limitation of prior MLLM-based referring methods that produce direct predictions without verifiable reasoning steps.

- **Significant improvement in rejection capability**: The model achieves a 68.2 rejection score on HumanRef versus 54.1 for RexSeek-7B (the strongest baseline also trained on HumanRef), representing a meaningful advance in the ability to correctly abstain from prediction when no object matches the expression—a critical capability for trustworthy deployment.

- **Rigorous ablation validating core design choices**: Table 5 demonstrates that neither standalone detectors nor MLLMs alone suffice for robust REC—detectors lack language comprehension (Avg. Precision: 25.7 even after fine-tuning), while MLLMs suffer from localization limitations (Avg. Recall: 69.4). The two-stage architecture is well-justified.

- **Effective two-stage training paradigm**: The ablation in Appendix A.3.5 confirms that CoT-based SFT cold-start is essential for GRPO success. Models trained with GRPO alone (without CoT initialization) produce unstructured reasoning traces (Figure 5), validating the principled training approach.

- **Large-scale dataset contribution**: HumanRef-CoT provides 90,824 structured reasoning annotations across six expression categories (attribute, position, interaction, reasoning, celebrity, rejection), enabling supervised training of grounded reasoning.

## Weaknesses

- **Post-hoc rationalization in data construction**: HumanRef-CoT is generated by providing GPT-4o with ground-truth answers and instructing it to produce reasoning traces that justify those answers. The system prompt (Figure 6) explicitly states: "Note that I will tell you which objects are the ground truth... pretend that you are thinking about it yourself." This produces backward rationalizations rather than genuine forward reasoning traces. The model learns to mimic the *appearance* of reasoning without necessarily learning to discover answers through authentic inference. The paper does not acknowledge this conceptual limitation.

- **Generalization claims are overstated**: The abstract claims "strong generalization in out-of-domain settings," but Table 4 shows Rex-Thinker-GRPO zero-shot achieves only 83.2%/83.3% on RefCOCOg val/test, notably lower than ChatRex-7B trained on RefCOCOg (87.2%/87.0%). Only after fine-tuning on RefCOCOg (Rex-Thinker-GRPO*) does it reach competitive performance (89.2%/88.8%). The zero-shot gap should be characterized more carefully.

- **Reasoning–answer inconsistency after GRPO**: Appendix A.4.2 acknowledges that the model sometimes produces reasoning traces inconsistent with final predictions (e.g., identifying 9 candidates in reasoning but outputting 8 boxes). This directly undermines the "verifiable reasoning" claim since the reasoning cannot be trusted to reflect the actual decision process. The frequency of such inconsistencies is not quantified.

- **No out-of-domain rejection evaluation**: The paper's central claim of improved trustworthiness via rejection capability is only evaluated in-domain (HumanRef rejection subset). Without testing rejection on RefCOCOg or other out-of-domain datasets, the generalization of this capability remains unverified.

- **Detector dependency without oracle analysis**: Performance is fundamentally capped by Grounding DINO's recall—if the detector misses a target object, the MLLM cannot recover it. Table 5 shows Grounding DINO achieves 87.3 recall, but no analysis examines how performance degrades with weaker detectors or whether the MLLM can correct candidate errors.

- **Missing comparison to other CoT-based MLLMs**: The paper does not compare against LLaVA-CoT, LlamaV-o1, or other recent CoT MLLM architectures applied to grounding. It is unclear whether gains come from the specific CoT formulation or from other architectural choices.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Quantitative interpretability evaluation**: A user study or automated metric measuring whether CoT traces actually help humans verify predictions would strengthen the interpretability claims.

- **Detector quality ablation**: Testing performance with weaker detectors (e.g., YOLO-World) would clarify the system's robustness to candidate proposal quality.

- **Analysis of reasoning length vs. accuracy**: Appendix A.3.3 shows stable completion length (~560 tokens), but it remains unclear whether complex expressions benefit more from CoT than simple ones.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:

- **"Most baselines are zero-shot on HumanRef"** — While true, this is disclosed in Appendix A.3.4, and the paper does include RexSeek-7B (trained on HumanRef) as the primary in-domain comparison. The improvement margin over RexSeek is modest but real (+1.2 DF1, +14.1 rejection).

- **"Category label requirement is a deployment limitation"** — For the stated scope of this work, using category labels for detector proposals is a reasonable design choice. This could be addressed in future work on fully open-vocabulary settings.

- **"Privacy concerns about celebrity recognition"** — The paper mentions NSFW filtering in Appendix A.2.4; celebrity recognition is a legitimate subset of referring expressions, and ethical considerations are noted.

- **"Missing RefCOCO/+/g fine-tuned results"** — The paper focuses on HumanRef training and zero-shot generalization. While in-domain results on standard benchmarks would strengthen the paper, their absence does not invalidate the core contributions.

## Novel Insights

The Planning–Action–Summarization framework reveals an important asymmetry in referring expression complexity: Table 14 shows CoT provides the largest recall gains in "Reasoning" (+5.29%) and "Attribute" (+4.48%) categories, but actually decreases recall in "Interaction" (−0.21%). This suggests the systematic candidate-by-candidate evaluation is most beneficial for compositional attribute reasoning but may struggle with spatial relationship reasoning when candidates overlap or occlude. The interaction category's unique challenge—requiring reasoning about relationships between multiple entities—is highlighted in Appendix A.3.7, where the authors hypothesize that occlusion and merged bounding boxes cause conservative (incorrect) rejections. This finding suggests future CoT formulations might need specialized handling for relational expressions, potentially by reasoning over pairs or groups of candidates rather than evaluating each individually.

## Suggestions

1. **Acknowledge the rationalization limitation explicitly**: Add a limitations section noting that HumanRef-CoT provides post-hoc justifications rather than authentic forward reasoning, and discuss implications for what the model actually learns.

2. **Add out-of-domain rejection evaluation**: Test on RefCOCOg expressions where no matching object exists to validate that rejection capability transfers across domains.

3. **Quantify reasoning–answer inconsistency**: Measure and report the frequency of cases where reasoning traces contradict final predictions; this is essential for assessing the trustworthiness of the "verifiable" reasoning.

4. **Include a weaker detector ablation**: Run experiments with a lower-capability detector (e.g., YOLO-World) to demonstrate robustness bounds and clarify the detector's role in overall performance.
