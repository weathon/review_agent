# Harsh Critic Review
## Section-by-Section Critical Review

**Title & Abstract:** The title is clear and accurately reflects the paper's core contribution: inductive reasoning for Temporal Knowledge Graphs (TKGs) with a focus on emerging entities. The abstract succinctly motivates the problem (closed-world assumption failing for emerging entities), presents the key insight (transferable patterns from semantically similar entities), summarizes the method (TRANSFIR with a codebook-based classifier), and quantifies the results (28.6% avg. MRR improvement). The claims are specific and supported by the later content.

**Introduction & Motivation:** The introduction effectively establishes the importance of TKG reasoning and clearly identifies the limitation of existing methods regarding emerging entities. The problem is well-motivated with real-world examples (new users, compounds). The distinction from static KG inductive learning (where new entities have *some* interactions) is crucial and correctly highlighted. The formal definition of the inductive reasoning task for emerging entities (Sec. 2) is precise. The contributions are listed clearly at the end of the introduction.

**Empirical Investigation (Sec. 3):** This is a major strength of the paper. The authors don't just propose a solution; they first empirically diagnose the problem from data, representation, and feasibility perspectives. The evidence is compelling: the ≈25% emergence rate (Q1), the visualization and *Collapse Ratio* metric for representation collapse (Q2), and the illustrative example of transferable patterns (Q3). This section provides a strong, data-driven foundation for the proposed method. A minor note: While the Collapse Ratio metric is inspired by prior work and seems sensible, a more detailed justification for its specific formulation and its direct link to downstream performance would strengthen this analysis.

**Methodology (Sec. 4):** The overall pipeline (Classification–Representation–Generalization) is logical and well-explained.
*   **Codebook Mapping:** The use of a VQ codebook on *frozen* textual embeddings is a clever design to avoid collapse and provide a stable, history-free prior. The explanation of the codebook and commitment losses is standard and appropriate. A key question is the choice of textual encoder. The paper uses BERT, and later experiments (Sec. 5.5) show robustness across PLMs, but the inherent quality and coverage of entity names/descriptions in the datasets (e.g., GDELT's noisy titles) is a fundamental limitation that could affect clustering quality. The authors acknowledge this in the ablation discussion.
*   **Interaction Chain Encoding:** The definition and construction of the Interaction Chain (IC) are clear. The Top-K selection based on relation similarity to the query is a reasonable heuristic to focus on relevant history. The encoding via component transforms and a Transformer is standard. The relation-guided attention (Eq. 8) is a good addition to make the chain encoding query-specific.
*   **Chain Pattern Transfer:** This is the core of the "transfer" mechanism. The idea of creating dynamic cluster prototypes by pooling IC representations of *query entities* and then using them to modulate the static embeddings of *all* entities (via a learned gating function Ψ) is innovative. However, the flow of information here requires careful scrutiny. The prototype **c**^dyn_k is computed from the IC embeddings **h**^IC_e of entities *that are query subjects/objects at time t*. How does this effectively transfer patterns to an emerging entity `e_new` that, by definition, is *not* a query entity at its first appearance `t_q = t_e(e_new)`? The answer seems to be that `e_new` is classified into cluster `k` via the VQ codebook, and then at time `t_q`, it receives the cluster prototype **c**^dyn_k built from the ICs of *other* (known) entities in the same cluster that were query entities at `t_q`. This is plausible but should be explicitly stated and justified. The pseudocode (Alg. 1, line 17) shows pooling over `eq : π(eq)=k`, confirming this.
*   **Overall Objective & Training:** The joint training of link prediction and codebook losses is standard. The use of ConvTransE as the scorer is fine. The complexity analysis in Appendix D.3 is adequate.

**Experiments & Results (Sec. 5):** The experimental setup is thorough and largely convincing.
*   **Datasets & Splits:** The use of a 5:2:3 chronological split (instead of 8:1:1) to induce more emerging entities is a good and necessary choice for evaluating the proposed task.
*   **Baselines:** The selection is comprehensive, covering graph-based, path-based, and static inductive methods. The effort to adapt static inductive methods to the temporal setting (by merging time windows) is noted. It is clear that these baselines are not designed for the "zero-history" emerging entity setting, which rightly highlights the novelty of the problem and the method.
*   **Main Results (RQ1):** The results in Table 1 are strong and consistently show TRANSFIR's superiority. The reported average improvement of 28.6% in MRR is significant.
*   **Analysis (RQ2):** The t-SNE visualizations and improved Collapse Ratio provide direct evidence that TRANSFIR mitigates representation collapse. The case study of clusters and pattern transfer is illustrative and helpful.
*   **Ablation Study (RQ3):** The ablation study (Fig. 5) effectively demonstrates the contribution of each component (IC, Codebook, Pattern Transfer, Textual Encoding). The observation about textual encoding on GDELT is honest and insightful.
*   **Extended Experiments (RQ4):** The experiments on the "Unknown" setting (providing some test-time history) are excellent. They show TRANSFIR's advantage persists even when some history is available, demonstrating its general utility beyond the strict zero-shot emergence moment. The hyperparameter sensitivity analysis is sufficient. The efficiency comparison (Fig. 7) is a strong point, showing TRANSFIR is memory-efficient.

**Limitations & Broader Impact:** The paper briefly mentions future work (improving textual embeddings with external knowledge/LLMs, handling emerging relations) which implicitly acknowledges current limitations (dependence on textual quality, not handling new relations). A dedicated "Limitations" subsection would be beneficial to explicitly discuss these points, e.g., the assumption that entity names/titles are sufficiently informative for semantic clustering, the sensitivity to the VQ codebook size `K`, and the fact that the method does not address emerging *relations*. The Ethics Statement is standard and appropriate. The Reproducibility Statement is good, with a promise of code.

**Writing & Clarity:** The paper is generally well-written and logically structured. Figures are informative. Some minor points: In Sec. 4.3, the description of how an emerging entity benefits from the cluster prototype at its first appearance could be more explicit. The pseudocode in Alg. 1 is very helpful.

### Overall Assessment
This is a strong paper that makes a clear and valuable contribution. It identifies a meaningful, real-world problem in TKG reasoning (emerging entities with zero history), provides empirical evidence of its prevalence and the failure of existing methods, and proposes a novel, well-motivated solution (TRANSFIR). The method is technically sound, combining ideas from VQ-VAEs, sequential encoding, and pattern transfer in an elegant way. The experimental evaluation is extensive, rigorous, and demonstrates significant improvements. The work meets the high standards of ICLR in terms of novelty, technical quality, and empirical validation. The most important concerns to address are a more explicit explanation of the pattern transfer mechanism for strictly zero-history entities and the addition of a dedicated limitations section. These do not undermine the core contribution.

# Neutral Reviewer
## Balanced Review

### Summary
This paper introduces TRANSFIR, an inductive reasoning framework for Temporal Knowledge Graphs (TKGs) that specifically addresses the challenge of predicting events involving *emerging entities*—entities that appear at test time without any historical interactions in the training data. The core idea is to transfer temporal reasoning patterns from known, semantically similar entities to these emerging ones. This is achieved through a three-stage pipeline: (1) a Vector-Quantized (VQ) codebook classifies entities into latent semantic clusters using frozen textual embeddings, (2) Interaction Chains (ICs) encode sequential past interactions of query entities, and (3) a pattern transfer mechanism propagates cluster-level temporal dynamics to provide informative representations for emerging entities.

### Strengths
1.  **Well-Motivated Problem and Empirical Analysis:** The paper begins with a thorough, data-driven investigation establishing the prevalence (~25% of entities) and challenge (severe performance drop, representation collapse) of emerging entities in standard TKG benchmarks. This provides strong justification for the proposed task and method.
2.  **Clear and Structured Methodology:** The proposed TRANSFIR framework is elegantly decomposed into Classification–Representation–Generalization stages. The use of a learnable VQ codebook over frozen textual embeddings is a clever design to provide stable, semantics-informed clusters without risking collapse for unsupervised emerging entities.
3.  **Extensive and Convincing Experimental Validation:** The paper includes comprehensive experiments across four standard TKG datasets, comparing against a wide array of strong baselines from graph-based, path-based, and inductive families. TRANSFIR demonstrates significant and consistent improvements (average 28.6% MRR gain). Ablation studies, sensitivity analyses, and efficiency benchmarks (lower GPU memory) are provided, making the empirical case very strong.

### Weaknesses
1.  **Baseline Comparability and Justification:** While many baselines are tested, several (e.g., CompGCN, InGram, MorsE) are designed for static, not temporal, KGs. Their adaptation to the temporal setting (as described in Appendix E.2) is a non-trivial modification. A more focused comparison and discussion on why standard TKG extrapolation methods fail, and how specifically designed inductive TK methods (like ALRE-IR) compare, would strengthen the narrative.
2.  **Limited Analysis of Codebook Learning:** The mechanism by which the VQ codebook discovers semantically meaningful clusters (e.g., "Country", "Civic & Parties") from only entity names and a task-driven loss is crucial but under-explored. An analysis of cluster consistency, sensitivity to entity name phrasing, or a comparison to supervised entity-type clustering would deepen understanding of this core component.
3.  **Dependence on Textual Information Quality:** The method's performance is inherently tied to the quality of pre-trained textual embeddings. The ablation shows performance degrades without them, and the authors note challenges with noisy GDELT text. The paper does not fully explore the consequences when textual descriptions are absent, highly ambiguous, or poorly aligned with the latent semantic structure needed for reasoning (beyond the single failure case in Appendix F.1).

### Novelty & Significance
**Novelty:** The paper makes a clear novel contribution by formally defining and addressing the inductive reasoning task for *emerging entities without historical interactions* in TKGs. The integration of a trainable VQ codebook for latent semantic clustering to enable temporal pattern transfer is a novel and technically sound approach for this setting.
**Significance:** The problem is practically significant, aligning with real-world dynamic systems where new actors constantly appear. The proposed solution is effective, as evidenced by the substantial performance gains. The work successfully bridges ideas from static KG induction and temporal reasoning, pushing forward the frontier of robust, open-world TKG forecasting.

### Suggestions for Improvement
1.  **Refine Baseline Comparisons:** Conduct a more targeted comparison with the strongest TKG reasoning baselines (e.g., LogCL, HisRes) and the few existing inductive TKG methods (e.g., ALRE-IR). A discussion or experiment explicitly analyzing *why* these models fail on emerging entities (beyond representation collapse) would sharpen the contribution.
2.  **Deeper Analysis of the Codebook:** Include a quantitative evaluation of cluster quality (e.g., purity if external entity types exist, or stability across runs). Analyze how cluster assignments evolve for an entity as it accumulates interactions. This would solidify the claim that the codebook learns transferable *semantic* priors.
3.  **Address the Textual Dependency Limitation:** Propose and evaluate (even preliminarily) a fallback or augmentation strategy for when textual information is weak. For instance, could initial clusters be seeded with a rule-based method or could the model learn to rely more on the interaction chain patterns when text is uninformative? Discussing this limitation and potential solutions more thoroughly is important for real-world applicability.
4.  **Clarify Experimental Details for Reproducibility:** The main paper mentions a 5:2:3 chronological split but does not specify how the validation set is used (e.g., for early stopping, hyperparameter tuning). Appendix E should explicitly state which set(s) contain emerging entities and the exact protocol for evaluating the "Emerging" and "Unknown" settings to ensure precise reproducibility.

# Spark Finder Review
## How to Improve This Paper

### Missing Experiments (top 3-5 only)
1. **Ablation on the core claim of "transferable patterns".** The paper claims patterns are transferred from known entities to emerging ones via semantic clusters. A critical missing experiment is to disable pattern transfer (e.g., by randomizing cluster assignments or using a single cluster) and show performance drops to near-baseline levels. Without this, the claimed mechanism is not causally verified.
2. **Direct comparison to a strong, truly inductive TKG baseline.** The baselines include static inductive methods (e.g., InGram) but not a recent, strong *inductive* TKG method adapted to the "emerging entity" setting (e.g., by using textual features). The claim of novelty is weakened without showing superiority over a properly adapted state-of-the-art inductive KG method.
3. **Analysis of performance relative to the "unknown" entity setting.** The paper shows TRANSFIR works well in the strict "emerging" (zero-history) setting. However, it should be compared directly on the same test queries as the "unknown" setting (where entities have some test-time history). This would isolate the benefit of the transfer mechanism versus simply using a few observed interactions.
4. **Sensitivity test to textual embedding quality/noise.** The method relies on frozen BERT embeddings. An experiment corrupting or ablating these embeddings (e.g., using random vectors, synonyms, or noisy descriptions) is needed to show robustness, especially given the note about GDELT's poor text quality.

### Deeper Analysis Needed (top 3-5 only)
1. **Quantitative evidence that the codebook clusters are semantically meaningful.** The paper shows a few cherry-picked clusters. A systematic analysis is needed: e.g., compute the consistency of cluster assignments with external entity types (if available) or use an external ontology to measure cluster purity. Without this, the "semantic similarity" claim is anecdotal.
2. **Isolating the effect of preventing representation collapse.** The paper shows improved Collapse Ratio but does not prove this is the *primary* reason for better performance. An analysis correlating per-entity collapse metrics (e.g., variance within its cluster) with its prediction rank would strengthen the causal story.
3. **Analysis of failure modes.** The paper mentions one failure case in the appendix. A systematic categorization of why TRANSFIR fails on certain emerging entities (e.g., due to poor textual embedding, outlier semantics, lack of similar known entities) is essential to understand its limitations.
4. **Component contribution breakdown beyond simple ablation.** The ablation removes whole modules. A finer-grained analysis is needed: e.g., what is the relative contribution of the codebook's clustering vs. its dynamic prototypes? How critical is the relation-guided attention in the IC encoder?

### Visualizations & Case Studies
1. **Visualize the "transfer" process for specific queries.** For a few example emerging entity queries, create a diagram showing: the entity's textual embedding, its assigned cluster and closest known entities, the specific interaction chains retrieved from those known entities, and how they inform the prediction. This would make the abstract mechanism concrete.
2. **t-SNE plots colored by ground-truth entity types (if available).** The current t-SNE only separates known vs. emerging. Coloring points by real-world categories (e.g., "Person", "Organization") would visually validate whether the learned latent clusters align with true semantics.
3. **Case studies of severe failures.** Alongside successes, show clear cases where transfer fails—perhaps where semantically similar entities behave differently, or where the text encoder provides misleading signals. This would better delineate the method's boundaries.

### Obvious Next Steps
1. **Controlled experiment on synthetic/controlled TKGs.** To unequivocally prove the method exploits transferable patterns, create a simple synthetic TKG where "emerging entities" are exact semantic copies of known entities with shifted timestamps. Show that TRANSFIR achieves near-perfect transfer while baselines fail.
2. **Incorporate a stronger baseline using LLMs for direct zero-shot prediction.** Given the use of textual embeddings, a baseline that uses an LLM (e.g., via prompting) to directly predict links for emerging entities based on their description and the query context would be a strong and relevant contemporary comparison.
3. **Hyperparameter analysis on the balance between codebook and link prediction losses (λ).** The paper studies many hyperparameters but not the critical weighting λ between L_lp and L_codebook. Sweeping this would show the trade-off between clustering quality and task performance.
4. **Evaluate on a dataset with explicit entity types/meta-data.** Using a dataset where entity types are known (e.g., YAGO) would allow a direct, quantitative evaluation of whether the learned clusters recover these types, strengthening the semantic transfer claim.

# Final Consolidated Review
## Summary
This paper introduces TRANSFIR, a novel inductive reasoning framework for temporal knowledge graphs (TKGs) that specifically addresses the challenging setting of predicting events involving *emerging entities*—entities that appear at test time without any historical interactions. The core idea is to transfer temporal reasoning patterns from known, semantically similar entities via a three-stage pipeline: a vector-quantized codebook classifies entities into latent semantic clusters using frozen textual embeddings, Interaction Chains encode relevant historical sequences, and a pattern transfer mechanism propagates cluster-level dynamics to emerging entities. Experiments demonstrate substantial improvements over a wide range of baselines.

## Strengths
- **Well-motivated problem with empirical grounding.** The paper begins with a thorough, data-driven investigation, establishing that emerging entities are prevalent (~25% of entities in standard benchmarks) and that existing methods suffer severe performance degradation and representation collapse on such entities. This provides a strong foundation for the proposed task.
- **Novel and well-structured methodological contribution.** The proposed TRANSFIR framework elegantly combines a learnable VQ codebook (for stable, semantics-informed clustering without collapse), sequential Interaction Chain encoding, and a novel pattern transfer mechanism to generalize temporal dynamics to unseen entities. The design is technically sound and addresses the core challenge directly.
- **Extensive and convincing experimental validation.** The paper includes comprehensive experiments across four standard TKG datasets, comparing against 17 baselines from graph-based, path-based, and inductive families. TRANSFIR demonstrates significant and consistent improvements (average 28.6% MRR gain). The evaluation is rigorous, including ablation studies, sensitivity analyses, efficiency benchmarks (lower GPU memory), and exploration of both strict "emerging" and more lenient "unknown" entity settings.

## Weaknesses
- **Dependence on textual information quality and limited analysis of learned clusters.** The method's first stage relies on frozen textual embeddings (e.g., from BERT) of entity names/descriptions for clustering. While an ablation shows performance degrades without them, and the paper notes challenges with noisy text (e.g., in GDELT), a deeper analysis of this dependency is missing. The claim that the codebook discovers *semantically meaningful* clusters is supported only by qualitative case studies (e.g., "Country", "Civic & Parties"). A more quantitative analysis of cluster quality (e.g., alignment with external entity types if available, or stability) would solidify this core claim and better delineate the method's boundaries.
- **Explanation of pattern transfer for strictly zero-history entities could be more explicit.** The mechanism by which an emerging entity at its first appearance `t_q` receives a useful cluster prototype **c**^dyn_k is central but somewhat implicit. The prototype is pooled from Interaction Chains of *other* (known) query entities in the same cluster at time `t_q`. While this is plausible and reflected in the pseudocode, the narrative in Sec. 4.3 could more directly walk through this critical step for a reader.
- **Baseline adaptation for static inductive methods.** Several strong baselines (e.g., CompGCN, InGram, MorsE) are designed for static, not temporal, KGs. Their adaptation (merging time windows) is a non-trivial modification. While necessary for comparison and the paper notes this adaptation, the results for these methods should be interpreted with this caveat in mind, as they are not natively designed for the temporal extrapolation task.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A more systematic analysis of failure modes, categorizing why TRANSFIR fails on certain emerging entities (beyond the single example in the appendix), would provide a clearer understanding of its limitations.
- Including a dedicated "Limitations" subsection in the main paper to explicitly discuss the textual dependency, assumption of informative entity names, current lack of handling for emerging *relations*, and sensitivity to codebook size would improve the paper's completeness.

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution*
- **Weakness: "Missing comparison to LLM-based zero-shot baseline."** This demands the paper address a direction (LLM prompting for direct prediction) outside its stated scope and methodological contributions. The paper uses PLMs for embeddings but is not about LLM-based reasoning.
- **Weakness: "Lack of controlled experiments on synthetic TKGs."** This demands methodological practices (synthetic data generation for proof) that are not standard or expected for evaluating TKG reasoning methods. The empirical evaluation on established benchmarks is sufficient.
- **Weakness: "Insufficient hyperparameter analysis for loss weight λ."** The paper includes extensive sensitivity analysis for key hyperparameters (codebook size, chain length, dimensions, layers). While λ is important, its omission from the sensitivity study is not a core flaw given the breadth of analysis provided.
- **Weakness: "Need for finer-grained ablation (e.g., clustering vs. dynamic prototypes)."** While interesting, this level of granularity goes beyond what is required to validate the core contributions. The provided ablation (removing entire modules) sufficiently demonstrates each component's importance.
- **Criticism about baseline justification being insufficient.** The paper adequately motivates the baseline selection (Sec. 5.1, App. E.2) and the failure of existing methods is empirically demonstrated in Sec. 3. Demanding a deeper theoretical discussion on *why* they fail is not required.

## Novel Insights
The paper provides a key novel insight: in temporal knowledge graphs, entities of similar semantic types exhibit transferable temporal interaction patterns (e.g., sequences of diplomatic visits for heads of state). This insight, empirically validated, directly motivates the core design of TRANSFIR—categorizing entities via a codebook and transferring cluster-level interaction dynamics—which enables reasoning for entities with zero interaction history, a scenario largely unaddressed by prior work.

## Suggestions
- In Sec. 4.3, add a concise, explicit explanation (perhaps with a short illustrative example) of how an emerging entity at time `t_q` obtains its cluster prototype **c**^dyn_k from the pooled Interaction Chains of other, known query entities in the same cluster at that same timestamp.
- Consider adding a quantitative measure of cluster quality if external entity type metadata is available for any dataset (even if only for a subset), or analyze cluster assignment consistency across random seeds, to strengthen the claim of learning semantically meaningful clusters.
