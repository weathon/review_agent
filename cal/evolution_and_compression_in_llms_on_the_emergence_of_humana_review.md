# Harsh Critic Review
## Section-by-Section Critical Review

**Title & Abstract**
The title clearly reflects the paper's core contribution: investigating the emergence of human-aligned categorization in LLMs via principles of evolution and compression. The abstract succinctly summarizes the problem, approach (two studies: English naming and IICLL), key findings (variation among models, Gemini's unique ability to recapitulate human-like IB tradeoffs), and the main conclusion (human-aligned categories can emerge via the same IB principle). All claims are supported by the paper's content.

**Introduction & Motivation**
The introduction effectively motivates the problem: understanding if LLMs, which are not trained for semantic efficiency, can develop human-aligned category systems. It clearly situates the work within cognitive science (IB framework, iterated learning) and identifies a gap in LLM research. The specific contributions are stated: 1) Analyzing English color naming across 39 LLMs, 2) Introducing IICLL to test inductive biases, 3) Showing that only models with strong in-context learning (Gemini) replicate the full range of human near-optimal tradeoffs. The link to practical implications (grounding for generative AI) is appropriate.

**Background and Motivation**
This section provides a solid foundation on color categorization, the IB framework, and iterated learning. The connection to prior LLM work (e.g., Marjieh et al., 2024; Zhu & Griffiths, 2024) is adequate but could be more deeply integrated to highlight the novelty of applying the full IB efficiency analysis and cultural evolution paradigm. The explanation of the IB model and its previous empirical support is clear and necessary for understanding the evaluation metrics.

**Experimental Setup**
The setup is generally clear and reproducible. The choice of 39 models across families, sizes, and training stages is a strength, allowing for meaningful analysis of factors influencing behavior. The use of the WCS grid and replication of human experimental procedures (Lindsey & Brown, 2014; Xu et al., 2013) is methodologically sound.
*   **Concerns:**
    1.  **Prompting and Constrained Generation:** The paper notes that for open-weight models, log-probability scoring was used to implement constrained choice because models often failed to generate allowed terms. This is a significant methodological detail. The choice of generation parameters (temperature=0.6, top_p=0.9) is stated but not justified. Could these settings influence the stochasticity of the encoder `q(w|m)` and thus the computed IB metrics? A brief justification or sensitivity analysis would strengthen the method.
    2.  **IICLL Procedure Details:** The description of the IICLL procedure (Section 3 and Appendix G) is somewhat fragmented. A clearer, sequential flowchart or pseudocode in the main text would aid reproducibility. The rationale for the sliding window of 10 previous interactions (Appendix K) is sensible but presented as a post-hoc analysis; integrating this rationale into the main method description would improve clarity.
    3.  **Stimulus Representation:** The finding that CIELAB inputs degrade performance (Appendix C) is important and well-handled. The decision to use sRGB for IICLL is justified as an "ideal condition," but it merits a brief discussion in the limitations: does this mean the observed bias is tied to the feature space (sRGB) most prevalent in the LLMs' training data, rather than a more general perceptual space?

**Results**
The results are presented comprehensively with supporting figures.
*   **English Color Naming (Section 4.1):** The correlation between model size/instruction-tuning and performance is clear. The analysis of the Olmo training trajectory (Appendix F) is a valuable addition. A minor point: the claim that "many prominent LLMs struggle" is supported, but a more precise statistical summary (e.g., the proportion of models below a certain alignment threshold) would be helpful.
*   **IICLL Results (Section 4.2):** The key finding is compelling: Gemini 2.0 evolves systems toward the IB bound and human alignment, while other high-performing models (Gemma, Qwen) converge to low-complexity solutions. The comparison to a nearest-neighbor baseline (Appendix M) effectively argues that Gemini's performance is non-trivial.
*   **Major Concerns:**
    1.  **Statistical Significance & Robustness:** The paper lacks any measures of statistical significance or uncertainty. For the IICLL trajectories, how variable are the chains? The human data (Xu et al.) involved multiple chains; how many chains were run per model per condition (`k`=2-6,14)? The main text mentions "chains" but does not specify the number of replications. This is critical for assessing the reliability of the convergence results. Error bars or confidence intervals on the trajectory plots are needed.
    2.  **Analysis of "Failure" Modes:** The paper excellently shows what successful evolution looks like (Gemini). However, a deeper analysis of *why* other models fail would significantly strengthen the contribution. For instance, the low-complexity convergence of Gemma and Qwen—is this due to weaker in-context learning, a different implicit prior, or prompt sensitivity? Some speculative discussion based on model architecture or training is warranted.
    3.  **The `k`=14 Condition:** This condition is presented as an "extreme-case sanity check" where only Gemini occasionally passed the comprehension threshold. This raises questions: If the model cannot reliably recall the training set, can the subsequent production phase be meaningfully interpreted as revealing an inductive bias? This should be framed more clearly as a boundary condition of the method rather than a core result.

**Writing & Clarity**
The paper is generally well-written. However, several figures are referenced in the text (e.g., Figure 1, 2a, 2b, 2c, 3, 4) but are not included in the provided text content. This makes it challenging to fully assess the narrative flow. Based on the captions and descriptions, the visual presentation seems appropriate. The structure is logical.

**Limitations & Broader Impact**
The discussion and future work section thoughtfully addresses key limitations: the need to integrate communicative pressure, the unclear origins of the efficiency bias, and the necessity to extend to more languages and domains. The broader impact statement is implicit but reasonable (improving human-AI alignment). One missing limitation is the dependency on specific prompt formulations and the constrained generation method, which may not fully reflect the model's "untethered" behavior. Additionally, the choice of color, while justifiable, is a highly structured, low-dimensional domain; the claim of potential generality (from the extra-domain experiment) is quite preliminary and should be tempered.

### Overall Assessment
This paper presents a novel, cognitively-grounded investigation into the inductive biases of LLMs. Its core strength is the rigorous application of the IB efficiency framework and the iterated learning paradigm to a large set of models. The finding that only the most advanced in-context learner (Gemini) recapitulates the full range of human efficiency tradeoffs is significant and suggests a non-trivial alignment emerging at the frontier of model capabilities. However, to meet ICLR's high bar, the paper must address the lack of statistical analysis for its key results and provide a more robust analysis of the number of experimental replications and variance. Furthermore, a deeper discussion of the failure modes of other models would elevate the contribution from a demonstration of a phenomenon to a more explanatory analysis. With these revisions, the paper represents a valuable contribution at the intersection of cognitive science and machine learning.

# Neutral Reviewer
## Balanced Review

### Summary
This paper investigates whether Large Language Models (LLMs) develop human-aligned semantic categorization systems driven by the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle of efficient compression, a framework with strong empirical support in human cognition. Using color naming as a testbed, the authors first evaluate 39 LLMs on an English color-naming task, finding that only larger, instruction-tuned models achieve high alignment and IB-efficiency. Second, to probe inductive biases beyond training data mimicry, they introduce Iterated in-Context Language Learning (IICLL), a cultural transmission simulation. Results show that LLMs, particularly Gemini 2.0, iteratively restructure random artificial systems toward greater IB-efficiency and human alignment, recapitulating patterns observed in human studies.

### Strengths
1. **Strong Interdisciplinary Framework**: The paper effectively bridges cognitive science (IB theory, iterated learning) and ML, providing a principled, theoretically-grounded lens to evaluate LLM semantic representations. The use of established human datasets (WCS, Xu et al. 2013) and the IB model from prior work allows for direct, quantifiable comparison.
2. **Comprehensive Empirical Evaluation**: The scale of the evaluation (39 models across 6 families, varying size, tuning, and modality) is impressive. The analysis of training dynamics (Olmo checkpoints) and the comparison of text vs. image inputs adds valuable depth.
3. **Novel Methodological Contribution**: The proposed IICLL paradigm is a clever adaptation of iterated in-context learning to study language evolution in LLMs. It is well-motivated by prior human experiments and provides a clear protocol for eliciting inductive biases.
4. **Rigorous Quantitative Analysis**: The use of IB efficiency loss, NID for alignment, and comparisons to optimal IB bounds and human data provides a robust, multi-faceted evaluation beyond simple accuracy metrics. The inclusion of a nearest-neighbor baseline (Appendix M) strengthens the claim that the observed effects are non-trivial.

### Weaknesses
1. **Domain Specificity and Generalizability**: The evidence is currently confined to the color domain. While the authors note a preliminary result in another domain (Appendix I), it is not detailed in the main text. For ICLR, stronger evidence or discussion about the expected generalizability to other perceptual or conceptual semantic domains is needed.
2. **Limited Mechanistic Explanation**: The paper identifies that model size, instruction-tuning, and in-context capability correlate with performance, but offers little insight into *why* these properties enable IB-efficient biases. The discussion on origins of the bias (Section 6) is speculative. A deeper analysis of model internals or training data properties could strengthen the contribution.
3. **Potential Confounding from Training Data**: Although the IICLL task uses pseudo-words, the models' strong performance on the English naming task suggests heavy exposure to color language data. The paper could do more to rule out that the IICLL results aren't indirectly influenced by learned priors over *color space structure* from this data, rather than a general IB bias. The CIELAB ablation (Appendix C) hints at this fragility.
4. **Negative Results for Many Models**: While insightful, the finding that most tested LLMs fail to capture English color naming efficiently could be seen as a limited advance for the ML community, emphasizing a deficiency rather than a new capability. The narrative is saved by Gemini 2.0's performance, but the reliance on a single, top-tier proprietary model may limit the reproducibility and broader impact of the central positive finding.

### Novelty & Significance
**Novelty**: The work is novel in several key aspects: (1) applying the IB efficiency framework systematically to evaluate LLM-internal semantic systems, (2) introducing the IICLL paradigm to study cultural evolution and inductive biases in LLMs, and (3) providing the largest-scale analysis of color categorization in LLMs to date, linking model architecture/training factors to cognitive alignment.

**Significance**: The significance is high for interdisciplinary research at the intersection of cognitive science and AI. It demonstrates that human-like semantic compression can emerge in LLMs without explicit IB training, suggesting a convergence between human and machine learning principles. For ICLR, it contributes a novel methodology for probing and evaluating LLM representations and biases, which aligns with the conference's interest in interpretability and understanding foundational model capabilities.

### Suggestions for Improvement
1. **Strengthen Generalizability Claim**: Conduct and report IICLL experiments in at least one additional, qualitatively different semantic domain (e.g., spatial terms, object categories) in the main text. This would powerfully demonstrate that the observed bias is not specific to color perception or the sRGB feature space.
2. **Deeper Analysis of Model Disparities**: Perform targeted experiments or analyses to hypothesize why Gemini 2.0 succeeds where other strong models (e.g., Llama 3.3 70B) do not. Is it purely scale/context length, training data diversity, or a specific architectural choice? Ablations on model size within a family or analysis of attention patterns could be insightful.
3. **Expand Discussion on Limitations and Future Work**: The future work section is good but could be more critical. Explicitly discuss the training data contamination concern and suggest experiments to disentangle learned priors about color space from a general compression bias. Also, discuss the implications of relying on API-based models for reproducibility.
4. **Improve Presentation for a ML Audience**: While the cognitive science background is well-explained, the core IB equations and metrics could be further motivated and connected to more familiar ML concepts (e.g., rate-distortion theory, clustering objectives) early on to enhance accessibility for the primary ICLR audience.

# Spark Finder Review
## How to Improve This Paper

### Missing Experiments (top 3-5 only)
1. **Control with non-linguistic clustering algorithms in IICLL.** The paper needs a direct comparison showing that the emergent IB-efficiency is specific to LLMs and not a trivial outcome of any clustering process given the task structure (e.g., k-means or nearest neighbor on sRGB features). Without this, the claim that LLMs possess a unique *human-like inductive bias* is not convincing.
2. **Ablation on input representation.** The core finding hinges on sRGB inputs. However, Appendix C shows models fail with perceptually uniform CIELAB inputs. An ablation is needed: does IICLL with CIELAB inputs still lead to IB-efficient systems? If not, the claimed bias may be an artifact of aligning with sRGB coordinate patterns in the training data, not a general efficiency principle.
3. **Testing memorization vs. generalization in IICLL.** The authors should explicitly test whether models are simply memorizing and regularizing the *random initial mappings* versus forming new categories. A control where initial "pseudo-words" are replaced with real English color terms would reveal if the bias is truly independent of prior linguistic knowledge.
4. **Systematic variation of IICLL hyperparameters.** The paper uses a fixed context window (10) and sampling strategy (6*k* examples). An ablation is needed on these parameters to show robustness. If efficiency only emerges under specific prompting conditions, it weakens the claim of a fundamental inductive bias.

### Deeper Analysis Needed (top 3-5 only)
1. **Quantitative analysis linking model architecture to the bias.** The paper correlates good performance with model size and instruction-tuning but does not disentangle these factors. A controlled analysis (e.g., comparing models from the same family across sizes and tuning stages) is needed to claim *which properties* cause the bias, rather than just noting a correlation.
2. **Analysis of pseudo-word semantics.** The claim that IICLL reveals a bias *independent of training data* is undermined if the nonsense syllables accidentally resemble real words or morphemes in the model's vocabulary. An analysis of the pseudo-words' embeddings or likelihoods is required to rule out this confound.
3. **Direct statistical comparison of IL trajectories between humans and LLMs.** The paper visually compares trajectories but lacks a statistical test (e.g., comparing convergence rates, final distribution distances) to substantiate the claim that Gemini "recapitulates" human ranges. Without this, the similarity is anecdotal.
4. **Error analysis for the English naming task.** For models that fail, a qualitative analysis of their errors (e.g., are they systematic confusions like merging green/blue?) is missing. This would clarify whether the failure is noise or a coherent but non-English system.

### Visualizations & Case Studies
1. **Visualization of IICLL chain dynamics for non-Gemini models.** Figure 3 shows only final generations. To understand why other models converge to low-complexity solutions, we need to see mode maps or category structures at intermediate generations. This would reveal if they collapse immediately or undergo restructuring.
2. **Case studies of failure modes in English naming.** Show concrete examples of prompts and model responses for poorly performing models (e.g., GPT-2). This would expose whether the failure is due to a lack of color knowledge, an inability to follow instructions, or coherent but alien categorization.
3. **Visual alignment of evolved categories with WCS languages.** For Gemini's final IICLL systems, a side-by-side visualization comparing the model's category boundaries to the most similar WCS language (beyond a single example in Appendix I) would strengthen the claim of human-alignment.

### Obvious Next Steps
1. **Test the generality of the bias in another semantic domain.** The paper's claim is about "human-aligned semantic categories" in general, but evidence is only from color. A minimal experiment in another grounded domain (e.g., geometric shapes, sounds) was necessary for this paper, not as future work.
2. **Incorporate a communicative task.** The IB principle is fundamentally about communication. An obvious extension missing from this paper is to test if IB-efficient categories emerge when two LLMs interact to solve a reference game, moving beyond passive iterated learning to active communication.
3. **Analyze the training data for color associations.** To rule out that good models are just regurgitating English-like categories from web text, a targeted analysis of the prevalence and nature of sRGB-coordinate descriptions in the training corpora (e.g., via data attribution methods) should have been conducted.

# Final Consolidated Review
## Summary
This paper investigates whether Large Language Models (LLMs) develop human-aligned semantic categorization systems via the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle of efficient compression, a framework well-supported in human cognition. Using color naming as a testbed, the authors first evaluate 39 LLMs on an English color-naming task, finding that only larger, instruction-tuned models achieve high alignment and IB-efficiency. Second, to probe inductive biases beyond training data mimicry, they introduce Iterated in-Context Language Learning (IICLL), a cultural transmission simulation. Results show that LLMs, particularly Gemini 2.0, iteratively restructure random artificial systems toward greater IB-efficiency and human alignment, recapitulating patterns observed in human studies.

## Strengths
- **Strong, Principled Interdisciplinary Framework**: The paper effectively bridges cognitive science (IB theory, iterated learning) and machine learning, providing a rigorous, theoretically-grounded lens to evaluate LLM semantic representations. The use of established human datasets (WCS, Xu et al. 2013) and the replication of human experimental protocols allows for direct, quantifiable comparison.
- **Comprehensive and Insightful Empirical Evaluation**: The scale of the evaluation—39 models across 6 families, varying size, instruction-tuning, and modality—is a significant strength. The analysis of training dynamics (Olmo checkpoints) and the inclusion of a nearest-neighbor baseline (Appendix M) to show the non-triviality of Gemini's performance add considerable depth.
- **Novel Methodological Contribution**: The proposed IICLL paradigm is a clever and well-motivated adaptation of iterated in-context learning to study language evolution in LLMs. It provides a clear protocol for eliciting inductive biases beyond patterns in training data.

## Weaknesses
- **Heavy Reliance on a Single, Proprietary Model for the Core Positive Finding**: The key result—that LLMs can recapitulate the wide range of near-optimal IB tradeoffs observed in humans—is demonstrated convincingly only by Gemini 2.0. While other strong models (Gemma 3, Qwen 2.5) are tested, their convergence to low-complexity solutions means the most striking evidence of human-like bias hinges on one frontier model, which may limit reproducibility and broad understanding of the phenomenon's origins.
- **Potential Dependency on Feature Space and Prompting Details**: The core results are obtained using sRGB color coordinates. Appendix C shows that performance degrades significantly with perceptually uniform CIELAB inputs, suggesting the observed bias may be tied to the feature space (sRGB) most prevalent in LLMs' training data, rather than a general bias operating over perceptual spaces. This dependency warrants deeper discussion as a limitation.
- **Insufficient Analysis of Why Other High-Performing Models Fail**: The paper identifies that model size, instruction-tuning, and in-context capability correlate with success, but offers little insight into *why* other capable models (e.g., Llama 3.3 70B Instruct) fail to evolve efficient systems in IICLL. A deeper mechanistic discussion or targeted hypotheses about architectural or training differences would strengthen the explanatory power of the work.
- **Unstable Boundary Condition in the IICLL Paradigm**: For the extreme `k=14` vocabulary condition, the paper notes that only Gemini "occasionally passed the comprehension threshold." If a model cannot reliably recall the training set, the subsequent production phase may not reliably reveal an inductive bias. This should be more clearly framed as a methodological boundary condition rather than a core result.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Extended Evidence of Generalizability**: The paper mentions a preliminary result in another domain (Appendix I). Including a more substantial analysis of IICLL in at least one other qualitative domain (e.g., geometric shapes) in the main text would powerfully support the claim that the observed bias is not specific to color.
- **Deeper Discussion of Training Data Influence**: While IICLL uses pseudo-words, a more detailed discussion could help disentangle whether the bias stems from learned priors about *color space structure* from web data versus a more general compression bias. Suggesting concrete experiments to test this would be valuable.
- **Clarification on Experimental Replications**: The number of IICLL chains run per model and condition (beyond the example chains shown) could be stated more explicitly in the main text for full clarity on robustness, though the human study it replicates also used limited chains (four per condition).

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution*
- **Weakness: "Lacks statistical significance measures or error bars on IICLL trajectories."** The human study (Xu et al., 2013) that this work replicates also presented results from a limited number of chains without error bars. The paper shows multiple example chains (e.g., Figure 13) and the analysis focuses on qualitative patterns of convergence, which is standard in this line of cognitive science work.
- **Weakness: "Prompting and generation parameters (temperature, top_p) are not justified."** The choice of standard generation parameters is reasonable for obtaining model responses, and the paper details its constrained generation method (log-probability scoring) to ensure fair comparison.
- **Weakness: "The paper should include a control with non-linguistic clustering algorithms in IICLL."** This control experiment is already conducted and reported in Appendix M (Nearest Neighbor baseline), which strengthens the paper's claim.
- **Weakness: "Failure to capture English naming is a limited advance."** This observation is a core empirical finding that sets the stage for the more nuanced IICLL analysis. It is valuable for diagnosing model capabilities and is not presented as the primary contribution.
- **Strength/Weakness about writing style, formatting, or missing figures.** These are nitpicks not relevant to the scientific contribution.

## Novel Insights
The paper provides a compelling demonstration that a drive for information-theoretically efficient compression—a principle argued to underlie human semantic systems—can emerge in state-of-the-art LLMs without explicit training for this objective. The key novel insight is that this efficiency bias surfaces most strongly under cultural evolutionary pressure (simulated via IICLL) and is not merely a reflection of memorized training data patterns. Furthermore, the finding that only the model with the strongest in-context learning capabilities (Gemini 2.0) fully recapitulates the human range of near-optimal tradeoffs suggests that this human-like inductive bias may be an emergent property at the frontier of model scale and capability, hinting at a convergence between human and machine learning principles.

## Suggestions
- To address the reliance on a single model, consider conducting a more granular analysis of the failing models (e.g., Llama 3.3 70B). For instance, analyze whether their collapse in IICLL is due to prompt sensitivity, context window limitations, or a fundamentally different implicit prior. This would transform the finding from a demonstration of a capability in one model to a more explanatory account of what architectural or training factors enable it.
- In the limitations section, explicitly discuss the implications of the sRGB/CIELAB discrepancy. Acknowledge that the observed bias is demonstrated within the sRGB feature space and that its operation in a perceptually grounded space like CIELAB remains an open question, which is important for claims about general, human-like perceptual categorization.
