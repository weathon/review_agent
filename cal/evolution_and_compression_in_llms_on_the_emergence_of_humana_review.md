# Harsh Critic Review
## Section-by-Section Critical Review

**Title & Abstract:** The title accurately reflects the paper's dual focus on evolution (via IICLL) and compression (IB-efficiency) in LLMs. The abstract clearly states the central question, methods, and key findings. Claims are specific and appear supported by the subsequent content.

**Introduction & Motivation:** The introduction is well-structured, effectively motivating the problem of semantic alignment between humans and LLMs. It clearly situates the work within cognitive science (IB framework, color categorization) and machine learning (understanding LLM inductive biases). The contributions are stated upfront and are substantial. One minor point: while the practical implications of color understanding for AI are mentioned, a slightly stronger case for why this specific cognitive alignment matters for core LLM capabilities or safety (beyond color applications) could strengthen the motivation for an ICLR audience, though the foundational science argument is valid.

**Method / Approach:**
*   **English Color Naming:** The procedure is clear and reproducible. The choice of 39 models across families, sizes, and training stages is a major strength, enabling insightful comparisons. The use of both text (sRGB) and image inputs for multimodal models is thorough. The reliance on log-probability scoring for open-weight models to enforce constrained choice is a practical and justified solution, though it inherently differs from the free-generation approach used for Gemini (via API). The authors should briefly discuss whether this methodological difference could bias comparisons, though it's unlikely to change the overall trends.
*   **IICLL Paradigm:** This is a novel and clever adaptation of iterated learning to LLMs. The procedure is described in detail in the main text and Appendix G. A key design choice is the inclusion of a sliding window of recent interactions to promote coherence. The sanity checks (comprehension accuracy) are appropriate. A significant concern is the **choice of initial random systems**. The paper states that for k=2..6, initial data came from Xu et al. (2013), but for k=14, they were randomly generated. This means the k=14 condition is not directly comparable to the human experiments, which is fine for exploring model capability but should be explicitly noted as a difference when comparing to human data (e.g., in Fig. 4c, the "human IL" points are for k≤6, while the LLM data includes k=14).
*   **Evaluation Metrics:** The use of efficiency loss (ε) and NID-based alignment is standard and appropriate for this line of work. The definitions are clear.

**Experiments & Results:**
*   **English Color Naming (Section 4.1):** The results are comprehensive and well-presented. The finding that larger, instruction-tuned models achieve better alignment and efficiency is clear from Figures 2, 7, 8. The analysis of the Olmo training trajectory (Fig. 10, App. F) is particularly valuable, showing that alignment jumps significantly during the second training stage (which includes instruction data). A **major point for discussion** is the interpretation of models that perform poorly on English but show some alignment with WCS languages (noted on pp. 5-6, 21). The authors suggest this indicates a "human-like color category" bias. This is an interesting hypothesis, but it requires more cautious phrasing. An alternative explanation is that these models are learning *some* coarse structure from textual descriptions in their training data, which may accidentally resemble *some* WCS systems. Without evidence that these models *systematically* converge to human-like systems under cultural evolution (IICLL), the claim of a human-like bias is premature. This should be toned down or presented as a speculative observation for future work.
*   **IICLL Results (Section 4.2):** This is the core novel finding. The result that Gemini 2.0 alone recapitulates the range of human IB tradeoffs is striking and well-demonstrated in Figure 3. The nearest-neighbor baseline (App. M) effectively rules out a trivial feature-clustering explanation for Gemini's performance, strengthening the claim of an emergent inductive bias.
    *   **Critical Concern - Model Selection for IICLL:** The paper states that only models performing well on the English task were used for IICLL (Gemini, Gemma 27B, Qwen 32B, Llama 70B). However, the key finding hinges on Gemini's unique performance. It is crucial to ask: **Was the strongest in-context learner (Gemini) also the best at the English task?** If so, the two capacities are confounded. The authors attribute Gemini's success to its "strongest in-context capabilities," but this is somewhat post-hoc. A more direct analysis or at least a discussion is needed: Is there a correlation between English-alignment/complexity and final IICLL performance across the four tested models? Figure 4 suggests Gemma and Qwen converge to low-complexity solutions; is this because they are worse at in-context learning, or because they have a different inductive bias? Some additional analysis or commentary on this point would greatly strengthen the argument.
    *   **Statistical Assessment:** The paper presents trajectories from multiple chains (presumably 4 per k-condition, following Xu et al.). While trends are visually clear, some measure of variance across chains (e.g., shaded regions in Fig. 4a,b,c) would help assess the robustness of the evolutionary trajectories.
*   **Additional Domain (Section 4.3):** The mention of preliminary results in another domain is good but too vague. For an ICLR submission, even a brief description of the domain, method, and a figure in the appendix would significantly bolster the claim of generalizability.

**Writing & Clarity:** The paper is generally well-written. The narrative flows logically from the naming study to the iterated learning study. Figures are central to the story. Some specific points:
*   Figure 1 is an excellent overview. The connection between panels (a), (b), and the IICLL paradigm in (c) could be explained more explicitly in the caption or text.
*   The captions for Figures 2, 3, and 4 are very detailed, which is good. However, the main text sometimes refers to findings in the figures without sufficient interpretation (e.g., on p.6, the reference to Fig. 9 in the context of WCS alignment could be elaborated).
*   The appendices are extensive and necessary. The prompt examples in App. J are helpful for reproducibility.

**Limitations & Broader Impact:** The discussion section and the "future work" paragraph appropriately list limitations: the need to integrate communication pressure, unclear origins of the bias, and the need to extend to more domains and languages. The broader impact statement is implicit but reasonable: understanding LLM alignment with human cognition. A limitation not explicitly addressed is the **dependence on prompt formulation and decoding strategy**. While the methods are described, the sensitivity of the results (particularly the IICLL dynamics) to these choices is unknown. A brief acknowledgment would be appropriate.

### Overall Assessment
This is a strong, interdisciplinary paper with a clear narrative and substantial empirical work. Its main contribution is demonstrating that a state-of-the-art LLM (Gemini 2.0), when subjected to simulated cultural transmission (IICLL), exhibits an inductive bias toward IB-efficient semantic categorization that closely mirrors human biases. The large-scale model evaluation in the first study provides important context. The primary concerns are: (1) the somewhat circular argument linking Gemini's IICLL success to its in-context learning ability without direct evidence, (2) the need for more cautious interpretation of poor English performers showing WCS alignment, and (3) the very preliminary nature of the evidence for generalization beyond color. Despite these issues, the core finding is novel, significant for understanding emergent structure in LLMs, and methodologically interesting. With minor revisions to address the points above, particularly the first, this paper would be a solid contribution to ICLR.

# Neutral Reviewer
## Balanced Review

### Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) possess a human-like inductive bias for efficient semantic categorization, as formalized by the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle. Using color categorization as a testbed, the authors conduct two large-scale experiments: 1) an English color-naming study across 39 diverse LLMs, and 2) a novel cultural evolution simulation called Iterated in-Context Language Learning (IICLL). The key finding is that while many LLMs fail to align with human English color naming, the largest instruction-tuned models (especially Gemini 2.0) not only achieve high alignment and IB-efficiency but also, through IICLL, evolve artificial category systems toward the same near-optimal IB tradeoffs observed in human languages.

### Strengths
1. **Strong Cognitive Science Foundation**: The paper is rigorously grounded in established theories of categorization (IB principle, iterated learning) and uses a domain (color) with uniquely rich, standardized human data (WCS, Lindsey & Brown). This provides a solid, falsifiable framework for evaluating LLMs.
2. **Impressive Scale and Model Analysis**: The evaluation spans 39 models across 6 families, systematically varying size, instruction-tuning, and modality. The analysis of learning trajectories (e.g., Olmo checkpoints) and the separate treatment of text vs. image inputs adds significant depth.
3. **Novel and Clever Methodology**: The introduction of Iterated in-Context Language Learning (IICLL) is a creative and methodologically sound extension of prior iterated learning paradigms to LLMs. It effectively tests for inductive biases beyond mere training data mimicry.
4. **Clear, Significant Core Finding**: The result that only the model with the strongest in-context learning (Gemini 2.0) recapitulates the full range of human near-optimal IB tradeoffs is striking, well-supported by the data, and speaks to a non-trivial emergent capability.
5. **Strong Reproducibility and Transparency**: The paper provides a detailed reproducibility statement, commits to releasing code and data, and meticulously documents prompts, model IDs, and hyperparameters in the appendices.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited Linguistic and Cultural Scope**: The study is almost exclusively anchored on English color naming and the WCS. While the IICLL uses pseudo-words, the initial bias is tested only against English alignment. Testing alignment with other specific languages or a more diverse set of semantic domains (beyond the brief mention in Appendix I) would strengthen the claim of a "human-aligned" universal bias.
2. **Under-explanation of Model Disparities**: The paper shows that model size, instruction-tuning, and in-context capability correlate with performance, but offers limited mechanistic insight into *why*. For instance, what specific properties of instruction-tuning or Gemini's architecture drive this bias? A deeper ablation or analysis could address this.
3. **Input Representation Sensitivity is a Major Confound**: Appendix C shows that performance degrades severely when using perceptually uniform CIELAB coordinates instead of sRGB, indicating a heavy reliance on the specific textual format of color data seen in pre-training (e.g., web/Code data). This challenges the generality of the findings and suggests the "bias" may be partly an artifact of learning statistical correlations in a specific data format, not a fundamental pressure for perceptual efficiency.
4. **Incomplete Integration with LLM Literature**: The discussion could better relate the findings to broader work on LLMs' world knowledge, grounding, and emergent capabilities. How does this IB bias connect to other measures of semantic representation in LLMs?
5. **Presentation of IICLL Results**: The main text figures (e.g., Fig 3, 4) are somewhat cluttered and could be simplified for clearer messaging. The reliance on appendices for critical results (e.g., the failure of smaller models in IICLL in App L) slightly fragments the narrative.

### Novelty & Significance
**Novelty is high.** The work successfully bridges cognitive science theory (IB, iterated learning) with modern LLM evaluation in a novel way. The IICLL paradigm is a genuine methodological contribution for eliciting inductive biases in LLMs. The systematic discovery that state-of-the-art LLMs can evolve human-like efficient categories *without being trained for it* is a new and important finding.
**Significance is substantial.** For cognitive science, it suggests that the IB efficiency principle may be a general property of powerful learning systems, not just humans. For AI/LLM research, it provides a rigorous, cognitively-grounded framework for evaluating semantic alignment and generalization. The findings have implications for building AI that communicates efficiently with humans and adapts to new domains.

### Suggestions for Improvement
1. **Address the Input Representation Issue Head-On**: The CIELAB vs. sRGB results warrant deeper discussion in the main text. Is the bias for *perceptual* efficiency or for *textual-coordinate* efficiency? Experiments or analysis disentangling the model's understanding of the color space from its familiarity with sRGB strings are crucial.
2. **Deepen the Analysis of "Why Gemini?"**: Conduct a more targeted analysis to hypothesize why Gemini 2.0 uniquely succeeds. Is it scale, training data diversity, a specific architectural feature (e.g., its multimodal training), or its exceptional in-context learning? Even a well-informed speculation would add value.
3. **Expand the Notion of "Human-Alignment"**: Include an analysis of alignment with a broader set of WCS languages (beyond English) in the main English-naming task results. This would test whether poorly-English-aligned models might still be aligned with *some* human system.
4. **Clarify the Relationship to Prior LLM Grounding Work**: Briefly discuss how these results confirm, extend, or contradict prior studies on color in LLMs (e.g., Marjieh et al., 2024; Patel & Pavlick, 2022) in the introduction or discussion.
5. **Streamline Visual Presentation**: Consider consolidating figures to more directly compare human and LLM IICLL trajectories (e.g., overlay key results from Xu et al. and Gemini in a single simplified plot) and highlight the critical finding—Gemini's unique ability to span the IB frontier—more prominently.

# Spark Finder Review
## How to Improve This Paper

### Missing Experiments (top 3-5 only)
1. **Ablation on the impact of training data and instruction-tuning.** The paper attributes performance differences to model size and instruction-tuning but provides no controlled experiment (e.g., fine-tuning a base model on instruction data) to isolate this factor. Without this, the claim that these properties are necessary for IB-alignment is correlational, not causal.
2. **Systematic comparison to simpler inductive bias baselines in IICLL.** The nearest-neighbor (NN) baseline is only shown for k=14. To convincingly argue that LLMs exhibit a unique *human-like* bias, they must compare IICLL trajectories against a range of algorithms (e.g., k-means, Gaussian mixture models, other clustering algorithms) across all vocabulary sizes to show LLMs are uniquely attracted to the IB front.
3. **Control experiment for the sliding window history.** The use of a 10-interaction history window is a major intervention in the IICLL paradigm, but its necessity and impact are not rigorously tested. An ablation showing results with no history and various window sizes across all models is needed to rule out that the emergent structure is an artifact of this specific prompting design.
4. **Expanded IICLL tests on more models.** The central claim about Gemini’s unique capability is based on testing only four models in IICLL. To generalize the finding that "only a model with strongest in-context capabilities" recapitulates human range, they must include other models with purported strong ICL (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) to see if the result holds or is specific to Gemini.

### Deeper Analysis Needed (top 3-5 only)
1. **Quantitative link between in-context learning ability and IB-efficiency.** The paper claims Gemini's success is due to its "strongest in-context capabilities," but provides no independent measure of ICL performance for the tested models. Without correlating a standard ICL metric with final IB-efficiency in the IICLL task, this explanation is speculative.
2. **Analysis of failure modes and low-complexity convergence.** The paper notes many models converge to low-complexity solutions but does not analyze why. A diagnostic analysis (e.g., examining the entropy of the encoder q(w|m), or the models' tendency to collapse labels) is needed to understand whether this is a representational limitation, a prompting artifact, or a different bias.
3. **Error analysis for the English naming task.** Many models perform poorly, but it's unclear if errors are systematic (e.g., consistent but non-English categories) or random noise. Analyzing the structure of "poor" systems (e.g., their alignment to WCS languages) is crucial to distinguish between a lack of bias and a different, but still structured, bias.

### Visualizations & Case Studies
1. **Side-by-side visualization of successful vs. failed category evolution.** The paper shows final mode maps but not the intermediate chaotic or collapsing structures for failing models. Animated or multi-panel figures showing the progression of a chain that collapses to one label versus one that forms structured categories would visually substantiate the claims about divergent inductive biases.
2. **Case studies of IICLL chains that converge to specific WCS languages.** Figure 13 shows final generations resemble some WCS languages, but a direct, stimulus-by-stimulus comparison highlighting where the evolved system matches and diverges from the human language would strengthen the claim of "human-alignment."

### Obvious Next Steps
1. **Include at least one other semantic domain in the main experiments.** The paper suggests the result may generalize but only provides a brief, non-quantitative mention. For an ICLR paper, a minimal quantitative analysis in another grounded domain (e.g., simple shapes, sounds) is necessary to support the claim of a general principle.
2. **Analyze the origin of the efficiency bias in pre-training data.** The paper leaves the origin of the bias as future work. A basic analysis correlating the frequency/co-occurrence statistics of color terms in the models' presumed training data with their emergent IB-efficiency would be an expected first step to rule out simple memorization.
3. **Test the interaction of communicative pressure with transmission.** The discussion rightly notes the importance of integrating communication. A straightforward extension within their framework would be a simple discriminative or referential game between two instances of the same LLM to see if utility pressure accelerates or changes the convergence toward IB-efficiency.

# Final Consolidated Review
## Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) develop human-aligned semantic categories through the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle of efficient compression. Using color categorization as a testbed, it conducts a large-scale English naming study across 39 models and introduces a novel cultural evolution simulation (Iterated in-Context Language Learning, IICLL). The key finding is that only the most advanced model (Gemini 2.0) recapitulates the range of near-optimal IB tradeoffs observed in human languages, revealing an emergent inductive bias toward efficient semantic systems.

## Strengths
- **Strong interdisciplinary grounding**: The paper rigorously integrates cognitive science theories—the IB framework and iterated learning—with LLM evaluation, leveraging rich human data (World Color Survey, English color naming) to formulate falsifiable hypotheses. (Sections 2.1-2.3, use of established IB model and human datasets.)
- **Comprehensive model analysis**: It systematically evaluates 39 LLMs across families, sizes, instruction-tuning status, and modality (text vs. image), providing nuanced insights into factors that correlate with human alignment and IB-efficiency. (Section 3, Table 1, Figures 2,7,8.)
- **Methodological innovation**: The introduction of Iterated in-Context Language Learning (IICLL) is a clever adaptation of iterated learning paradigms to LLMs, enabling direct testing of inductive biases beyond training data mimicry. (Section 3, Figure 1c, Appendix G.)
- **Clear and significant core result**: Gemini 2.0 uniquely evolves artificial color-naming systems that span the near-optimal IB frontier observed in human languages, a striking demonstration of an emergent human-like bias in a state-of-the-art model. (Figures 3,4, comparison to human IL data.)

## Weaknesses
- **Input representation confound**: Performance degrades severely when using perceptually uniform CIELAB coordinates instead of sRGB (Appendix C), indicating that the observed bias may be tied to familiarity with specific textual formats rather than a general drive for perceptual efficiency. This challenges the claim of a fundamental alignment with human color perception.
- **Speculative attribution to in-context learning**: The paper attributes Gemini’s success to its "strongest in-context capabilities," but provides no independent measure of in-context learning performance for the tested models. Without this, the explanation is post-hoc and undermines the mechanistic insight. (Section 4.2 relies on correlation, not causation.)
- **Overinterpretation of poorly aligned models**: While some models that fail at English naming show resemblance to certain WCS languages, the paper suggests this indicates a "human-like color category" bias without supporting evidence from IICLL or controlled analysis. This risks overclaiming where alternative explanations (e.g., coarse data-driven patterns) are plausible. (pp. 5-6, 21.)
- **Lack of statistical robustness in IICLL trajectories**: The evolutionary dynamics are presented without measures of variance across chains (e.g., error bands or confidence intervals), making it difficult to assess the consistency and reliability of the convergence patterns. (Figures 3,4 show point estimates only.)

## Nice-to-Haves
- Analysis of failure modes in IICLL (e.g., why some models collapse to low-complexity solutions) to better understand the representational or inductive limitations.
- Inclusion of additional models with strong in-context learning reputations (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) in IICLL to test the generality of the link between ICL capability and IB-efficiency.
- Deeper investigation into the origins of the bias, such as correlating efficiency with properties of pre-training data or instruction-tuning protocols.

## Novel Insights
The paper demonstrates that advanced LLMs can exhibit an inductive bias toward information-theoretically efficient semantic categorization without being explicitly trained for this objective, mirroring a key principle observed in human language evolution. This suggests that IB-efficiency may emerge as a general property of powerful learning systems under cultural transmission pressures, bridging cognitive science and machine learning in a novel way.

## Suggestions
- Measure and report a standard in-context learning metric for the models used in IICLL to quantitatively substantiate the claim about Gemini’s capabilities.
- Add variance estimates (e.g., shaded regions or error bars) to the IICLL trajectory plots to enhance statistical credibility.
- Refine speculative language about models with poor English alignment; present their WCS similarities as an observation for future work rather than evidence of a human-like bias.
