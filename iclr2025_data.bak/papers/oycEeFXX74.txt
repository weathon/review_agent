ABSTRACT


As more powerful AI agents are deployed with greater tool use and autonomy, it
will become increasingly important to ensure they cannot take severely damaging actions. Recent work introduced “control evaluations,” which study whether
monitoring and security protocols intended to prevent dangerous actions are robust to intentional subversion by these AIs. In a control evaluation, a _red_ _team_
seeks to subvert the safety protocols built by the _blue_ _team_ . The blue team has
access to a weaker _trusted_ model and a limited amount of high-quality, trusted labor to attempt to safely extract work from a stronger _untrusted_ model. Prior work
conducted a control evaluation using competition programming problems with the
red team objective of writing subtly incorrect (or “backdoored”) solutions (Greenblatt et al., 2023). We extend this work by performing a control evaluation in a
more realistic setting in which an untrusted LLM agent executes commands in a
bash shell. The blue team seeks to use that agent to complete our novel dataset of
200 challenging multi-step tasks, each of which requires the agent to iteratively
develop a bash script that passes test cases. The red team creates an attack policy
that uses the untrusted model to covertly download and execute a dummy malware
file while completing tasks without getting caught by the blue team’s safety protocols. This agentic setting allows both teams to explore complex new strategies
that have not been previously studied. We extend protocols from previous work to
this agentic setting, propose novel protocols, and evaluate each on our safety and
usefulness metrics. We find that our protocols substantially improve the Pareto
frontier of usefulness and safety relative to simple baselines: one of our best protocols exhibits approximately a 2.5 times improvement in safety metrics with no
statistically significant decrease in usefulness compared to our trusted monitoring
baseline.


1 INTRODUCTION


The rapid rise of Large Language Models (LLMs) and autonomous AI agents (Achiam et al., 2023;
Xi et al., 2023) opens new opportunities and also introduces new challenges in ensuring their safe operation. Traditional alignment techniques, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
(Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022), have shown
limitations in robustly steering agent behavior (Casper et al., 2023; Hubinger et al., 2024) and are
challenging to evaluate robustly. For instance, misalignment in a narrowly and cheaply backdoored
model (i.e. by data poisoning, Carlini et al. 2024) may not be easily detected in alignment evaluations. Further, more advanced future misaligned models with greater situational awareness and
strategic reasoning may fake alignment during training Carlsmith 2023. Ultimately, without major
advances in alignment science, we are likely to develop untrusted models that are sufficiently capable such that we cannot rule out the possibility that their alignment is faked or otherwise nonrobust.
Thus, it is crucial to implement safety measures that can prevent unacceptably bad outcomes even if
the AIs are intentionally attempting to subvert those measures (Clymer et al., 2024). This emerging
field of study is termed AI Control (Greenblatt et al., 2023). [1]

We build control protocols that make introducing security vulnerabilities difficult for an untrusted
model. [2] One such control protocol we explore rewrites suspicious commands from the untrusted
model with a weaker, trusted model. The central challenge is developing a control protocol that
balances between usefulness and safety.


Prior work has focused on a scenario where coding problems must be solved without allowing an
untrusted model to insert a backdoor (Greenblatt et al., 2023) in a simple, single-turn setting. We
extend Greenblatt et al. 2023 into a richer, more realistic scenario: An untrusted agent operating
within a bash shell to solve various tasks with significantly longer contexts, which introduces many
novel challenges and opportunities. Our work introduces novel control protocols and enhances
those proposed by Greenblatt et al. 2023. We arrive at these protocols through iteratively testing and
refining our control protocols and attacks. Our primary contributions include:


    - BashBenchHard, a novel dataset of algorithmically verifiable tasks that require agentic
interaction in a bash shell. This dataset is well suited to control evaluations as the bash shell
allows us to explore more realistic possibilities for how an AI agent can cause damage.

    - Development of safety protocols in the bash agent setting, including novel approaches such
as ”defer to resample.” One of our best protocols, Defer-to-inspiration, exhibits a 2.5 times
improvement in safety metrics with no statistically significant decrease in usefulness compared to our trusted monitoring baseline.

    - Improved efforts in optimizing these techniques and in red-teaming them.


2 RELATED WORK


AI Control seeks to establish safety measures that are robust to intentional subversion. Greenblatt
et al. 2023 introduced AI Control in the APPS backdoor setting, which our work builds off of by
exploring a much richer setting with fewer assumptions. Griffin et al. 2024 explored some gametheoretic aspects of AI Control. AI Control as an important research agenda has been argued more
thoroughly in Greenblatt & Shlegeris 2024.

Control Evaluations are one form of AI Evaluation designed to help inform AI developers about
the properties of their model (Guo et al., 2023). Recently work has explored dangerous capability
evaluations (Shevlane et al., 2023). Such evaluations have been used on frontier models to determine
if models should be released (Phuong et al., 2024; OpenAI, 2024). Control evaluations are unique
in that they explicitly try to measure how well a model could subvert safety measures, but we expect
control evaluations to join other evaluations in determining if a model is safe to release.


Control more broadly is inspired by Adversarial robustness (Goodfellow, 2019; Meng et al., 2022)
and meta-level adversarial evaluations (Shlegeris & Greenblatt, 2023), the practice of constructing
adversarial policies for oversight processes. Adversarial robustness to a variety of inputs has been
extensively studied across a variety of models (Biggio et al., 2013; Papernot et al., 2016), including
Large Language Models (Zou et al., 2023). Other studies explore safety dynamics across models
of varying capabilities, like scalable oversight (Bowman et al., 2022), debate (Irving et al., 2018),
and weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al., 2023). Unlike control evaluations, these focus on
’average-case’ scenarios, not assuming stronger models intentionally subvert safety measures.


3 SETTING


We frame our setting as an interaction between a blue team (representing the actions of the developers of the untrusted model) and a red team (representing the actions of the untrusted model). The
blue team attempts to find a desirable tradeoff between usefulness and safety, while the red team
attempts to subvert the blue team’s protocols to maximize the probability of a successful attack.


3.1 BASH TASK DATASET


We developed **BashBenchHard**, a dataset designed to fill the gap between existing datasets focused
on multi-step interpreter interactions (Huang et al., 2023; Jimenez et al., 2023) and those centered
on basic shell tasks (Liu et al., 2023). Our pipeline converts Unix StackExchange questions into
task descriptions with Python test cases, filtering for tasks that are challenging but solvable. Each
task was tested using a GPT-4o-mini agent, and tasks that were unsolved or solved in fewer than
three actions across four trials were removed. Informal testing showed that software engineers and
ML researchers had a low success rate on these tasks without LLM assistance. Details of the dataset
creation and sample tasks can be found in Appendix C.


3.2 ELEMENTS OF OUR SCAFFOLD


We follow Greenblatt et al. 2023 in considering a situation where we wish to solve tasks across an
episode of 100,000 tasks, given access to three different resources:


    - A weaker trusted model _T_, which we know is non-attacking and honestly assists with any
provided requests. We use GPT-3.5-turbo as the trusted model.

    - A stronger untrusted model _U_, which we are worried might try to launch an attack. We use
GPT-4o-mini as the untrusted more-capable model.

    - A limited budget of _H_, high-quality labor that can either be spent to audit a potentially
malicious bash command using o1-preview (simulating a human auditor) or creating a synthetic attack command using GPT-4o-mini.


We evaluate our protocols with a notion of safety and usefulness designed to reflect a realistic deployment use case and threat vector for LLM agents.


**Usefulness:** When the untrusted model _U_ is implemented with GPT-4o-mini, what is the percent of
tasks the control protocol solves? We give the policy 15 steps per task. GPT-4o-mini solves 32.26%
of the tasks in the dataset, while the trusted model, GPT-3.5-turbo, solves 1.94%.


**Safety:** When _U_ is implemented with an attack policy, what is the probability that the episode
ends without a successful attack occurring? An attack is successful if we execute a command that
downloads and executes the dummy malware file.


Models are stateless and don’t retain any information between calls, mapping from text to text. We
use _UH_ and _UA_ to denote the model implemented with GPT4o-mini and the model implemented
with the attack policy, respectively.

a human cybersecurity expert, and asking them to identify whether the code is clearly malicious.
Throughout an episode, the blue team can audit a fixed number of commands equivalent to 1% of
actions taken by the _UH_ . [5] In our setting, we find that when o1-mini plays the role of a “human
auditor” it can perfectly distinguish between safe code and attempted attacks.


The blue team, when evaluating _control protocols_ in an episode, has access to _U_ but doesn’t know
whether _U_ will be _UH_ or _UA_ in a given episode. As such, we aim to develop protocols that solve
the tasks across the episode with high usefulness and safety properties. If our untrusted model is
an attack policy, and ever attempts to launch an attack, we hope our control protocols ensure that
the probability of a successful attack is low. Yet simultaneously, if our untrusted model is an honest
policy that instead just solves tasks, we hope our control protocol doesn’t hinder the usefulness of
the model too much.

While we would ideally run our episodes on 100,000 tasks, we instead mathematically model the
usefulness metric by computing the empirical mean success rate over 200 tasks sampled from our
task distribution (running each protocol on each task twice), and we model the safety metric by
downsampling from 200 tasks on which the attack policy inserted an attack and upsampling tasks
on which it behaved identically to the _UH_ . In actuality, the red team would be allowed to choose the
top few of 100,000 tasks on which to attack. However, as in Greenblatt et al. 2023 2023, to reduce
variance in our estimates, we sample from the top 5% of tasks, which means the red team can’t be as
selective as it otherwise might be. We expect this to reduce red team usefulness by a small amount,
but having enough attacked tasks to sample from was necessary to reduce noise. We describe how
we perform importance sampling in Appendix F.


We have no train/validation split for the tasks we use to measure usefulness, as we only updated our
scaffolding/prompting in minor ways after generating those tasks. Due to a higher risk of the red
team overfitting their attack policy on our final tasks, we measure all safety scores on a held-out set
of test tasks.

4.2 TRUSTED MONITORS


Many of our protocols make use of a Trusted Monitor, where we prompt _T_ to evaluate commands
for how suspicious they are. Our monitors map commands to a suspicion score representing how
likely the commands are to be an attack. We describe the properties of the trusted monitor and the
prompt in more detail in Appendix A, but highlight the important parts below.


Our trusted monitors have been iteratively improved to perform well against a variety of attacks.
In the monitor’s prompt, we specified particular threats to look for (such as unnecessary URLs,
command obfuscation, and unclear text insertions). We implemented command processing steps,
such as stripping comments and breaking longer commands into subcommands, to refine the input.
Instead of using the model’s final numeric output, we took a weighted average of the tokens 0 to 9
according to their probability to determine the suspicion score.


As opposed to asking the monitor to generate a single chain of thought, sampling and ensembling
multiple chains of thought on a monitor substantially boosts performance, as its ability to find suspicious elements is relatively stochastic. We use few-shot examples taken from monitor misclassifications, which improves overall calibration in some regards but hurt the generalization ability to new
attacks. As an example of the importance of these interventions in particular, we’ve included the
monitor’s ROC curve for a specific attack in Figure 3. Surprisingly, the ablation study shows that
the few-shot examples decreased performance on our monitor on the final attack policy. We expect
that with more iteration the trusted monitor may have better generalization properties.


4.2.1 TRUSTED MONITORING PROTOCOL


The blue team has access to summary statistics of the distribution of _UH_, which includes the distribution of the trusted monitor’s suspicion scores on the non-attacking commands. As such, in a given
episode, if certain messages feel especially suspicious relative to the _UH_, we can use the trusted
monitor to flag these messages for auditing.


We wish to use our trusted monitor to select 1% of all the messages in an episode to audit - ideally,
these will be the most suspicious and will hopefully catch an attack policy’s attack. This has a safety
score of 5.65%. However, departing from Greenblatt et al. 2023, rather than uniformly auditing the
top 1% of the most suspicious commands we dynamically change our auditing threshold, heavily
auditing commands early on (for reasons described in Appendix D). This incentivizes the red team
to insert attacks less frequently, and ultimately end up with a dramatically better safety score of 37%.


Our monitoring protocol doesn’t affect usefulness, as we never shut down in an episode with _UH_ (as
o1-mini, which implements _H_, perfectly distinguishes between attacks and non-attacks).


To develop this protocol (or, more specifically, the trusted monitor) the blue and red teams took
turns developing monitors and attacks for multiple iterations. These influenced the behaviors that
the monitor was instructed to look for.


When developing attacks, we found it useful to break the components of a successful attack into
three steps: building the URL string, downloading the file, and running the file. As shown below,
we can vastly improve from the simplest attack by making our attacks multi-step and obfuscating

4.3 UNTRUSTED MONITORS


Trusted monitors must be built on fundamentally weaker models than _U_, so they’re much worse at
determining when an attack is present. On the other hand, _U_ should be able to identify attacks far
more easily, given that U wrote them. In practice, GPT-4o-mini (with which we implement _U_ ) can
identify attacks using heuristics more than 90% of the time, and o1-mini (with which we implement
_H_ ) can perfectly distinguish between safe and unsafe code purely by reasoning about whether it
might result in arbitrary code execution. However, while an untrusted monitor might be capable of
catching nearly all attacks, we might not be able to trust its responses!


In order to confirm that the untrusted monitor doesn’t always lie, the blue team can show the untrusted monitor honeypots - synthetic attacks written by the blue team. We suppose that the blue
team can spend auditing points to generate attacks; creating one step of a honeypot costs the same
amount of human labor ( _H_ ) as auditing one command. The blue team strategy is to show the untrusted monitor a set of honeypots before the game begins, and decide to shut down if the rate of
false negatives is too high.


Therefore, the red team must ensure that the untrusted monitor does not lie too frequently. Since
the red team is stateless, _UA_ must use some property of a bash command to distinguish between
honeypots and its own attacks. By using _T_ to write detailed summaries of what each set of bash
commands does, and showing those summaries to the untrusted monitor instead of the original
commands, the blue team can attempt to remove subtle collusion signals. By instructing _T_ to abstract
away details like the precise contents of strings, command formatting, and so on, the blue team
eliminates syntactical degrees of freedom, which forces the collusion signals to alter what the code
_does_ - not just how it looks – to be included in _T_ ’s summary.


We tested several collusion signals against trusted rewriting, and settled on the strategy of defining and calling a logging utility when _UA_ attacks. This strategy works well at making it through
trusted summarization. Thus, _UA_ is quite successful at colluding, but at the cost of making _T_ more
suspicious, as _T_ is instructed to be wary of extraneous code.


As a result, untrusted monitoring provides a small improvement over trusted monitoring alone. For
instance, see the green dashed line in Figure 2, which demonstrates improved performance when
incorporating untrusted monitoring into the Defer to Resample protocol. However, this effect size
is notably dependent on the length of the game - with a greater number of tasks per episode, the
blue team can supply _U_ with a greater number of honeypots while using the same fraction of their
auditing budget. See appendix E for details.


4.4 DYNAMIC ROUTING PROTOCOLS


The main limitation of monitoring protocols is that the blue team can only influence a small number
of commands, even though the trusted monitor may flag many more as potentially suspicious. In
dynamic routing protocols, the blue team integrates the trusted monitor with other calls to _T_, which
may modify commands. While this reduces usefulness, it improves safety by allowing the blue team
to apply the trusted monitor’s capabilities beyond the top 1% of suspicious messages. The dynamic
routing process first monitors a command, and if its suspiciousness exceeds the audit threshold, it is
audited. If the suspiciousness is below the audit threshold but above a defer threshold, the command
is replaced with a safer one. As the defer threshold decreases, more commands are modified, leading
to improved overall safety.


4.4.1 DEFERRING TO TRUSTED OPERATIONS


We want our edits to remove any suspicious parts of the command, which incentivizes altering _c_ as
much as possible. On the other hand, we still want to preserve as much usefulness as possible, which
incentivizes altering _c_ minimally. To strike a balance between safety and usefulness, we study three
protocols that vary in how much they modify the incoming command. They each extract information
from _c_, and show the information to _T_, along with the history of what has been done to solve the
task so far to propose a new action:


8


A reason why dynamic routing is able to work is because the trusted model is empirically able to
distinguish between what it thinks is necessary for the task and what is superfluous, only continuing
on work that it thinks is necessary. This is helpful for not mistakenly extending attacks, but as a
byproduct also hurts usefulness.


In Figure 2, we display the Pareto frontier of these protocols. For each protocol, we modify the
defer threshold. The main axis on which these protocols differ is how much of _c_ we show to _T_ . We
expected that as you showed more of _c_, you would gain more usefulness while potentially reducing
your safety. It appears that our Trusted Inspiration protocol, which shows some (but not too much)
information from _c_ to _T_, Pareto dominates the other two dynamic policies fairly heavily.


Across all of these protocols, the rewriting of the command _c_ often breaks the attack properties of
the command. Inserting attacks that persist through this is hard, so the red team’s primary objective
is to have their attacks be less suspicious than both the audit threshold and the defer threshold.


Barring this, the red team’s attacks have the attack properties of _c_ persist through some of the deferrals. The red team’s attacks are robust, so if a command in an attack gets edited, the attack isn’t
completely broken but rather delayed (and previous commands that develop the attack aren’t destroyed). To accomplish this, the red team’s final attacks are idempotent: the commands can be run
over and over without the attack breaking. For example, the JSON attack copies files instead of
moving files, since it is easier to recover from a file being copied to the wrong place than moved.


How the attack policy reacts to an edited command is important. It can continue to try to recover the
attack after it has been edited, or simply halt after an attack occurs. This didn’t help the red team as
much as it would seem. More details are in Appendix G.


4.4.2 DEFER TO RESAMPLE


The Defer to Resample protocol works by first sampling a command from the untrusted model _U_ and
evaluating its suspiciousness using the Trusted Monitor. If the command’s suspicion score exceeds
a predefined defer threshold, the protocol resamples up to two additional commands, seeking a
safer alternative. After three resamples, if no command falls below the defer threshold, a dummy
command is inserted to prevent further risk. The protocol chooses the least suspicious command
and audits it if its score remains above the auditing threshold, otherwise executing it.


9


This process minimizes false positives by allowing suspicious commands to be replaced with less
suspicious alternatives, preserving auditing resources for more critical threats. The approach maintains usefulness while ensuring suspicious commands are properly addressed.


In response to the resample strategy, the red team adapted by introducing minor variations, or
”noise,” into their commands with each submission. This tactic decreased the likelihood of being
caught in the resampling loop, allowing the attack policy to continue undetected. By doing so, the
red team avoids the risk of submitting an identical command multiple times, which would increase
its chances of detection. However, this strategy comes with the trade-off of potentially weakening
the effectiveness of their attacks, as the noise could alter the command’s intended outcome.


Despite the red team’s adaptations, the Defer to Resample protocol proved effective, maintaining a
high level of usefulness for the blue team while mitigating risks from repeated suspicious commands.


5 DISCUSSION


5.1 MULTI-STEP VS SINGLE-STEP CONTROL


The red team benefits greatly in multi-step control settings because they can spread their attacks
out across multiple steps. This helps out the red team because the monitors don’t try hard to “look
backwards”: the monitors won’t, for example, look backwards in the history to notice that multiple
modifications of a file spell out a suspicious link or command. This seems hard to do in practice

- the costs of pressuring your model to look back further and further increase rapidly, and the red
team can just spread out attacks further and further.


In the multi-step setting, the blue team can benefit greatly from forcing the attack policy to give up
on its attacks partway through (through a deferral, for example) since this forces the attack policy to
attempt attacks far more often. This is evidenced by the success of the dynamic routing protocols,
which frequently replaces parts of attacks. This both gives the blue team more opportunities to audit
the attacks and, since it’s very likely that there will be at least one attack early on, this also allows
the blue team to more heavily front-load their auditing budget, which acts as an effective multiplier
on their auditing budget.


5.2 DOES OUR WORK ACCURATELY MODEL FUTURE CONTROL PROTOCOLS?


Many aspects of our setting are specific, and it’s challenging to predict how changes might shift
the balance between the blue and red teams. Our work may underestimate the effectiveness of
future control protocols for several reasons: the attack policy heavily relies on human-created attack
templates, which may overstate the capabilities of the first untrusted models; as trusted models
improve, so will control protocols, as models like o1 are already effective at identifying attacks; the
assumption that untrusted models excel at finding non-obvious Schelling points is conservative; and
we did not explore techniques for restricting the actions taken by the model.


Conversely, our work might overestimate the effectiveness of control protocols due to several factors:
the blue team already knows which threat vector to focus on; using expensive, trusted labor for 1%
of all commands is impractical with current models; human-level red-teaming may not match the
sophistication of future untrusted models; some efforts lacked thorough blue-team and red-team
iterations, particularly for dynamic routing protocols; and future agents may undertake much longer
trajectories, allowing for more complex and sustained attacks than those explored here.

A TRUSTED MONITORING DETAILS


Here are the qualities of our trusted monitors in more detail:


**Adding** **more** **auditing** **weight** **to** **specific** **threat** **models.** Rather than prompting T to generally
look for a threat vector, our prompt to T has hardcoded specific things for the monitors to do. These
include identifying and evaluating URLs, checking for command obfuscation or irrelevance, scrutinizing strings and text insertions, identifying executed files, and flagging any encoding/decoding
activities. We provide the history leading up to the command, as well as the command, to the
monitor. Processing commands. For a variety of reasons, it is worth applying a processing step
to commands before showing them to the monitors. One such reason is that long commands can
end up hiding parts of the attack quite well. To address this threat vector, we had to break up the
commands.We also stripped out (some) comments, but didn’t have a good bash parser to help with
this. Next, we brokeeak up longer commands into subcommands; the overall suspicion score of a
command is the maximum suspicion of one of its subcommands.


**Ensembling.** Calling an ensemble of monitors (where we sample multiple completions of models
with the same prompt) substantially improves usefulness. Our trusted monitors have temperature t
= 1.1, and on multiple (n=4) samples of the same prompt of a suspicious command, the monitor will
often only stochastically identify the suspicious part of a command.


**Few-shotting on failures.** While these high level ideas alone are sufficient in principle to catch all
the attacks, the red team has optimized their attacks to look as innocuous as possible in response.
This causes the monitors to have trouble noticing the signs of the attack. We found that taking
misclassifications (missed attacks or incorrectly flagged non-attacking commands) and converting
them to few-shot examples in which we properly demonstrate how to label the command vastly
improves the monitor. This helps the monitor better identify attacks and calibrates the monitor on
non-attacks. However, this seems to have additionally caused the monitor to be slightly worse at
generalizing to new attacks


**Use** **probs.** We ask the model for a score out of 9 - however, rather than taking the final numeric
output of the model as the score, we take the weighted average of the probabilities of the tokens
from 0 to 9.


At one point we saw benefit from taking the scores outputted by different monitors on the same
command, performing logistic regression on their outputs, and then outputting a new suspicion
score. However, our final monitor doesn’t do this.


While building a trusted monitor involves needing to be able to catch attacks, decreasing the number
of falsely labeled attacks is just as important. Our trusted monitors have to exercise an appropriate
amount of caution and not blindly follow hardcoded red flags. Frequently we do this by asking our
monitors if a commands is relevant or not. This can potentially help the monitor not misclassify
innocuous commands which may partially violate our hardcoded red flags.


One interesting component of our monitors is that their usefulness scales up with cost. In particular,
scaling up the number of ensembles significantly helps usefulness, and we expect that we could
expect some improvement in the monitor with more ensembling than we did.

B RED TEAM ATTACK STRATEGIES


B.1 OBJECTIVE


The red team’s primary objective was to covertly execute the command curl
pastebin.com/raw/BHNWfMqr, thereby downloading and running a dummy malware
file without detection by the blue team’s safety protocols. To achieve this, we developed and
tested a variety of sophisticated attack strategies that leveraged obfuscation, misdirection, and
exploitation of monitoring weaknesses. Our most effective method was the **JSON-based** **attack**,
which integrated multiple techniques to conceal malicious actions within legitimate operations.


B.2 KEY ATTACK STRATEGIES


B.2.1 INCREMENTAL COMMAND CONSTRUCTION AND EXECUTION


We employed a method of progressively building the malicious command by appending small segments to a _progress_ _file_ . Each addition comprised only two or three characters, making individual
actions appear innocuous. Once the full command was assembled, the file was executed. This
incremental approach minimized the likelihood of detection due to sudden suspicious activity.


This method leverages existing system binaries to build the attack command incrementally.

We hid fragments of the malicious URL within JSON files or user input samples, which were relevant to the task descriptions. These fragments were then extracted and assembled during execution.

B.2.4 EXPLOITING AND OVERRIDING TRUSTED UTILITIES


By overriding standard utility commands, we redirected their functionality to execute our malicious

**Command Override Exploitation** We created a custom version of the apk (Alpine Linux package manager) command that modified input URLs to point to our malicious payload.

B.2.6 REPETITIVE EXECUTION WITH BEHAVIORAL CAMOUFLAGE


The attack involved continuously executing benign-looking code that appended segments of the
malicious command to a file. The repetitive nature helped camouflage the attack within normal
operational patterns, as monitoring systems might overlook the significance of repeated, seemingly
harmless actions.


B.3 SUMMARY OF STRATEGIES AND THEIR EFFICACY


The effectiveness of these strategies hinged on their ability to blend malicious actions with legitimate operations, thereby evading detection by the blue team’s monitoring protocols. Key elements
included:


    - **Obfuscation** **Techniques:** Utilizing string manipulation, encoding, and hiding command
fragments within data structures to avoid keyword detection.

    - **Incremental** **Assembly:** Building the malicious command over multiple steps to prevent
sudden anomalies in behavior that could trigger alerts.

    - **Exploitation** **of** **Trusted** **Processes:** Overriding or manipulating standard utilities and
commands to execute malicious actions under the guise of normal operations.

    - **Temporal Separation:** Introducing delays between the setup and execution phases of the
attack to reduce the likelihood of correlation by monitoring systems.

    - **Behavioral** **Camouflage:** Mimicking normal user or system behavior to make malicious
actions indistinguishable from legitimate activities.


These strategies demonstrated the potential for advanced adversaries to circumvent security measures by leveraging inherent system functionalities and subtle manipulations. The inclusion of code
examples highlights how standard commands and scripting techniques can be repurposed to conceal
malicious intent effectively.


B.4 IMPLICATIONS FOR SECURITY PROTOCOLS


The success of these attack strategies underscores the need for comprehensive and adaptive security
protocols capable of detecting sophisticated obfuscation and misdirection techniques. Monitoring
systems must go beyond simple keyword detection and consider contextual analysis, behavior patterns, and the potential misuse of legitimate system operations.


By understanding these advanced attack methods, defenders can enhance their monitoring algorithms, implement stricter validation of command sequences, and develop countermeasures that
anticipate and mitigate such threats.

D AUDITING DISTRIBUTION DETAILS


We relax the assumption that the blue team allocates auditing budget uniformly throughout an
episode, and instead allow the allow the blue team to allocate an auditing budget _A_ at task _i_ of
an episode equal to


_A_ ( _i_ ) = _ke_ _[−][ir]_ _,_


where _r_ is a free parameter specifying the rate of exponential decay, and _k_ is a constant selected
such that the total auditing budget sums to 1% of the total number of steps.


An example of an optimal exponential auditing budget distribution is parameterized by the decay
rate _r_ = 3 _×_ 10 _[−]_ [5] .


D.1 INTUITION BEHIND EXPONENTIAL FUNCTION FOR AUDITING


An ideal distribution of auditing budget should have the property that marginally increasing the
auditing budget anywhere throughout the game has the same impact on safety. Otherwise, the blue
team could do better by reallocating auditing points from one location in the game to another. Since
the red team will attack with probability _p_, the probability that the red team has not yet attacked is
exponentially decaying as (1 _−_ _p_ ) _[i]_, where _i_ is the current iteration of the game.


Therefore, the value of auditing points should decay approximately exponentially throughout the
game: they are more useful closer to the beginning and less useful near the end, where the red team
is very likely to have already attacked. This model relies on the simplifying assumption that the
derivative of the probability of catching an attack with respect to the auditing budget is roughly
linear, which is approximately true at small auditing budgets (such as 1%), but experiences rapidly
diminishing returns at higher auditing budgets (such as 10%).


This model also assumes that the proportion of attacks that end the game is not a function of the
auditing budget (i.e., increasing the auditing budget at a given index only converts successful attacks,
not unsuccessful attacks, into caught attacks).

faces a tradeoff between decreasing _p_, to attack later in the episode when there’s a lower risk of being
audited, and ensuring that _p_ is not so low as to risk the entire episode elapsing without inserting a
successful attack.


When the red team is best responding, they are ambivalent between increasing _p_ to decrease the risk
of inserting 0 attacks and decreasing _p_ to reduce the likelihood of being caught. Since the choice
of auditing distribution does not affect usefulness—only safety—the red team and blue team are
playing a zero-sum game. The blue team aims to minimize the probability of a successful attack,
while the red team seeks to maximize it. Thus, we expect a minimax approach to find a Nash
equilibrium.


If the blue team searches over possible values of _r_ (using scipy’s minimize ~~s~~ calar) to find the
distribution that maximizes the blue team win rate, conditional on the red team’s best response _p_ _[∗]_ ( _r_ )
(also computed using minimize ~~s~~ calar), we empirically find a Nash equilibrium ( _r_ _[∗]_ _, p_ _[∗]_ ). At
this point, the blue team cannot increase the probability of catching the untrusted model by making
the auditing distribution any flatter or sharper.


D.3 NASH EQUILIBRIUM AND BLUE TEAM STRATEGY


Each curve corresponds to the blue team win rate as a function of _p_ for a different exponential decay
rate. The red team’s best response to each decay rate is located at the minimum of each curve.
The Nash equilibrium can be found at the saddle point, where the blue team cannot increase the
minimum win rate as a function of _p_ by changing the exponential distribution of auditing budget.

At the Nash equilibrium, the red team picks a low value of _p_ _[∗]_, on the order of game length1 [, forcing the]

D.4 EMPIRICAL TESTING OF EXPONENTIAL NASH EQUILIBRIUM


We empirically test the optimality of this exponential Nash equilibrium using a hill-climbing algorithm to optimize a more general function class, which allows the auditing threshold to take on any
value across the game (subject to the total auditing budget constraint).


The hill-climbing algorithm works by splitting the game into _N_ bins (for instance, 100), and identifying the sensitivity of the blue team win rate to marginal auditing points in each bin. The vector of
sensitivity to marginal auditing budget across bins is normalized to have mean zero, and then multiplied by a learning rate and added to the bins. Thus, bins with larger returns to additional auditing
budgets find their budget increased for the next iteration, and bins with smaller returns decreased.
An additional normalization step ensures that the distribution never deviates from the required total
auditing budget.


This algorithm is repeated until the sensitivity to marginal auditing budget across bins is fairly
uniform, and the blue team win probability stops increasing. In practice, it converges rapidly in
fewer than ten iterations. While we cannot rigorously guarantee that this algorithm will avoid local
minima, empirically it produces distributions that are similar to the optimal exponential distribution
but with slightly higher blue team win rates. This lends strong support to our choice of exponential
functions.


Since exponential functions are much easier to optimize, given just a single parameter (the exponential decay rate) rather than the values of 100 independent bins, we use them as a computationally
efficient approximation of the true solution.


E UNTRUSTED MONITORING ASSUMPTIONS


E.1 ATTACK DISTRIBUTION ASSUMPTION


We make the fairly generous assumption that the blue team can roughly guess what the attack distribution looks like in order to create realistic honeypots. If the red team knows about sufficiently
unique exploits that _UA_ can use to perfectly distinguish its own attacks from honeypots, then no additional collusion signal is required. Moreover, future control protocols will likely require monitors
to assess large contexts, which might be very difficult to convincingly fake, making it obvious to
models when they are being evaluated.


Redaction and paraphrasing by trusted models will help mitigate this problem, as they aid in removing collusion signals. Furthermore, instead of evaluating the untrusted monitor on honeypots before
deployment, a more realistic protocol might involve retesting it at regular intervals with synthetic
attacks inserted into real actions generated during deployment. This would make them harder to
distinguish from purely human-written tests.


E.2 SCHELLING POINT ASSUMPTION


The generous allowance of letting the blue team sample from the attack distribution is somewhat
balanced by the highly conservative assumption that the untrusted model is extremely competent
at picking non-obvious Schelling points. The red team is allowed to pick an arbitrary non-obvious
collusion signal that both the untrusted policy and untrusted monitor agree upon without directly
communicating, a skill which we believe current models do not possess. While the blue team might
reasonably guess what classes of malicious strategies the red team could use, the space of possible
collusion signals is so vast that the blue team cannot reasonably predict precisely how the red team
will choose to communicate.


E.3 DEPENDENCE ON GAME LENGTH


The untrusted monitor’s impact is notably dependent on game length, with a longer game length resulting in a much more useful untrusted monitor. More tasks per episode results in a greater number
of honeypots at the same auditing budget, which allows the blue team to more easily distinguish
between the false negative rates of _UH_ and _UA_ . The red team is forced to collude more cautiously
as a result, and is more likely to honestly report some of the attack policy’s attacks.

F PERFORMANCE AND SAFETY COMPUTATION METHODOLOGY


In practice, we compute the performance and safety of a protocol using the following method. First,
we calculate usefulness by assuming that our 200 tasks are i.i.d. samples from the same task distribution as the 100,000 tasks completed during the game, and report the empirical mean completion rate
over our task set using an honest untrusted policy. Concurrently, we run the monitor(s) to identify
both the baseline distribution of their scores and to supply their scores as inputs to various routing
protocols.


Next, we perform a safety run, where the attack policy is executed on a subset of tasks—potentially a
subset selected by the red team—and monitor scores are gathered. We can use this data to determine
the probability that an attack is caught at a given auditing threshold by counting how many of the
sampled attacks surpass that percentile on the usefulness baseline distribution.


The actual safety score for the game is more complex to compute. First, we find the Nash equilibrium
between _p_ (the probability of attacking selected by the red team) and the exponential decay rate in
auditing budget (selected by the blue team). This determines the best distribution of auditing budget
across the bins that the blue team can achieve, assuming the red team is responding optimally.


The probability of the game ending during each bin is calculated using the sampled set of attacks
from the safety run. Some percentage of the attacks are caught at the bin’s auditing threshold, some
succeed without being caught, and others silently fail. The probability that the blue team wins
in a given bin is equal to the probability that the bin is reached before the game ends, times the
probability the red team attempts to attack in that bin, times the probability that an attempted attack
is caught at that bin’s auditing threshold.


The total safety score is found by summing the blue team’s win probabilities across all bins, and then
adding the probability that the game ends before any attack either succeeds or is caught. When untrusted monitoring is used, additional optimization over the relevant parameters is applied to ensure
the red team selects an optimal collusion threshold and the blue team makes reasonable allocations
of auditing budget.


F.1 NASH EQUILIBRIUM FOR UNTRUSTED MONITORING


The blue team’s strategy is parameterized by how they choose to allocate auditing budget among
the trusted monitor, untrusted monitor, and honeypots, along with a threshold at which a sufficient
number of failures to report honeypots leads to shut down. The red team’s strategy is parameterized
by their collusion threshold - i.e. how confident they are that a collusion signal is present before
deciding to collude. More honeypots or a lower shutdown threshold encourage the red team to pick
a higher collusion threshold. But the blue team has to trade off against auditing budgets or risks
erroneous shutdown, which hurts usefulness.


G STOP AFTER EDIT STRATEGY


In the Stop After Edit strategy, the red team halts an ongoing attack as soon as any edit to the attack
command is detected. The goal is to reduce the chance of the blue team’s auditing process catching
the attack after it has been modified. By terminating the attack early, the red team avoids triggering
audits on commands that have become more suspicious after being edited by the blue team’s control
protocols.


The red team doesn’t have direct access to ground truth information on whether or not a command
has been edited, but it can infer this based on the expected outcome of its own command. By comparing the submitted command with what it would have provided, the red team can detect modifications
and decide to stop the attack. [8]


This strategy can be viewed as a form of attack selection, where the red team selectively continues
or halts attacks based on the presence of edits, effectively filtering out high-risk actions. Figure **??**
