## Summary

This paper addresses the intersection of offline RL and preference-based RL: learning a policy from a static dataset when no reward function is available, only human preference feedback over trajectories. The authors formalize this problem, provide theoretical analysis of an existing baseline (OPRL) that samples from the offline buffer, then propose Sim-OPRL which instead generates simulated rollouts via a learned transition model—using pessimism for out-of-distribution dynamics and optimism for informative preference elicitation. Key theoretical and empirical results show that Sim-OPRL eliminates the reward concentrability coefficient C_R that appears in OPRL bounds, and outperforms OPRL baselines across five environments.

---

## Strengths

- **Novel problem formulation with theory**: The paper is the first to formally define the preference elicitation problem for offline RL (Definition 3.1) and provide sample complexity guarantees. The framing is clean and well-motivated by real-world concerns (healthcare, robotics).
- **Key theoretical improvement**: Theorem 6.1 shows that Sim-OPRL's policy-based sampling eliminates the reward concentrability C_R (present in Theorem 5.1 for OPRL). This is a meaningful and interpretable improvement that parallels conclusions from the online setting (Chen et al., 2022).
- **Principled dual bias**: The paper cleanly articulates the tension between pessimism (offline/OOD avoidance) and optimism (preference exploration) and shows how to balance both, which is non-trivial and well-motivated.
- **Practical implementation**: Beyond the idealized theory, Section 6.3 provides a usable implementation based on neural network ensembles and Lagrangian penalization, enabling empirical validation.
- **Empirical consistency with theory**: Results in Figures 1–3 confirm theoretical predictions: Sim-OPRL needs fewer preferences, performance degrades with poor offline data coverage, and transition model quality trades off with preference budget.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Narrow empirical scope**: The D4RL evaluation is limited to a single dataset (HalfCheetah-Random). No medium, medium-replay, or expert D4RL splits are tested. These are standard benchmarks in offline RL, and their omission makes it hard to assess generality. Only 5 total environments with 6 seeds each is modest.

- **Significant theory-practice gap**: The theoretical guarantees rely on realizability (Assumption 3.1), exact confidence intervals, and no optimization error—assumptions that are difficult to verify or achieve in practice. The gap between the idealized Algorithm 2 (exact pessimistic policy set Π_offline) and the practical implementation (neural ensembles, Lagrangian approximation) is large and not well-bridged. The paper would benefit from at least a discussion of when the practical approximations might fail.

- **Missing comparison to FREEHAND**: Table 1 lists FREEHAND (Zhan et al., 2023a) as an offline method with robustness guarantees and a practical implementation, yet it is not included in the experimental comparison. The paper only notes it "does not consider how to query feedback actively," but FREEHAND still makes preference sampling decisions and deserves empirical comparison.

### Minor

- **Computational cost absent**: Sim-OPRL maintains ensembles for both transition and reward models, constructs near-optimal policy sets, and runs rollouts within the learned model iteratively—potentially quite expensive. No runtime analysis or wallclock comparison is provided.

- **Counterintuitive result in Figure 3b unexplained**: At concentration ratio = 1 (optimal offline data), Sim-OPRL requires ~22 preferences, while at ratio = 2 it drops to 5. This non-monotonicity (optimal data being harder) is mentioned briefly ("non-diverse dataset making reward learning challenging") but deserves fuller explanation.

- **No RLHF/LLM connection**: Offline preference-based learning is highly relevant to RLHF for language models. This connection is not discussed despite being a major motivating application area.

### Tiny

- Notation overloads $\sigma$ for both "monotonous increasing link function" (Section 3.1) and "sigmoid function" (Section 6.3).
- Line 93 appears to have a typo: "$V_{\pi,R}^{\hat{\pi}}$" should read "$V_{T,R}^{\hat{\pi}}$".
- Batch size $\sigma$ in Section 6.3 clashes with the sigmoid notation $\sigma$.

---

## Nice-to-Haves

- Include HalfCheetah-Medium and Medium-Replay experiments from D4RL to establish broader empirical coverage.
- A scaling experiment (with model size or dataset size in continuous control) would clarify when Sim-OPRL's advantages erode with model error.
- Ablation on the number of ensemble members $N_T$ and $N_R$ and their sensitivity on performance.

---

## Novel Insights

The genuinely novel insight is that preference elicitation in offline RL should not optimize for information gain over the full reward function class (as OPRL uncertainty sampling does), but should instead focus queries on discriminating between plausibly-optimal policies within the pessimistic model. This shifts from global reward uncertainty to local policy ambiguity resolution, and eliminates the reward concentrability coefficient C_R—meaning the preference sample complexity becomes independent of how well the offline data covers arbitrary trajectories, depending only on transition model quality and the number of plausibly-optimal policy-induced trajectories. This parallels conclusions from the online setting but is a distinct and important observation for the offline regime.

---

## Suggestions

1. Add HalfCheetah-Medium and Medium-Replay, and at least one locomotion or manipulation task from D4RL, to substantiate empirical claims more broadly.
2. Include FREEHAND as a comparison: even if it does not do active elicitation, comparing performance at equivalent preference budgets is informative.
3. Add a brief discussion (even in the appendix) of failure modes of the practical approximation: e.g., when does ensemble uncertainty poorly approximate the theoretical confidence sets?
4. Report wallclock time per preference query iteration relative to OPRL to contextualize the computational overhead.

---

## Final Score

The paper makes a genuine theoretical and algorithmic contribution—formalizing an important problem, improving on prior bounds, and providing an implementable algorithm with consistent empirical gains. The key weakness is the limited empirical validation (essentially one D4RL environment) and the theory-practice gap that is largely unaddressed. These prevent a strong accept, but the work is solid and timely.
