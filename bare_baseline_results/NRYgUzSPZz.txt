## Summary

This paper argues that discrete diffusion language models are fundamentally better suited than autoregressive (AR) models for complex reasoning and planning tasks. The authors introduce the concept of "subgoal imbalance"—the observation that AR models struggle disproportionately with certain hard subgoals requiring long-range planning—and demonstrate both theoretically and empirically that discrete diffusion models overcome this limitation via a multi-view learning dynamic. They further propose Multi-Granularity Diffusion Modeling (MGDM), which adds token-level difficulty reweighting on top of discrete diffusion, achieving 91.5% on Countdown-4, 100% on Sudoku, and state-of-the-art results on SAT, substantially outperforming size-comparable AR models.

## Strengths

- **Compelling empirical results**: MGDM's 6M-parameter model beats LLaMA 13B on Countdown and Sudoku, highlighting a striking paradigm advantage over mere scaling of AR models. The Game of 24 results (76% MGDM vs. 74% GPT-4+ToT at 186× fewer tokens) are particularly striking.
- **Clear theoretical framing**: The reformulation of the diffusion ELBO as a sum of token-level cross-entropy losses (Eq. 6) and the multi-view learning interpretation elegantly explain why diffusion handles hard subgoals that AR cannot.
- **Diagnostic planning experiment**: The synthetic graph-planning task with controllable planning distance is a well-designed vehicle for isolating the subgoal imbalance phenomenon; showing AR requires exponentially more data as PD increases is a clean, reproducible result.
- **Insightful error analysis**: The "Regretful Compromise" analysis in Countdown is a genuinely illuminating qualitative explanation of AR failure modes—the model is forced to contort arithmetic at the final step to compensate for poor early planning.
- **Ablation rigor**: Table 3 cleanly isolates contributions of sequence-reweighting, token-reweighting, and decoding strategy.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Task scope is too narrow for the paper's claims.** Countdown, Sudoku, and 3-SAT are constraint-satisfaction problems where the solution inherently depends on all positions simultaneously. Bidirectional models (any masked or diffusion approach) are structurally advantaged over left-to-right AR on such tasks—the experiment is partially tautological. The bold title "Beyond Autoregression" implies broad language understanding advantages that are not shown; no natural language or translation experiments are included, and text generation quality is entirely ignored.
- **Unfair training data advantage in Sudoku.** The model trains on 100k solved Sudoku grids from a 1M-game dataset. It is unclear whether the model genuinely solves Sudoku logic or pattern-matches from the dense training set (Sudoku grids have significant repetitive structure). No analysis of generalization to puzzle types or difficulty levels not seen at training is provided.
- **MGDM's marginal contribution over baseline diffusion.** The headline results in Table 1 are largely attributable to diffusion generally (RDM already achieves 87.0% on CD-4); MGDM's token-reweighting adds ~4.5 pp. This incremental gain does not strongly justify the named method or the central billing of MGDM in the abstract.
- **Comparison against stronger AR baselines is missing.** No comparison is made against AR models that use constrained decoding, constraint propagation, or even simple beam search on these tasks. Search-augmented AR is only compared for Game of 24 but not for Sudoku or SAT.

### Minor

- Figure 2's alt-text description appears to contradict the paper's claims ("AR and Reverse AR reach 100% accuracy quickly, while Teacherless and Diffusion remain low"), causing confusion. The paper's narrative says diffusion achieves perfect accuracy at all PDs, but the embedded figure description says the opposite.
- Hyperparameter α and β in the token-reweighting term are selected via grid search with no principled guidance; the optimal setting differs between Table 3 rows (linear vs. original sequence-reweighting), suggesting sensitivity.
- Decoding speed analysis (Figure 6a) uses batch size 1, which is an unusual and favorable setting for diffusion. A fair throughput comparison at typical batch sizes is not provided.
- Proposition 1 is stated very generally ("some subgoals may require substantially more data to learn or may even be infeasible to learn") and is nearly a truism; the formal backing for the synthetic task's specific exponential data scaling is not given.

### Tiny

- The paper mentions "10 nodes (0-9)" in Figure 1 but then lists node 10 as well, producing 11 nodes.
- Minor grammatical inconsistencies throughout ("We observed that the slope of countdown 4 is smaller compared to countdown 5 in the trade-off" is ambiguous).

## Nice-to-Haves

- Experiments on at least one natural language reasoning task (e.g., math word problems, logical entailment) would greatly strengthen the generality argument.
- An analysis of whether MGDM advantages persist when AR is given chain-of-thought supervision or verifier-guided search (both of which are practical and commonly deployed) would sharpen the contribution.
- Theoretical lower bounds on AR sample complexity for high-PD planning would formalize the exponential scaling observation beyond empirics.

## Novel Insights

The multi-view learning interpretation of diffusion training is genuinely illuminating: treating each noise level $t$ as a distinct "view" of the ground truth means that a hard subgoal (difficult under one view) is learned via complementary, easier objectives at other noise levels, circumventing the fixed left-to-right conditioning that makes certain subgoals infeasible for AR. This reframing connects discrete diffusion to curriculum learning and multi-view representation theory in a way that is underexplored in the literature and provides a principled explanation for the empirical superiority of diffusion on constraint-dense tasks beyond prior "bidirectional context helps" intuitions.

## Suggestions

1. Add at least one natural language reasoning benchmark (e.g., GSM8K, FOLIO, or a logical reasoning dataset) to test whether the claims hold outside toy constraint-satisfaction domains.
2. Provide an analysis of Sudoku generalization to puzzles with unseen difficulty levels or to a held-out test set drawn from a different generator to rule out pattern-matching.
3. Report inference throughput at batch sizes ≥ 32 to give a fair picture of the accuracy-speed tradeoff in deployment settings.
4. Formalize Proposition 1 with an explicit complexity statement for the synthetic planning task (e.g., prove or cite that AR requires Ω(exp(PD)) samples).
5. Compare against AR with lookahead/backtracking on SAT to demonstrate MGDM advantages beyond the no-search AR regime.
