## Summary
SIGnAL (Self-Informed Generative Active Learning) proposes an RL-based framework for generative active learning where an LLM (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) is used to synthesize candidate data points, a novel acquisition function selects informative and in-distribution instances (KL divergence over embedding distance), and PPO is used to fine-tune the LLM so it generates increasingly informative instances over successive AL rounds. Experiments on three text classification benchmarks (SST-2, AGNEWS, QNLI) at 0.1% and 1% data regimes show improvement over pool-based baselines.

---

## Strengths
- **Interesting problem framing**: Combining generative models with RL to optimize data synthesis for active learning addresses a real and underexplored challenge — the difficulty of translating "informativeness" into a differentiable loss.
- **Acquisition function intuition is sound**: The ratio of KL divergence to embedding distance as a joint informativeness-relevance score is a clean and interpretable design that guards against OOD generation.
- **Experiments cover multiple datasets and scales**: SST-2, AGNEWS, QNLI at two dataset scales provide some breadth; the 0.1% regime is a genuinely challenging and practically relevant setting.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*(None that definitively kill the contribution, but the Major issues collectively undermine its validity.)*

### Major
- **Fundamentally unfair comparison**: SIGnAL uses a 7B-parameter instruction-tuned LLM (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) as a data generator and fine-tunes it with PPO. The baselines (BADGE, CAL, BERTKM, Entropy, Random) are purely pool-based methods with no access to this generative capacity. The improvement over baselines is almost entirely attributable to having unlimited LLM-generated data, not to any novel AL strategy. A critical missing baseline is: *random synthetic data generation + the same classifier training*, without any acquisition function or RL. Without this, the paper cannot attribute gains to SIGnAL's methodology.

- **No ablations whatsoever**: There is no ablation study. It is impossible to disentangle the contributions of (a) simply using LLM-generated data, (b) the proposed acquisition function, and (c) the RL optimization of the generator. This is a severe gap for a methods paper.

- **Circular/impure oracle annotation**: Labels for synthetic data are assigned by classifiers fine-tuned on the same datasets (achieving 91–93% accuracy), not a true oracle. This introduces systematic label noise, undermines the data quality argument, and makes reproducibility/practicality questionable. The paper notes this could "result in performance degradation in practice" but offers no analysis of its impact on reported numbers.

- **Computational cost is not discussed**: RL fine-tuning of a 7B model with PPO in each active learning round is orders of magnitude more expensive than pool-based methods. No wall-clock times, GPU hours, or efficiency analysis are provided. The framework may not be practically viable relative to simply doing large-scale data augmentation once.

- **The RL component's incremental benefit is undemonstrated**: SIGnAL acknowledges that in early rounds the generator just copies in-context examples and the method underperforms baselines. There is no evidence showing that RL specifically is responsible for later improvements vs. simply accumulating more LLM-generated data over rounds.

### Minor
- The acquisition function is directly built on CAL's informativeness notion — the novelty is adding the distance denominator, which is incremental rather than conceptually new.
- Inconsistent capitalization of the method name (SIGNaL vs. SIGnAL) throughout.
- Only 3 tasks, all text classification; the claim of being a "general framework" is not supported by any cross-modal or more diverse task evaluation.
- The paper body is ~9 pages and quite short; the experimental analysis section is thin with limited discussion.

### Tiny
- Missing reference in Section 5.4: "We present the results of our experiments in 3" (should be "Figure 3").
- The introduction's motivation about "obtaining original unlabeled data posing challenges" is weakly argued for text classification tasks.

---

## Nice-to-Haves
- An ablation comparing: (1) random LLM generation + BERT-base training, (2) proposed acquisition function only (no RL), (3) full SIGnAL — this is essential to validate the framework.
- Comparison with cost-equivalent baselines (e.g., LLM-annotated pool-based AL, or simple LLM data augmentation at the same computational budget).
- True human annotation experiments or a principled simulation of oracle noise.
- Extension to a non-classification task (e.g., NER or QA) to support generality claims.

---

## Novel Insights
The notion of using a delayed, non-differentiable reward (instance informativeness) to guide generation via RL is a reasonable conceptual bridge between active learning and LLM alignment. However, the paper does not demonstrate empirically that the RL loop is what drives improvements, and the proposed acquisition function is a straightforward extension of CAL. The most interesting observation — that the generator learns to balance label distributions in QNLI over time — is noted qualitatively but never rigorously analyzed. There are no genuinely novel empirical or theoretical insights beyond the paper's stated contributions, and even those contributions are not individually validated.

---

## Suggestions
1. Add a "random LLM generation" baseline and an "acquisition-only, no RL" ablation to isolate each component's contribution.
2. Report wall-clock time / GPU cost comparisons with pool-based baselines to contextualize practical viability.
3. Replace or supplement the pseudo-oracle annotation with true label propagation analysis, or at minimum quantify how label noise level (91–93% accuracy) affects reported results through sensitivity analysis.
4. Evaluate at least one non-classification task or one additional modality to substantiate the "general framework" claim.
5. Provide significance testing (t-tests or bootstrap CIs) rather than just mean ± std, given the small number of trials.
