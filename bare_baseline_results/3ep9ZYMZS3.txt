## Summary
HyPER (Hybrid PDE Predictor with Reinforcement Learning) proposes a framework that learns an RL-based policy to decide when to invoke a costly PDE simulator as a "knowledge-guided correction" during autoregressive neural surrogate rollouts. Using REINFORCE with a binary action space (call surrogate vs. call simulator), HyPER minimizes cumulative rollout MSE while constraining simulator usage to a user-specified fraction λ of timesteps. The key claimed advantages are: no differentiability requirement on the simulator, surrogate/simulator-agnosticism, and adaptability to changing physical conditions and noise. Experiments on 2D Navier-Stokes and subsurface flow (Richards equation) show large reductions in rollout error over surrogate-only baselines.

---

## Strengths
- **Practical motivation and clear design**: The approach of using an RL policy to selectively invoke a non-differentiable simulator is well-motivated—many legacy simulators cannot produce gradients, and this constraint-free design is a genuine engineering advantage over existing hybrid methods.
- **Thorough experimental coverage**: The paper tests in-distribution rollout (RQ1), changing boundary conditions (RQ2), noisy input (RQ3), and ablates vs. a random policy (RQ4), and examines cost-accuracy trade-offs (RQ5) across two distinct PDE systems. The 93–97% cumulative MSE reduction on the subsurface flow (100 timesteps) is impressive.
- **Cost-awareness via λ**: The user-controllable fraction of simulator calls is a practically useful design choice, and Figure 5 illustrates the error-per-second trade-off clearly.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **Weak RL advantage in the noise-free setting** — Table 4 shows only a 57% win rate over a random policy with the same simulator budget in the noise-free case, and the cumulative MSE gap is modest (0.186 random vs. 0.164 HyPER). This raises the question of whether the RL policy is doing much beyond uniformly distributing simulator calls. The lion's share of improvement over SUG baselines seems to come simply from calling the simulator 30% of the time, not from the intelligent scheduling. This undermines a central claim of the paper.

2. **Missing comparison to rule-based hybrid baselines** — The paper cites Zhang et al. (2022), which also selects between a surrogate and simulator in a hybrid fashion (albeit with a fixed pre-defined schedule). Comparing HyPER against this directly would quantify the value of the learned policy over a simple rule-based scheduler (e.g., "call the simulator every k steps" or "call if the estimated error exceeds a threshold"). Without this baseline, it is unclear how much of HyPER's gains require RL vs. a simple heuristic.

3. **Fairness of the RQ1 comparison** — The SUG baselines are trained purely on data; HyPER additionally calls the (ground truth) simulator during inference 30% of the time. This is a fundamentally different inference budget. A fair comparison would require accounting for this—e.g., presenting results at matched compute budgets or explicitly benchmarking against a "call the simulator every 3 steps deterministically" baseline.

4. **Reward/baseline function design is under-motivated** — The baseline function b (mean squared error of randomly calling the simulator the same number of times) is unusual and its design choices are not ablated. It is not clear how sensitive HyPER's performance is to this choice or whether standard REINFORCE variance reduction techniques would suffice.

### Minor
- The RL policy architecture (π_θ) is not described in the main text; the appendix is absent in the submitted version, making reproducibility harder to assess.
- All experiments use relatively small grids (64×64 and 50×50). The scalability of the RL policy, which takes the full PDE state as input, to larger resolutions is not addressed.
- The improvement in the unimodal noise experiments over the strongest trained baseline (UNet trained on noisy data) is only 19.81–49.90%—much less dramatic than the headline numbers against un-adapted models.
- REINFORCE is known to be high-variance and sample-inefficient compared to actor-critic methods; the authors acknowledge this but do not quantify the data efficiency shortfall.

### Tiny
- Many figure captions are repeated verbatim two to three times in the text (artifact of the PDF-to-text conversion).
- The paper says "first of its kind" in multiple places, which overstates the novelty—rule-based hybrid simulators/surrogate switching has been studied (Zhang et al., 2022); the novelty is specifically the RL-learned policy without differentiability.

---

## Nice-to-Haves
- An ablation comparing the learned RL policy against a threshold-based heuristic ("call simulator if estimated error exceeds τ") would greatly strengthen the claim that RL is needed.
- Experiments on a larger-scale PDE problem (e.g., 256×256 or turbulent 3D flow) would significantly boost the impact of the contribution.
- Sensitivity analysis on λ across a wider range would help practitioners understand the error/cost frontier.

---

## Novel Insights
The framing of "when to invoke a non-differentiable simulator during rollout" as a reinforcement learning problem with a cost-constrained reward is a clean and practically relevant reformulation. The observation that the RL policy provides substantially more value (≥70–80% win rates) when there are sharp, temporally localized error spikes (noisy/changing-boundary conditions) but only marginal value (57%) in smooth in-distribution rollouts is an interesting empirical finding: it suggests that the RL policy's learned advantage lies specifically in responding to sudden, local perturbations rather than in global scheduling.

---

## Suggestions
1. Include a deterministic/heuristic hybrid baseline (e.g., uniform periodic simulator calls, or a threshold-based rule) to isolate the RL policy's contribution.
2. Provide wall-clock-time-matched comparisons to fairly evaluate HyPER against SUG methods that are not allowed simulator access.
3. Test on a larger-resolution benchmark (e.g., PDEBench or ERA5 weather) to demonstrate scalability.
4. Ablate the baseline function b in the reward to clarify its contribution to stability.
5. Describe π_θ's architecture and the sensitivity to RL hyperparameters in the main paper (not just the appendix).

---

## Final Score

The paper addresses a real problem with a clean formulation, good experimental coverage across multiple settings, and practically useful results. However, the core RL contribution appears modest in clean settings (57% win rate over random), a key related hybrid baseline is absent, and the inference-time compute advantage of HyPER over pure SUG approaches is not adequately controlled for. These are significant gaps that limit confidence in the claimed contributions.
