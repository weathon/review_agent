## Summary
This paper investigates the root causes of inconsistency in perturbation-based fidelity metrics for saliency maps (e.g., AOPC, Average Drop, faithfulness). The authors formalize two assumptions underlying these metrics — that perturbing any pixel reduces output probability (P1), and that the magnitude of the drop is proportional to pixel importance (P2) — and propose two conformity measures, DROP and PSim, to empirically quantify how often these assumptions are violated. Experiments across five models, three datasets, and nine perturbation types consistently show both assumptions fail, confirming that perturbation-based fidelity metrics are unreliable.

---

## Strengths
- **Relevant and clearly scoped problem**: Fidelity of saliency maps is a critical open issue in XAI; formalizing *why* existing metrics fail (not just *that* they fail) is a worthy goal, and the problem statement is clearly articulated.
- **Comprehensive empirical coverage**: The study uses 5 models (including 2 adversarially trained ResNet50s), 3 datasets (Imagenette, Oxford Pets, PASCAL VOC 2007), 9 perturbation types, and 2 perturbation schemes, totaling ~75 million model calls — a notable empirical investment that gives the results substantial breadth.
- **Actionable finding on Gaussian Blur**: The observation that Gaussian Blur perturbations produce more consistent DROP and PSim scores than other perturbations is a concrete, practically useful result for practitioners choosing perturbation types.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Incremental over Tomsett et al. (2020)**: The paper explicitly casts itself as "complementing" Tomsett et al., who already demonstrated and published that these fidelity metrics are inconsistent across perturbations. The novel contribution reduces to: (a) a slightly more formal notation for two already-intuitive failure modes, and (b) a larger-scale empirical sweep. This is a meaningful contribution in scope but not in depth — ICLR expects more fundamental advances.

- **Theoretical framework is definitional, not explanatory**: Section 2.1 formalizes assumptions P1 and P2, but does not explain *mechanistically* why modern DNNs violate them. The DROP score of ~0.5 (near chance) for most perturbations is striking, but the paper offers no mechanistic account (e.g., gradient saturation, adversarial-like regions in pixel space, smoothness of decision boundaries) for this phenomenon. Without this, the "theoretical framework" amounts to restating what a working metric would require.

- **No remedy or improved metric proposed**: The paper's conclusion is essentially "use fidelity metrics cautiously and specify your perturbation type." While caution is warranted, a paper diagnosing a problem at this scale ought to propose a path forward — even a partial one (e.g., constrained perturbation selection criteria, an alternative metric formulation, or a bounding argument). The contribution remains purely diagnostic.

- **Equation (9) appears to contain a self-referential typo**: Equation 9 defines PSim as a sum over PSim, rather than a sum over per-image PSim scores — a notational error that suggests incomplete proofreading.

### Minor

- Selecting only 50 random pixels to approximate the full pixel ranking is justified by an appeal to rank-order subsets (Appendix S2), but this is a strong simplification; the paper does not analyze sensitivity to this choice or compare to full-pixel evaluations.
- Figure captions are duplicated verbatim in the body text (once as alt-text for the image, once as a proper caption) — a formatting artifact that signals careless paper preparation.
- Adversarial model analysis (Table 2) reaches no conclusions due to architecture availability limitations; including it without meaningful conclusions adds length without insight.
- The paper does not discuss related work on out-of-distribution issues with perturbation-based evaluation (e.g., Hooker et al.'s ROAR/KAR, or distribution-shift critiques), which is directly relevant and partially explains P1 violations.

### Tiny

- Redundant mention of adversarial model extension as "future work" when it is already included in experiments.
- Formatting artifacts (raw line numbers visible in the manuscript body, e.g., lines 270–323) reduce professional presentation.

---

## Nice-to-Haves
- A mechanistic explanation (even brief) grounded in the geometry of DNN decision boundaries for why single-pixel perturbations so frequently *increase* model confidence (~50% of the time), violating P1.
- A practical protocol recommending a specific perturbation type (e.g., Gaussian Blur) and a minimum conformity threshold before reporting fidelity scores, rather than the general caution note.
- Comparison to ROAR/KAR or other retrain-based evaluation approaches that sidestep the perturbation-sensitivity problem entirely.

---

## Novel Insights
The most genuinely novel empirical insight is that DROP scores cluster around 0.5 for most perturbation types — meaning that roughly half of single-pixel perturbations *increase* the model's output probability, contrary to the foundational assumption of pixel-importance-based metrics. This is more surprising than the general inconsistency finding and deserves deeper analysis. Additionally, the relative consistency of Gaussian Blur across models and datasets is a practically useful observation that points toward perturbation-type selection criteria.

---

## Suggestions
1. Add a mechanistic discussion of why p(p_i^φ > p_0) ≈ 0.5 for most perturbations — connect to smoothness/local linearity of DNNs or OOD distribution shifts.
2. Propose a minimum DROP/PSim threshold (e.g., > 0.85) as a pre-condition for reporting fidelity scores, turning the conformity measures into a usable evaluation protocol.
3. Extend the related work to explicitly contrast with retrain-based evaluation (ROAR/KAR), addressing how those methods escape the problem studied here.
4. Fix the self-referential definition in Equation 9 and eliminate duplicate figure captions.
