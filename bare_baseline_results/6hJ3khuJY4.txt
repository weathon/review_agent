## Summary
The paper proposes the **Proximal Transformation Network (PTN)**, a plug-and-play data-centric module for time series forecasting (TSF). PTN learns a general input/target data transformation via co-optimization of a proximity loss (keeping transformed data close to raw data) and a downstream forecasting loss. The architecture uses a multi-depth CNN encoder and an attention-based decoder, optionally extended with a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) design. When added to linear and Transformer backbone models, PTN achieves competitive results on standard TSF benchmarks.

---

## Strengths

- **Novel data-centric framing.** Reformulating TSF as a two-step process—first learn a data transformation, then forecast on the transformed data—is a fresh angle distinguishing this from purely model-centric advances. The explicit connection to prior heuristic smoothing methods (moving average, patching, instance normalization, sparse downsampling) is well-motivated.
- **Plug-and-play and broadly applicable.** PTN is shown to improve five diverse backbones (LINEAR, DLINEAR, RLINEAR, iTRANSFORMER, PatchTST) and achieves wins on 12/14 averaged metrics in Table 2 compared to strong baselines.
- **Interpretability analysis.** The visualizations in Sections 5.2 (channel distribution alignment via the CNN encoder, self-supervised predictability clustering in the extended loss space) are genuinely insightful and support understanding *why* the module works, not just that it works.
- **Data transfer protocol.** Using a lightweight linear model to learn the transformation and transferring it to a heavier model is an interesting and underexplored idea; Table 4 shows it is competitive with fully end-to-end training.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Proposition 1 is nearly trivial.** The proposition asserts that for any predictor with nonzero test error, some transformation of training data exists that improves performance. This is true almost by definition—one can always construct a contrived transformation—and provides essentially no guidance on *which* transformation to seek or why the proximal constraint is correct. The provided linear-model sketch in Appendix A.1 is an illustrative example, not a proof of the broader claim. As stated, it does not meaningfully justify the specific PTN design.

- **PTN degrades MSE on Traffic for most backbones (Table 3).** For the Traffic dataset, adding PTN *hurts* MSE for LINEAR (0.626→0.675), DLINEAR (0.627→0.643), iTRANSFORMER (0.428→0.463), and PATCHTST (0.481→0.584). These are non-trivial regressions (up to ~21% for PatchTST). The paper does not analyze or explain this systematic failure mode on a major benchmark, which significantly undermines the "consistent improvement" claim made in the abstract and Section 5.1.

- **Misleading SOTA claims in Table 2 for Traffic.** PTN-iTR achieves MSE 0.463 on Traffic while vanilla iTRANSFORMER achieves 0.428. PTN thus makes iTRANSFORMER worse yet is still listed alongside "SOTA" results. The 12/14 win-count statistic obscures several important losses.

- **Insufficient computational cost analysis.** PTN adds a CNN encoder + two attention modules + MoE stack on top of every backbone. No training time, memory, or FLOPs comparison is provided. For baselines like RLINEAR that are intentionally lightweight, the additional complexity from PTN could be prohibitive; the cost–benefit trade-off is not addressed.

### Minor

- **MoE routing by sequential patch order** is unusual and lacks motivation. Why would chronologically ordered patches naturally correspond to specialized experts? No ablation comparing sequential routing to other routing strategies (learned gating, random, etc.) is provided.
- **Equations 4 and 5 are formally identical**—both express standard scaled dot-product attention. The distinction between intra-patch and channel-wise attention lies entirely in how the input tensor dimensions are permuted before calling this formula, which is not made explicit in the equations themselves. This is a presentation issue but causes real confusion.
- **Ablation on the decoder (Figure 6) shows no consistent winner.** On some sub-tasks IP-only wins; on others Ch-only wins; their combination is not always best. Using both as a fixed design choice "to avoid hyperparameter search" is a pragmatic but not principled decision.
- **Toy example limitations.** The synthetic toy experiment (linear mapping with noise) is used to motivate the entire paper, but it considers only a *denoising* view of transformation. Real TSF datasets involve non-stationarity, seasonality, and complex inter-variable dependencies that are not captured by this setup.

### Tiny

- Several recent strong baselines (e.g., TimeMixer, FITS, SCINet variants) are absent from comparisons; the chosen baselines include some older works (FEDformer, TimesNet) while newer ICLR/NeurIPS 2024 methods are not considered.
- The "Pareto frontier" language implies formal Pareto optimality guarantees (Section 3.3), but the gradient-convergence constraint in Equation 3 is dropped for non-linear models, and no rigorous Pareto characterization is given.
- Minor notation inconsistency: the paper uses both $L$ (look-back) and $H$ (horizon) with swapped roles at different points.

---

## Nice-to-Haves

- A theoretical or empirical study of *when* the proximal constraint helps vs. hurts (e.g., the Traffic failure case) would substantially strengthen the paper.
- Efficiency profiling (training time, parameter count, GPU memory) comparing PTN-enhanced models against their respective baselines would help practitioners decide when to apply PTN.
- Comparison of sequential MoE routing against learned or random routing in an ablation.

---

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation is the emergent self-supervised predictability clustering: in the joint proximity–prediction loss space, data samples naturally segregate into high-variance (less predictable) and low-variance (more predictable) clusters, with PTN's performance gains concentrated in the latter. This suggests that the co-optimization inadvertently learns to assess predictability without explicit supervision—an interesting emergent property not clearly anticipated by the design. The related finding that the CNN encoder acts as a channel-distribution aligner (Figures 5c/5d) offers a new lens on why channel-independent strategies work: learned proximal transformations can align cross-channel distributions even when standard normalizations like RevIN cannot.

---

## Suggestions

1. **Investigate and explain the Traffic regression.** Understand why PTN consistently hurts MSE on Traffic for most backbones, and either fix the design or clearly scope the method's applicability.
2. **Strengthen or remove Proposition 1.** Either provide a meaningful proof with constructive guarantees about the proximal transformation, or replace the proposition with a clearly labeled empirical/intuitive argument.
3. **Add computation cost analysis** (parameters, runtime) relative to each backbone to assess practical overhead.
4. **Ablate MoE routing strategy** (sequential vs. random vs. learned) to justify the current design.
