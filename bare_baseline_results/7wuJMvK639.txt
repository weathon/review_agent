## Summary
"Puppeteer" is a hierarchical JEPA-style world model for visual whole-body humanoid control with a 56-DoF simulated robot. It trains a low-level tracking agent on MoCap data (reused across tasks) and a high-level puppeteering agent (trained per task via online RL) that outputs end-effector reference positions, both implemented as TD-MPC2 world models. The method achieves competitive task performance vs. baselines while producing motions strongly preferred by human evaluators (97.8% preference).

---

## Strengths

- **Elegant reuse of a single low-level world model** across all downstream tasks, requiring only ~3M environment steps vs. ~150M for MoCapAct — this is a substantial efficiency gain evidenced in Section 4.1.
- **Compelling naturalness results**: user study with 51 participants shows 97.8% preference for Puppeteer over TD-MPC2; quantitative proxies (torso height, episode length) further support this in Table 1.
- **Novel treatment of termination signals in world model planning** (Section 3.3): soft truncation via cumulative discounting of termination probabilities is a concrete and non-trivial engineering contribution not previously explored.
- **Zero-shot generalization**: generalizes to gap lengths 3× the training range without fine-tuning (Figure 9), suggesting the hierarchy encodes useful structure.
- **Well-structured ablations**: data mixture, number of MoCap clips, hierarchical planning ablation, and high-level pretraining all provide actionable insights.
- **Open benchmark + code**: the 8-task visual humanoid suite fills a genuine gap; HumanoidBench (concurrent work) does not handle visual inputs.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Performance–naturalness trade-off left unresolved**: Puppeteer underperforms TD-MPC2 on the *stairs* task (Section 4.2), which the authors attribute to reward hacking, but no mechanism is proposed to detect or prevent this. The hierarchy can still be "puppeteered" into unnatural behavior if task rewards incentivize it, meaning naturalness is contingent on benign reward design rather than structurally guaranteed.

- **Computational cost is prohibitive**: pretraining the tracking world model alone takes ~12 days on a single RTX 3090. This is presented as a one-time cost, but it severely limits accessibility and reproducibility. No wall-clock numbers for the puppeteering stage are provided for each task individually, making total cost hard to assess. Comparison with baselines that train from scratch in presumably less time warrants explicit discussion.

- **Missing ablation on temporal abstraction (k)**: k (the number of low-level steps per high-level step) is explicitly introduced as a key design parameter enabling temporal abstraction, yet k=1 is used throughout. No ablation over k is provided. This is a notable gap given the paper's framing of temporal abstraction as a benefit of the hierarchical approach.

- **Naturalness metrics are weak proxies**: only torso height and episode length are reported quantitatively (Table 1, evaluated only on *gaps*). These are highly indirect. For a paper that explicitly proposes "evaluating naturalness" as a contribution, pose-distribution metrics (e.g., FID against MoCap distribution) or kinematic energy/smoothness measures across all tasks would be more rigorous and convincing.

### Minor

- The JEPA-style framing (LeCun, 2022) is invoked but not deeply operationalized — the architecture is really two TD-MPC2 world models stacked, with no prediction-in-latent-space mechanism that distinguishes JEPA from standard latent dynamics models. The framing adds conceptual motivation but may be slightly misleading.
- User study participants are exclusively undergraduate/graduate students ("across multiple universities and disciplines"), which is a convenience sample. No inter-rater reliability measure (e.g., Fleiss' κ) is reported. Results are overwhelmingly one-sided, so this is unlikely to change conclusions, but methodological completeness would help.
- The visual tasks all share an identical reward (forward velocity). This limits evaluation diversity and makes it hard to assess whether the hierarchy truly generalizes across reward types.

### Tiny

- Figures 5 and 8 legends are inconsistent (Puppeteer is labeled "red" twice in Figure 5 legend — likely a typo for orange/red).
- The paper does not report confidence intervals in Figure 9, explicitly noting they are "omitted for visual clarity," but this omits important information for a key claim.

---

## Nice-to-Haves

- Evaluate naturalness metrics (FID or similar) against the MoCap dataset distribution across all 8 tasks rather than just the *gaps* task.
- Ablation over k ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8} to characterize the control-granularity/motion-prior trade-off.
- Report wall-clock costs per task and compare to baselines more explicitly, given compute is a major practical consideration.

---

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel insight is the demonstration that a *single* low-level tracking world model, trained offline on a large MoCap corpus via online+offline data mixture, can serve as a universal physics "interface" enabling high-level policies to learn diverse visual locomotion tasks with orders-of-magnitude fewer environment interactions. This reframing — where MoCap data is not used for reward shaping or adversarial supervision but as a pretraining dataset for a reusable physics controller — is a meaningful conceptual shift. The finding that planning at *both* levels of the hierarchy is critical (normalized score drops from 43.0 to 6.4 without low-level planning; Figure 8) is also a notable empirical result, suggesting that model-free high-level controllers cannot effectively exploit learned low-level world models in high-dimensional settings.

---

## Suggestions

1. Add an ablation over k and discuss the motion-prior vs. control-granularity trade-off more explicitly.
2. Include distribution-level naturalness metrics (e.g., FID against MoCap reference poses) for all 8 tasks.
3. Provide a compute-normalized comparison (e.g., performance as a function of GPU-hours) against MoCapAct and DeepMimic, even if observation spaces differ, to give readers better context for the efficiency claim.
4. Analyze failure modes: when does Puppeteer produce unnatural motions despite the MoCap prior? This would sharpen the contribution and guide future work.

---

## Final Score

The paper addresses a well-motivated and challenging problem with a clean hierarchical design, strong naturalness results, a useful new benchmark, and solid ablations. The core algorithmic contribution is somewhat incremental (two TD-MPC2 models, one pretrained on MoCap), and there are meaningful gaps in ablation coverage (k) and naturalness evaluation rigour. The computational cost is a practical barrier. Overall, a solid contribution to the field at the intersection of model-based RL and humanoid control.
