## Summary
MoS (Mixture of Shards) proposes a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that extends LoRA by combining inter-layer and intra-layer parameter sharing with four differentiation strategies: subset selection, pair dissociation, vector sharding, and shard privatization. A MoE-like routing mechanism selects and concatenates shards from global shared pools to construct low-rank matrices, achieving approximately 8× parameter savings over standard LoRA while maintaining competitive performance on instruction-following benchmarks.

## Strengths
- **Well-motivated empirical analysis of sharing vs. differentiation trade-off** (Table 1): The paper rigorously demonstrates that pure parameter sharing degrades performance and that differentiation is necessary to recover and surpass baseline LoRA—this is a genuinely useful finding that guides the design.
- **Comprehensive ablation study**: Each of the four differentiation strategies is individually removed and the performance impact measured (Table 2), validating that all contribute, with pair dissociation and shard privatization being most critical.
- **Practical advantages preserved**: MoS inherits LoRA's no-inference-latency and weight-merge properties, making it immediately applicable to existing serving frameworks.
- **Multi-scale experiments**: Results on LLaMA2-7B, LLaMA2-13B, and LLaMA3.2-3B provide reasonable robustness evidence across model sizes.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The "8× savings" framing is misleading.** The claim compares MoS at 19.99M params to LoRA at rank 64 (159.91M params, avg 37.53). But LoRA at rank 8 (19.99M params, same budget) achieves 36.89, while MoS achieves 37.63 — a 0.74% improvement at identical cost. The "8× savings" only holds if one specifically targets the rank-64 LoRA performance level, which is a cherry-picked target. No justification is given for why rank-64 is the appropriate target rather than rank-8 or rank-16.
- **The routing is frozen random, not truly learned.** Index matrices are randomly initialized and frozen — this is not a genuine MoE routing mechanism. The paper frequently invokes "MoE-like" as a strength and positions novelty on it, but the core mechanism is static random selection. The comparison to MoE (which learns routing) is overstated and not critically discussed.
- **Narrow benchmark scope.** All experiments are on instruction-following tasks (MMLU, BBH, GSM8K, TyDi QA, HumanEval). There is no evaluation on commonsense reasoning, NLU benchmarks (GLUE/SuperGLUE), or generation tasks, leaving unclear whether gains generalize beyond instruction tuning.
- **Missing baseline comparisons.** (IA)³, prefix-tuning, full fine-tuning, and adapter-based methods are absent. More importantly, there is no comparison with DARE or other recent parameter-reducing methods.

### Minor
- The combinatorial diversity theoretical motivation (Appendix B.1) is relegated to the appendix and never elaborated in the main text, weakening the theoretical grounding.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (shard size $l$, pool size, ratio of public/private shards) is not analyzed, leaving practitioners with little guidance.
- The absolute performance differences between MoS and baselines at 5M params are modest: 36.39 vs. 36.03 (ProLoRA) — roughly 0.36% average improvement. The practical significance for a user is debatable.

### Tiny
- The paper uses "MOS" and "MoS" inconsistently throughout.
- Figure 2 caption appears twice with slightly different wordings.
- The claim of being "the first to apply MoE for parameter savings in a single-task LoRA" is narrowly scoped to avoid prior work, but the boundary is not clearly defined.

## Nice-to-Haves
- An analysis of the training time / memory overhead compared to vanilla LoRA (the routing mechanism may add non-trivial overhead during training).
- An experiment showing actual GPU memory savings when serving 10,000 simultaneous LoRA modules (the motivating example in the introduction) rather than just counting parameters.
- Investigation of whether learned (gradient-based) routing could further improve over frozen random routing.

## Novel Insights
The insight that parameter sharing and differentiation must be balanced — and that naive pure sharing actually hurts performance — is well-argued and empirically grounded. The pair dissociation idea (treating A and B matrix vectors as independently sampled from separate pools rather than as fixed pairs) is an elegant and simple trick that substantially improves combinatorial diversity with zero parameter cost. These two ideas together provide a clean conceptual framework for thinking about PEFT parameter sharing that could generalize beyond LoRA.

## Suggestions
- Report a proper iso-parameter comparison table: for each parameter budget (1M, 5M, 20M, 40M), show all methods' performance. This is cleaner than the mixed "8×" framing.
- Replace or augment the "MoE-like" branding with an honest discussion of frozen vs. learned routing, and add an experiment with learned routing (e.g., Gumbel-softmax) as an ablation.
- Add at least one non-instruction-following benchmark suite (e.g., GLUE/SuperGLUE or commonsense QA) to establish broader generalizability.
- Provide a wall-clock training time comparison to validate the "nearly cost-free" claim for the differentiation strategies.
