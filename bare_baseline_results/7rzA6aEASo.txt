## Summary
This paper studies the trade-off between ensemble size and per-model size under a fixed total parameter budget for ensembles of random-feature ridge regression (RFRR) models. The central theoretical contribution ("no free lunch" Theorem 2) proves that with a fixed total feature count M, optimal test risk is always achieved by K=1 (a single large model) when the ridge parameter is optimally tuned. The paper further derives scaling laws parameterized by a "growth exponent" ℓ, identifying regimes (r > 1/2 easy tasks) where near-optimal performance can still be achieved with ensembles. Empirical experiments on CNNs (CIFAR-10) and transformers (C4) suggest the principle extends to feature-learning deep networks when weight decay and richness are jointly tuned.

---

## Strengths

- **Rigorous theoretical foundation**: Theorems 1 and 2 are cleanly stated and proved within the RFRR framework, building naturally on prior work (Simon et al., 2023). The bias-variance decomposition in Eq. (16) is a crisp formulation that makes Theorem 2 almost transparent.
- **Novel scaling law framework**: The growth exponent ℓ (Eq. 20) elegantly parameterizes the joint scaling of K and N, giving analytic scaling exponents (Eq. 21). This characterization of when near-optimal scaling is achievable with ensembles (r > 1/2 regime) is a genuine contribution beyond the simple no-free-lunch statement.
- **Theory-experiment alignment**: Figures 1–4 demonstrate that the omniscient risk estimate (Eq. 16) closely tracks numerical simulations across a range of P, N, K values, lending credibility to the theory.
- **Practical extension to deep networks**: The experiments spanning lazy and rich regimes on CNNs, ResNets, and transformers (Fig. 5) meaningfully connect the RFRR theory to contemporary deep learning practice, especially with the μP parameterization enabling width-consistent comparisons.
- **Clear practical conclusion**: The condition under which the result holds (optimal regularization/weight decay) is stated transparently, which is intellectually honest and informative.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Feature learning is absent from the theory**: The proof of the "no free lunch" theorem applies only to RFRR, which lacks feature learning. The extension to the rich/feature-learning regime of deep networks is entirely empirical, and the authors acknowledge this gap. Since practical deep learning is dominated by feature learning, the most important regime is left without theoretical grounding. This significantly limits the scope of the theoretical contribution.
- **Strong dependence on perfect hyperparameter tuning**: The central result requires the ridge parameter (or weight decay + richness γ) to be *optimally* tuned. The paper itself notes in Figure 2C that ensembles can outperform a single large model at suboptimal λ, and at intermediate γ values, the monotonicity can be reversed before optimal tuning is applied (Section 6). In practice, optimal tuning is rarely achievable, which meaningfully undermines the practical prescription "use one large model."
- **Limited scale of deep learning experiments**: The CNN experiments use a small 2-layer architecture, and the transformer experiments run for only 5,000 training steps on C4. These are quite small-scale compared to settings where ensemble decisions are actually made in practice. Stronger claims about deep ensembles would require validation at larger scale.
- **Mixture of Experts excluded without analysis**: MoE architectures are explicitly carved out as out of scope (Section 7), yet they are the most common modern approach to scaling via ensemble-like sub-networks. The no-free-lunch theorem is silent on the regime where MoE would apply, limiting its practical relevance to state-of-the-art practice.

### Minor
- The CIFAR-10 and MNIST experiments use binarized class subsets, which are substantially simpler than the full multi-class tasks. Fitted eigenstructure parameters (e.g., r ≈ 0.038 for binarized CIFAR-10) may not reflect the difficulty of actual real-world tasks.
- The comparison focuses exclusively on ensembles trained on the same dataset. Disjoint data-sharding across ensemble members (common in distributed training) is not considered, which could change the variance structure.
- Figure 5C shows monotonic increase in loss with K for all γ values in the transformer case without any weight decay or tuning — this is consistent with the claim but also the simplest case and not fully discussed.

### Tiny
- Typo: "Defillipis et al. et. al." in Section 3.1.
- The Gaussian universality assumption is invoked without a discussion of when it is expected to hold for the ReLU RF models used in experiments.

## Nice-to-Haves
- An analysis of heterogeneous ensembles (different widths per member) or data-sharded ensembles would round out the practical picture.
- A deeper examination of the intermediate γ regime in deep networks, where the no-free-lunch principle can temporarily fail before optimal tuning, would strengthen the claim.
- Connecting the RFRR scaling results to empirical scaling exponents for deep feature-learning models (e.g., neural scaling laws for transformers) would significantly boost practical impact.

---

## Novel Insights
The most genuinely novel insight is that *optimal regularization is the key factor eliminating any advantage of ensembling*: under optimal ridge tuning, the variance reduction from averaging K models is always dominated by the additional bias reduction achievable by allocating all M features to a single model. This explains, from first principles, why the empirical deep learning community has largely shifted toward single large models with careful regularization. The scaling law framework with the growth exponent ℓ adds a precise characterization of the task-dependent conditions (source exponent r and spectral decay α) under which this principle is sharp vs. near-degenerate — an insight that goes beyond what was known from single-model scaling laws.

---

## Suggestions
- Extend the formal theory to at least a simplified feature-learning model (e.g., a two-layer network in the mean-field limit or the lazy-to-rich interpolation of Bordelon et al. 2024b) to close the gap between RFRR theory and the deep network experiments.
- Report results at larger model scales (e.g., full-scale CIFAR-10, longer transformer pretraining runs) to increase confidence in the extension to deep feature-learning models.
- Add an experiment or analysis of ensembles where each member is trained on a disjoint data shard to address the data-sharding scenario.
