## Summary
This paper introduces mixed-curvature decision trees (DTs) and random forests (RFs) that operate natively on product manifolds—Cartesian products of hyperbolic, hyperspherical, and Euclidean components. The core technical contribution is an angular reformulation of splits: by projecting data into 2D subspaces and thresholding angles, the authors obtain splits that are geodesically convex, maximum-margin, and composable across all constant-curvature geometries. The method is evaluated on 57 benchmarks spanning synthetic data, graph embeddings, VAE latent spaces, and empirical datasets.

---

## Strengths

- **Principled geometric unification**: The angular reformulation elegantly unifies DT learning across all constant-curvature manifolds within a single framework. By parameterizing splits as angle thresholds in 2D ambient-space subspaces, the authors recover hyperbolic DTs (Chlenski et al., 2024), introduce hyperspherical DTs, and trivially recover Euclidean DTs—each with the same algorithm skeleton but manifold-specific midpoint formulas. The geometric properties (geodesic convexity, maximum-margin) are cleanly stated and theoretically grounded.

- **Comprehensive empirical evaluation**: The benchmark suite is genuinely broad—57 tasks across classification, regression, and link prediction, with diverse geometric structures, task types, and data sources (synthetic Gaussians on manifolds, graph embeddings, VAE latent codes, geospatial data, neural spike trains). The use of Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon tests for statistical significance is rigorous.

- **Strong single-manifold results**: The proposed method dominates all baselines on all 11 single-manifold classification benchmarks (top-2 in 11/11) and all 11 regression benchmarks (ranked first in 11/11), with statistical significance. The land-vs-water visualization (Figure 5) nicely illustrates the advantage of geodesically-informed splits over Euclidean or tangent-plane approximations.

- **Practical composability for product manifolds**: The extension of feature subsampling (for random forests) to non-Euclidean spaces is non-trivial and practically useful. Recasting downstream tasks (link prediction, time-series analysis) as inference on product manifold coordinates is a valuable perspective.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **k-NN dominates in product manifold classification (Table 2)**: In every single synthetic multi-K classification experiment (8/8 signatures), k-NN with proper product manifold distances outperforms both Product DTs/RFs and ambient DTs/RFs, often by a wide margin (e.g., 47.5 vs. 40.0 on H⁴). This is the core new setting the paper claims to address—and the proposed method loses to a trivial baseline. The paper acknowledges this briefly but offers no analysis of *why* k-NN is so strong here, nor whether this gap would narrow with better hyperparameter tuning, more data, or deeper trees.

- **Product method loses on real empirical regression tasks**: On Temperature and Traffic (Table 3), the Product RF is strictly worse than both Ambient and k-NN. These are the most "real-world" datasets in the benchmark. Similarly, for Landmasses classification, k-NN wins with 91.4% vs. 84.2% for Product RF. The paper's headline claim ("ranked first on 39 of 57 benchmarks") masks that the product manifold benchmarks—the key novelty—are where performance is most competitive or negative relative to k-NN.

- **Narrow baseline comparison**: No comparison to gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, LightGBM) or oblique DT methods that also use projections. These are the competitive state-of-the-art for tabular/embedding classification tasks, and the reviewers are left unsure whether the proposed method is competitive with widely-used tools. MLP/GNN comparisons are deferred entirely to the appendix without discussion in the main text.

### Minor

- **Assumption of known signature**: The experiments use the optimal signature (chosen by metric distortion), which is an idealized setting. In practice, signature selection is a hard, unsolved problem. The paper does mention this as a limitation, but since the entire method is predicated on knowing the signature, this deserves more discussion.

- **Euclidean embedding lift (Eq. 17)**: Embedding ℝᴰ into ℝᴰ⁺¹ via φ(u) = (1, u) solely to fit the angular framework is an unnecessary formalism for what reduces to standard axis-aligned splits. It adds notational overhead without additional expressiveness.

- **"Maximum-margin" claim is informal**: The paper states splits are "maximum-margin" but does not define the margin in the manifold sense or prove this property rigorously. For hyperbolic space this likely follows from Chlenski et al. (2024), but for spherical and product cases no proof or citation is given.

### Tiny

- The paper has significant formatting artifacts (blank line numbers spanning pages 7–9), suggesting incomplete PDF-to-text conversion.
- Figure 1's caption appears verbatim three times, and Figure 2 similarly.
- The claim that the method "ranks first on 21 of 22 single-manifold benchmarks" in the abstract vs. "10 out of 11 classification signatures" in Section 4.4 requires reconciliation with Table 1's counts (10+11=21, with the 22nd being the one borderline case).

---

## Nice-to-Haves

- An analysis of *why* k-NN wins on product manifold classification when the signature is known—is it because decision boundaries in mixed-curvature spaces are inherently non-axis-aligned? This could motivate future oblique variants.
- A proper complexity vs. accuracy tradeoff analysis comparing product DTs/RFs to k-NN (k-NN has O(n) inference cost, whereas DTs are O(depth)).
- Experiments with signature mismatch (using a wrong/approximate signature) to understand robustness.

---

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel technical insight is the observation that homogeneous hyperplanes are geodesically convex in *all* constant-curvature manifolds, not just hyperbolic space—which allows the angular parameterization of Chlenski et al. to immediately generalize. The resulting composability (each component manifold contributes its own angular features, and the DT allocates splits across components by information gain) is elegant and practically useful. However, the surprising finding that k-NN with the correct manifold metric outperforms geometry-aware decision trees on product manifold classification is an underanalyzed empirical observation that, if explained, could substantially advance understanding of when non-Euclidean inductive biases in tree models actually help.

---

## Suggestions

1. Provide a systematic analysis of when k-NN dominates product DTs in Table 2—examine factors such as sample size, dimensionality, number of classes, and degree of manifold mixing.
2. Include at least one comparison to XGBoost or LightGBM applied to ambient-space features, to establish competitive standing in the broader ML landscape.
3. Clarify and formalize the "maximum-margin" claim with a short proof or reference for the spherical case.
4. Add an experiment varying the number of training samples to show that the product method's advantage over k-NN grows with data size (a natural hypothesis given that DTs are more data-efficient at large scales).
5. In the main text, briefly discuss the MLP/GNN appendix results and their implications.

---

## Final Score

The paper makes a solid, well-motivated contribution—extending geometry-aware DTs to the full range of constant-curvature manifolds and product manifolds—with a comprehensive evaluation. However, the core novelty (product manifold DTs) is consistently outperformed by k-NN in the synthetic classification benchmarks, the real-world regression results are mixed, and the baselines do not include state-of-the-art tree methods. The single-manifold results are impressive and the angular framework is elegant, but the product-manifold case needs more investigation and analysis to be fully convincing.
