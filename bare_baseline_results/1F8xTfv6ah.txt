## Summary
This paper proposes a novel OOD detection method that exploits the **local neuroplasticity** of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs). The key idea is to compare activation patterns of a trained KAN against an untrained counterpart: InD samples activate "trained" spline regions (producing large differences from the untrained reference), while OOD samples activate unmodified regions (small differences). To handle joint feature distributions, the method decomposes the training set into partitions, training a separate KAN per partition and aggregating scores. The approach is validated on seven benchmarks (CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet-200/1K FS, three tabular medical datasets) and achieves competitive or top performance across most benchmarks.

---

## Strengths

- **Principled and elegant core insight.** B-splines are locally-supported basis functions, so trained KAN coefficients encode a geometric "memory" of InD data. The mathematical derivation (Eq. 5: decomposing Δ into coefficient differences masked by basis functions) clearly explains *why* the method works, offering more interpretability than most OOD detectors.
- **Post-hoc and architecture-agnostic.** The detector plugs into any pre-trained backbone without modifying training, making it broadly applicable.
- **Robustness to dataset size (Table 6).** The KAN detector remains nearly flat as training-set size drops from 100% to 0.1%, far outperforming VIM, KNN, and NAC at low-data regimes. This is a practically valuable property with clear mechanistic explanation.
- **Broad evaluation.** Seven benchmarks spanning two domains, multiple baselines, statistical significance testing with Welch's t-test, and ablations on key hyperparameters (partitions, grid size, scoring function).
- **Ablation on task agnosticism (Section 3.3).** Showing that the training task need not be OOD-related (regression to a constant works comparably) strengthens the conceptual story.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Overstated claims of superiority.** The abstract and conclusions state the method outperforms SOTA "across all seven benchmarks." However, on CIFAR-100 (Table 1), KAN (83.44 ± 1.99) vs. NAC (83.36 ± 0.84) and RMDS (82.00 ± 1.15) have widely overlapping confidence intervals; the margin is negligible and may not reach statistical significance in the overall average. On the Age medical benchmark (Table 4), KAN achieves 50.5±0.5—barely above chance and within the margin of KLM (51.0±0.7) and MDS (50.8±1.1). These cases are not "wins."
- **No computational cost analysis.** Training one KAN per partition (up to P=10 used in CIFAR experiments), each requiring forward/backward passes over the full InD dataset, plus dual forward passes per test sample—this is substantially more expensive than KNN (an index lookup) or NAC. The paper never reports training time, inference latency, or memory footprint, which is a serious omission for any claim of practical utility, especially on ImageNet-1K with P partitions × large feature sets.
- **Fundamental limitation in joint distribution modeling underplayed.** Section 2.3 acknowledges the method captures only marginal distributions. The partitioning fix is essentially an ensemble of marginal detectors. With P=10 class-based partitions on CIFAR-10, each KAN sees only one class at training—this is essentially a one-class per KAN classifier. The claim of "capturing joint distribution" is weak: a KAN trained on a single class still only sees pairwise spline functions, not true joints. The analysis in Figure 3 is only 2D.

### Minor
- **Scoring function details hidden in removed appendix.** The choice of median as F_score and max as F_agg is stated but the justification in Appendix A.1 is unavailable. The main text does not sufficiently motivate these choices beyond a brief remark.
- **Performance on Age benchmark (Table 4) is barely above random (50.5 AUROC),** suggesting the KAN detector fails to generalize when the InD/OOD separation is subtle at the feature level. This limitation warrants more discussion.
- **Histogram ablation comparison (Section 3.3, 85.29% vs. ~94%)** is presented as evidence of KAN superiority, but the histogram baseline is not fairly tuned (no partitioning strategy described for it). This may inflate the perceived advantage.

### Tiny
- "InD" (In-Distribution) is non-standard; the community convention is "ID."
- Section 4.2 cites "(Hou & Zhang, 2024)" as introducing KAN, inconsistent with the primary KAN citation "(Liu et al., 2024b)"—likely an erroneous citation.
- Figure 1 caption is confusing: it describes the untrained KAN as processing the InD sample and the trained KAN processing the OOD sample separately, rather than showing both networks receiving both samples.

---

## Nice-to-Haves
- A comparison of inference time and memory cost against KNN, NAC, and VIM would greatly strengthen the practical argument.
- A sensitivity analysis to spline order *k* (the paper only varies G) would be informative.
- Extending the method to detection without any backbone (raw-pixel features) for images would clarify the true scope of the approach.

---

## Novel Insights
The most genuinely novel observation is that B-spline local support in KANs can be leveraged directly as a **topographic memory** of training data: the coefficient difference map |c_trained − c_untrained| functions as a spatial "saliency map" of InD regions within activation space, and the basis functions B_i(x) serve as a geometric mask selecting which regions a test point activates. This insight—that an untrained KAN acts as a natural null-distribution reference—is clean and connects architecture design (local basis functions) to a downstream capability (OOD detection) in an interpretable way. This contrasts with most OOD methods, which treat the network as a black box and derive scores from logits or gradients. The idea of using the *difference between trained and untrained model activations* as a score is, to the reviewer's knowledge, novel.

---

## Suggestions
1. Include wall-clock training and inference time comparisons against top baselines (KNN, NAC, VIM) across all benchmark scales.
2. Temper claims of universally superior performance by acknowledging statistical ties (CIFAR-100, Age benchmark) in the abstract and conclusions.
3. Provide a theoretical or empirical analysis of *how many* partitions are needed to approximate the true joint distribution as a function of input dimensionality and class structure.
4. Evaluate the histogram baseline with the same partitioning strategy used for KAN to ensure a fair ablation.
