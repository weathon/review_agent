## Summary
This paper investigates why grokking (delayed generalization) often fails to occur without regularization. The authors identify two related phenomena: *Softmax Collapse* (SC), where floating-point absorption errors in the Softmax cause gradients to vanish when logits become extreme, and *Naïve Loss Minimization* (NLM), a training dynamic where gradients align with the weight direction and scale logits without changing predictions. To address these, the authors propose StableMax (a numerically stable Softmax replacement) and ⊥Grad (an optimizer that projects gradients orthogonal to the NLM direction), both of which induce grokking in settings where it was previously absent without regularization.

---

## Strengths

- **Clear mechanistic diagnosis with mathematical precision**: SC is formally defined via floating-point absorption errors (Def. 1–3) and NLM via positive homogeneity of the network (Defs. 5–6). The causal chain overfitting → NLM → logit growth → SC → zero gradients → no grokking is clean and falsifiable.

- **Experimental validation via targeted interventions**: The hypothesis that SC blocks grokking is tested directly: (i) changing FP precision (float16 → float32 → float64) shifts SC onset predictably; (ii) StableMax eliminates SC and induces grokking without regularization across modular addition, multiplication, and sparse parity; (iii) artificially zeroing gradients replicates SC behavior (App. B.1). This is scientifically rigorous design.

- **Unifying explanatory power**: The paper coherently explains multiple prior findings—why weight decay induces grokking, why MSE loss doesn't need regularization on shallow networks (MSE doesn't benefit from logit scaling), why scaling initial weights delays generalization. This breadth is a significant plus.

- **Practical contributions that validate theory**: Both StableMax and ⊥Grad are simple and elegant. Their effectiveness provides strong support for the theoretical claims, and the code is released.

- **Evidence of NLM across architectures**: Fig. 5 shows cosine similarity between gradients and the NLM direction approaching 0.9 for output layers in both MLPs and one-layer transformers, with and without bias, strengthening the generality claim.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Theoretical treatment of non-homogeneous models is incomplete** — The NLM theory is formalized only for positively homogeneous networks (ReLU MLPs/transformers without biases). The paper notes that models with bias terms are "quasi-homogeneous" per Kunin et al. (2023) and empirically shows NLM behavior in them, but provides no formal guarantee that the weight direction remains an NLM direction in these models. Since all standard architectures have biases, this gap weakens the theoretical grounding for the most practically relevant setting.

- **StableMax design is ad hoc** — The choice of s(x) = x+1 for x≥0 and 1/(1-x) for x<0 is not justified from first principles. There is no ablation exploring alternative numerically stable functions (e.g., Softplus-based, clipped exponentials, etc.), no analysis of what properties make StableMax the "right" choice vs. other options, and no discussion of how changing the activation function affects the loss landscape properties beyond preventing SC.

- **⊥Grad has underexplored practical implications** — The projection removes all gradient components along the weight direction, which could slow convergence on tasks that need weight norm growth. No analysis of the optimizer's effect on learning dynamics beyond grokking tasks is provided. Furthermore, it's unclear how biases (which are not subject to NLM) are handled in the projection—if projected jointly with weights, the bias terms would also be modified in potentially incorrect ways.

### Minor

- **All experiments are on small-scale toy tasks**: Modular arithmetic, sparse parity, and a 200-sample MNIST subset. The paper doesn't validate whether SC is a practical concern in real-world deep learning (e.g., in language model training with cross-entropy). The authors claim grokking is becoming recognized as a broader phenomenon, but the empirical scope remains narrow.

- **Figure 4 (middle) shows weight norms growing monotonically to ~40k during StableMax grokking**, yet no discussion of whether this growth eventually causes any downstream numerical issues or if StableMax simply delays SC indefinitely.

- **The relationship between ⊥Grad and Nesterov/projected gradient methods** is noted briefly ("similar projections used in other settings") but not fully explored. A deeper comparison would contextualize the novelty.

- **The sudden onset of grokking remains unexplained**: The paper explains *why* grokking is delayed (NLM/SC) and *why* it can occur (after these are mitigated), but provides little insight into why generalization happens *suddenly* in one phase transition rather than gradually.

### Tiny

- Figure captions appear duplicated in text (the alt text and caption are printed twice), which seems like a formatting artifact.
- "Goldilock's Zone" is misspelled inconsistently ("Goldilocks Zone" vs. "Goldilock's Zone").

---

## Nice-to-Haves

- A study of whether StableMax + ⊥Grad generalize to even slightly larger tasks (e.g., full MNIST, CIFAR-10) would significantly strengthen the claim that these findings are broadly applicable beyond toy grokking settings.
- A theoretical analysis of why weight decay, when fighting NLM, produces a *sudden* phase transition in grokking rather than smooth generalization (unlike ⊥Grad which generalizes quickly without a phase transition).

---

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel insight is the reframing of "why grokking needs regularization" as fundamentally a **numerical implementation artifact** rather than a purely optimization-theoretic phenomenon. The observation that float32 vs. float64 precision qualitatively changes generalization behavior—shifting SC onset and thus the possibility of grokking—is surprising and under-appreciated in the grokking literature. This suggests that published "no-grokking-without-regularization" results may be implicitly dependent on standard float32 implementations, and that some claimed theoretical necessity of regularization may actually reflect engineering constraints. The NLM concept, while related to known gradient alignment results (Lyu & Li 2020), provides a practical and operationalizable lens for understanding why gradients become unhelpful specifically after overfitting in tasks with cross-entropy loss.

---

## Suggestions

1. Add a formal treatment of quasi-homogeneous models with bias terms to complete the theoretical gap, or explicitly scope the theoretical claims to bias-free architectures.
2. Ablate the StableMax design: compare against Softplus-based CE, clipped-logit CE, and temperature-scaled CE to establish whether the specific s(x) formulation is important or if any numerically stable alternative works similarly.
3. Clarify how bias terms are handled in ⊥Grad (projected jointly with weights or separately/excluded) and test whether this design choice matters.
4. Run at least one experiment on a non-trivial task (e.g., full MNIST or a small language modeling task) to test whether SC is observable outside the canonical grokking setup.

---

## Final Score

The paper makes clear, well-validated mechanistic contributions to a topic of significant interest in the deep learning theory community. The identification of SC as a floating-point phenomenon and NLM as its cause is novel and experimentally well-supported. The main detractions are the incomplete theory for non-homogeneous models, the limited experimental scope to toy tasks, and the ad hoc nature of the StableMax design. Overall, this is a solid paper that advances understanding of grokking meaningfully.
