## Summary
This paper proposes AdaptiveComp, a framework for dynamically allocating test-time computational resources (e.g., reasoning steps or beam width) in LLMs based on estimated query complexity. It combines information-theoretic feature extraction, a neural predictor, and an early-stopping mechanism. The authors claim significant efficiency gains (47.3% average compute reduction) while maintaining performance close to a uniform high-compute baseline across eight reasoning and coding benchmarks, supported by a theoretical optimality analysis.

## Strengths
- **Clear, Well-Motivated Problem**: The paper identifies a concrete inefficiency—uniform compute allocation for diverse queries—and proposes a direct, multi-component solution. The problem is timely and practically significant for LLM deployment.
- **Strong Empirical Results**: The core efficiency claim (47.3±3.2% compute reduction) is supported by comprehensive experiments across eight diverse benchmarks (mathematical reasoning, code synthesis, multi-step planning). AdaptiveComp consistently achieves performance statistically close to the Uniform-High baseline (e.g., GSM8K: 85.9 vs. 86.3) while using much less compute (avg cost 8.4 vs. 16.0), and outperforms other adaptive baselines (Supervised, Reinforcement Learning).
- **Theoretical Contribution**: The paper provides a formal problem statement (Eq. 3) and derives an optimal allocation condition (Eq. 9) using Lagrangian optimization. The efficiency bound (Eq. 10) linking gains to task heterogeneity and prediction quality offers a principled theoretical grounding beyond typical empirical work in this area.
- **Rigorous Ablation Study**: Table 2 systematically validates each component (information-theoretic features, continuous allocation, early stopping, uncertainty adaptation), showing that each contributes meaningfully to performance and efficiency, with the feature-based component being particularly important.

## Weaknesses
### Major:
- **"True Complexity" Definition and Oracle Construction are Undefined and Circular**: The method requires training a complexity predictor `f_θ` and evaluating against an "Oracle" baseline with "perfect complexity knowledge." The paper never defines how this ground-truth complexity is obtained. Section 6.2 references "True Complexity" in Figure 2, but the text does not explain its source. The most plausible interpretation—and the one implied by the experimental setup—is that "true complexity" is derived from the model's own performance profile (i.e., the optimal `c` for each query found via exhaustive search on the target model). This creates a circular evaluation: the method learns to predict the compute budget that maximizes performance for *this specific model* on *these specific queries*, then claims efficiency by allocating that budget. This severely limits the generalizability and novelty of the "complexity estimation" contribution. The paper must explicitly define and justify the construction of the oracle/complexity labels.
- **Compute Metric is Abstract and Uncalibrated to Real-World Cost**: The paper defines compute budget `c` in Section 3.1 as "reasoning steps, beam search width, or verification iterations." In experiments (Table 1), baselines use fixed `c` values (2, 8, 16). The claimed "47.3% efficiency gain" is calculated as a reduction in this abstract `c` unit (Avg Cost column). There is no validation that a unit increase in `c` corresponds to a proportional increase in concrete computational cost (FLOPs, latency, energy, dollars). For example, doubling "reasoning steps" may not double inference time if steps are lightweight. The headline result of "substantial computational cost reduction" is therefore misleading—it shows a reduction in an uncalibrated, author-defined unit, not necessarily in real-world resource consumption.
- **Missing Critical Baseline: Simple Confidence-Based Early Stopping**: The framework includes an "uncertainty-aware early stopping mechanism." A fundamental and highly relevant baseline is a model that uses the uniform high compute budget (`c=16`) but stops early based on a simple confidence metric (e.g., the model's own output probability or entropy). This is a standard technique for adaptive computation. The paper only compares against fixed-budget Uniform-High, which never stops early. The improvement over this simple, intuitive baseline is not shown, making it impossible to judge whether the elaborate complexity estimation machinery adds value beyond basic early stopping.
- **Theoretical Analysis is Decorative, Not Integrated with the Empirical Method**: While Section 4 derives an optimal allocation condition (`∂P/∂c = λC'(c)`), the proposed allocation function (Eq. 8) is a simple sigmoid `π(q) = c_min + (c_max - c_min)·σ(β(κ̂(q) - κ_0))`. There is no argument connecting this heuristic form to the theoretical optimum. The efficiency bound (Eq. 10) is presented but not used to inform the design or analyze the results (e.g., to explain why gains are 47.3% rather than the bound). The theory feels tacked-on rather than foundational.

### Minor
- **Incomplete Method Details and Parsing Artifacts**: Several key details are missing or garbled (likely due to PDF parsing): the semantic entropy equation (Eq. 4) indices are misformatted; the early stopping and uncertainty adaptation mechanisms are mentioned but not described; Figure 1 and parts of Figures 2/3 are corrupted with placeholder text. This hinders reproducibility and full understanding.
- **Limited Analysis of Failure Modes and Risks**: The paper shows average gains but lacks a per-query error analysis. It does not systematically show: (1) which query types suffer from complexity misestimation (e.g., underestimation leading to performance drop), (2) the distribution of performance difference (AdaptiveComp vs. Uniform-High) across queries, or (3) the trade-off between risk (potential performance degradation) and efficiency. Such analysis is crucial for assessing robustness in deployment.
- **Evaluation Limited to Single Model Family**: Experiments use only Llama-2 variants (7B, 13B, Code-Llama-34B). While this is reasonable for an initial study, the generalizability of the complexity prediction and allocation strategy to other architectures (e.g., GPT-family, Mixture-of-Experts) is not explored. The learned "complexity" might be highly model-specific.

### Trivial
- **Table 1 is Incomplete**: The table cuts off after LogiQA, omitting results for CommonsenseQA and MultiArith (listed in Section 5.1). The "Avg" performance column is also missing. This is likely a formatting issue but obscures the full results.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Sensitivity Analysis of Allocation Hyperparameters**: The allocation function (Eq. 8) has parameters (`c_min`, `c_max`, `β`, `κ_0`). Showing how efficiency gains vary with their calibration across tasks/models would help assess robustness.
- **Quantification of Complexity Predictor Overhead**: The framework adds computation for feature extraction and prediction. Reporting this overhead as a percentage of base inference cost would clarify whether net savings remain positive.
- **Visual Case Studies**: Showing specific query examples with predicted complexity, allocated compute, and reasoning traces would make the system's behavior more intuitive and concrete.

## Suggestions
1. **Define and Justify "True Complexity" and Oracle Construction**: In the Method or Experimental Setup section, explicitly describe how ground-truth complexity labels are obtained. For example: "For each query, we define true complexity as the minimal compute budget `c` required to achieve at least 95% of the maximum performance achievable with `c_max`, determined via a binary search over `c` values using the base model." Clarify if this is model-specific.
2. **Calibrate Compute Metric to Real-World Cost**: Either provide evidence (e.g., latency measurements) that changes in `c` linearly correlate with concrete computational costs, or rephrase the efficiency claims to clarify they refer to reductions in the abstract "reasoning steps" unit, not guaranteed real-world savings.
3. **Add Confidence-Based Early Stopping Baseline**: Implement and compare against a baseline that uses Uniform-High compute (`c=16`) but stops early when a confidence metric (e.g., sequence probability) exceeds a threshold. This will directly test whether complexity estimation adds value beyond simple uncertainty monitoring.
4. **Connect Theory to Practice**: Revise Section 4 or Section 3.3 to explain how the sigmoid allocation function (Eq. 8) approximates or satisfies the optimality condition (Eq. 9), perhaps by showing that the predicted complexity `κ̂(q)` correlates with marginal performance gain `∂P/∂c`.
5. **Conduct Per-Query Error Analysis**: Add a subsection analyzing failure cases. Show a histogram of performance difference (AdaptiveComp - Uniform-High) per query, list examples where complexity was severely misestimated, and discuss potential safeguards (e.g., a minimum compute floor).
6. **Clean Up Method Details and Figures**: Provide corrected equations (especially Eq. 4), a clear description of the early stopping criterion, and clean versions of Figures 1, 2, and 3. Ensure Table 1 includes all benchmarks.

**Novelty**: The integrated framework for query-level adaptive compute allocation in LLMs is novel, though the individual components (feature extraction, early stopping) have precedents. The theoretical treatment of the allocation problem is a distinct contribution.
**Technical Soundness**: The empirical evaluation is comprehensive and statistically rigorous. The theoretical derivation is mathematically sound but not deeply integrated with the method. The circular definition of "true complexity" and uncalibrated compute metric undermine the soundness of the central claims.
**Empirical Support**: Experiments are extensive across eight benchmarks and show clear efficiency gains. However, missing critical baselines and undefined oracle construction limit the conclusiveness of the findings.
**Significance**: The problem is highly relevant to LLM deployment efficiency. If the claims hold with clarified definitions and added comparisons, the work could be significant.
**Clarity**: The writing is generally clear, but key methodological details are missing or garbled, and figures are corrupted. The paper requires substantial revision to be fully understandable and reproducible.
