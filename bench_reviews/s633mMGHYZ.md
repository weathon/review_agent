## Summary
This paper introduces MPS, a benchmark for evaluating spurious correlations in text classification. It systematically constructs imbalanced variants of eight existing datasets based on five categories of spurious correlations (SCS, CCS, NBS, QBS, WFS) and evaluates a wide range of models and mitigation methods. The core contribution is a unified framework for assessing robustness across diverse spurious correlation types, culminating in a comparison with human performance.

## Strengths
- **Comprehensive Benchmark Construction:** The benchmark integrates eight diverse, widely-used text classification datasets and methodically introduces five distinct categories of spurious correlations for each, creating 40 dataset variants. This provides a structured, multi-dimensional evaluation framework that is more expansive than prior benchmarks like WILDS, which cover fewer correlation types.
- **Systematic Evaluation Across Model Families:** The paper conducts extensive experiments across multiple model families (MLMs, LLMs, traditional ML) and state-of-the-art mitigation methods (DRO, JTT, NFL-family, etc.), revealing distinct vulnerabilities. For example, it identifies that autoregressive LLMs like GPT-2 are particularly susceptible to NBS-type correlations, while domain-specific MLMs (ETC, TCM) lack robustness outside their target domains.
- **Introduction and Preliminary Analysis of a Novel Correlation Type (QBS):** The paper proposes QBS (Question-Based Spurious correlations) as a novel type and provides initial evidence that it poses a unique challenge, citing that models "frequently produce systematic erroneous predictions" (e.g., Imbalanced W-ACC = 0%) on social media datasets like TweetEval. This conceptual direction is interesting and merits further study.

## Weaknesses
### Major:
- **Methodological Flaw in Benchmark Construction and Evaluation:** The paper's primary evaluation metrics, δ and Δ, are defined based on comparing model performance after training on an "Imbalanced" set versus a "Balanced" set. The Balanced set is described as being created "by sampling an equal number of instances from the original dataset, ensuring that within each ‘spurious attributes’ group, class labels are distributed as uniformly as possible." If this Balanced set is a uniform subsample of the original data, and the Imbalanced set is a manipulated version of the same original data, then the two sets differ not only in the spurious correlation but also in underlying distribution, sample size, and difficulty. This conflates the effect of the induced spurious correlation with other uncontrolled factors, undermining the validity of δ and Δ as clean measures of "collateral effect" and "potential gain." This structural issue compromises the core empirical findings about model and method vulnerabilities.
- **Insufficient Validation and Analysis of QBS:** While QBS is introduced as a novel spurious correlation type, the paper does not provide a rigorous analysis establishing why interrogative semantics ('?') constitute a distinct, *non-predictive* spurious attribute. The claim that "existing mitigation methods consistently underperform in addressing QBS-type spurious correlations" is stated but not substantiated with comparative data in the main text (Tables 3, 4 are placeholders). Without a detailed breakdown showing QBS results are systematically worse across methods, this central claim about the benchmark's novel contribution is unsupported.
- **Unfair and Misleading Human Evaluation Comparison:** The human evaluation compares zero-shot human performance (on a test set sampled from MPS) against NFL-CO models that were *trained* on the Imbalanced training set. This is an apples-to-oranges comparison: a human's ability to ignore a spurious correlation they were never exposed to is fundamentally different from a model's ability to *unlearn* a correlation it was explicitly trained on. The conclusion that "models’ capability to counteract spurious correlations... consistently underperforms compared to human performance" is not justified by this setup. A fair comparison would require training humans on analogous distributions or testing models in a few-shot/zero-shot setting.

### Minor:
- **Lack of Key Quantitative Results in Main Text:** Critical numerical findings (e.g., specific model performance numbers like "DeBERTa-v3 (94.51% on CCS:IMDB)") are referenced in the text but the corresponding results tables (Tables 2, 3, 4) in the main body are placeholders. All detailed results are relegated to the appendix. This breaches standard practice for a primary submission and makes it impossible to assess the validity of the textual conclusions without the appendix.
- **Ambiguity in Dataset Construction Details:** The process for constructing imbalanced/balanced subsets (e.g., the exact ratio for "overwhelmingly dominant" class labels, the sampling procedure for uniform distribution) is described at a high level but lacks precise algorithmic or statistical details. This ambiguity hinders reproducibility and clear understanding of the benchmark's composition.
- **Overinterpretation of Results Without Supporting Analysis:** The paper frequently makes strong causal claims (e.g., "the learning dynamics of these models remain significantly shaped by all five categories of spurious correlations") based on observed correlations in performance metrics. Without controlled ablation studies or feature attribution analyses (e.g., probing whether models actually attend to the spurious attribute), such claims remain suggestive rather than definitive.

### Trivial:
- **Generic Guidance Section:** The recommendations in Section 6 ("plug-and-play methods warrant further exploration") are generic and not strongly grounded in unique findings from the MPS benchmark, partly because the benchmark's core results are unverifiable in the main text.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Include a Simple Baseline of Training on "Balanced" Data:** The most straightforward way to break a spurious correlation is to train on data where the correlation is absent. Comparing complex methods against this simple, strong baseline would help gauge whether sophisticated methods offer any real benefit over basic balancing.
- **Feature Attribution Analysis for Each Spurious Type:** To confirm the link between benchmark construction and observed failures, an analysis (e.g., using Shapley values or integrated gradients) could show that model failure cases correlate with high attention/salience on the spurious attribute (e.g., question marks for QBS).
- **Breakdown of Worst-Group Composition:** Analyzing which specific (label, attribute) combinations are hardest and why (e.g., for QBS failures, is it all questions or only questions in a specific semantic context?) would provide granular insight into the nature of the challenge posed by each correlation type.

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.*

**Strength: "The topic is important."** - REMOVED. This is a generic strength that applies to any paper in the area. The review should highlight strengths specific to this paper's contributions.

**Weakness: "The benchmark's reliance on LLM-generated labels is a significant design choice needing more justification."** - REMOVED AS A MAJOR WEAKNESS, MOVED TO NICE-TO-HAVE. The paper addresses this concern by stating: "For the two categories of conceptual spurious correlations, we conducted supplementary manual verification to validate the reliability of the ‘spurious attributes’ generated by large language models on textual data. Specifically, 10% of the data from each respective dataset was sampled for validation. The results indicate that the ‘spurious attributes’ generated by the large language models exhibit high accuracy and strong reliability." While a deeper analysis of label noise impact would be beneficial (as suggested by Spark), the paper provides a reasonable addressal. This is a methodological choice common in the field, not a fundamental flaw.

**Weakness: "Potential data contamination issues." & "Limited discussion of benchmark validity criteria." & "Over-reliance on worst-group accuracy without sufficient justification." & "Limited exploration of real-world applicability." & "Insufficient comparison with recent spurious correlation mitigation methods." & "Human evaluation limitations." & "Lack of sensitivity analysis for group construction."** - REMOVED OR WEAKENED. These points from Human Finder are largely generic concerns or demand practices (like hiding test sets, applying specific validity desiderata, exploring group construction sensitivity) that are not standard requirements for presenting a new benchmark. The paper provides its evaluation metrics (δ, Δ) and compares to WILDS; it is not obligated to follow every validity framework from other works. The human evaluation uses a sampled test set, which is a practical approach given cost constraints. The paper includes a representative set of anti-spurious methods (DRO, JTT, NFL-family, etc.); omitting every recent method is not a fatal flaw.

**Weakness: "Missing experiments: Evaluate methods on truly 'Balanced' vs. 'Imbalanced' train sets."** - REMOVED BUT NOTE KEPT. Spark correctly identifies a critical missing control experiment: evaluating methods when trained on the Balanced dataset (where the spurious correlation is absent) to assess their ability to *avoid learning* the correlation, rather than just compensating post-hoc. This is a substantive methodological gap that should be highlighted, but it is framed as a "missing experiment" suggestion rather than a weakness of the presented work. It is noted here as an important direction for improvement.

**Weakness: "Direct test for 'exploitable features' claim." & "Statistical significance and variance of results."** - WEAKENED TO NICE-TO-HAVE. The claim about "exploitable features" is interpretive based on negative δ values; a probing analysis would strengthen it but is not required. Reporting variance across multiple seeds is good practice but not universally standard in large-scale benchmarking papers; its absence is a minor limitation.

## Suggestions
- **Redesign the benchmark construction and evaluation protocol to ensure comparable splits.** The "Balanced" training set should be constructed via the same label redistribution procedure as the "Imbalanced" set, but with the correlation removed (i.e., uniform label distribution per attribute). Alternatively, define δ and Δ based on performance differences when models are trained on the same base dataset but with different induced correlations, ensuring the only variable is the presence/strength of the spurious correlation.
- **Provide all critical quantitative results in the main text.** At minimum, include summarized tables showing average worst-group accuracies for each model family across correlation types, and key δ/Δ values for major methods. Do not rely on placeholder tables.
- **Deepen the analysis of QBS.** Provide a linguistic or logical rationale for why interrogative semantics represent a distinct, challenging spurious attribute. Show concrete examples of model failures on QBS and include a comparative analysis in the main text demonstrating that methods underperform on QBS relative to other types.
- **Revise the human evaluation comparison.** Either design a protocol where humans are exposed to the imbalanced training distribution (e.g., via a brief training phase) or compare models in a zero-shot/few-shot setting to match the human's lack of training. Alternatively, clearly frame the comparison as measuring human *zero-shot robustness* versus model *post-training robustness*, avoiding overinterpreted conclusions about relative "capability to counteract spurious correlations."
