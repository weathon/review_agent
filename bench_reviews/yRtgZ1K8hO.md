## Summary
Polar Express is a new iterative method for computing the polar decomposition and matrix sign function, designed for the constraints of deep learning—primarily for use within the Muon optimizer. The core contribution is a greedy, iterative selection of polynomial updates that, when composed, is proven to minimize the worst-case approximation error in the spectral norm. This provides a theoretically grounded alternative to heuristic methods, achieving both rapid early convergence and asymptotic super-exponential convergence while maintaining GPU-friendly operation (matrix-matrix products only). Empirical evaluations demonstrate that integrating Polar Express into Muon leads to consistent improvements in validation loss when training GPT-2 models.

## Strengths
- **Strong and elegant theoretical foundation:** The reduction of the matrix approximation problem to a scalar minimax problem (Eq. 5-6) is clear, and Theorem 3.1 provides a compelling and rigorous optimality guarantee. The greedy composition strategy is proven to yield the best possible worst-case error for a given polynomial degree and iteration count.
- **Practical, well-motivated algorithm design:** The method is explicitly built for the deep learning environment: it uses only matrix-matrix multiplications (ideal for GPUs), includes thoughtful modifications for stability in bfloat16 (Section 3.4), and employs offline precomputation for efficiency. The implementation is provided as simple, dependency-light PyTorch code.
- **Convincing and comprehensive empirical validation:** The paper demonstrates superior convergence on synthetic matrices and on actual gradient matrices from a GPT-2 model (Figure 3). Most importantly, when integrated into Muon for training GPT-2 Small and Large on the FineWeb dataset (1B and 10B tokens), Polar Express consistently yields the best final validation loss across a wide range of learning rates, with and without weight decay (Figures 1, 4, 6, 12, 13). The ablation study (Figure 5) thoughtfully establishes that 5-6 iterations are sufficient and that more accurate (or exact SVD) computation does not improve optimization performance.

## Weaknesses
### Major:
- **Sensitivity to the lower singular value bound (`ℓ`) is a critical practical weakness.** The method's optimality and convergence speed depend on knowledge of a lower bound `ℓ` for the input matrix's singular values. While the paper sets a fixed heuristic (`ℓ=10⁻³`) based on bfloat16 precision, Figure 3 clearly shows that performance degrades significantly if `ℓ` is misset by orders of magnitude. The justification that gradient matrices "tend to have small effective rank" is insufficient, and no robust, efficient mechanism for estimating or adapting `ℓ` online is provided. This leaves the method's performance vulnerable in unseen settings and makes its claimed practical advantage potentially reliant on fortunate hyperparameter tuning.
- **The analysis of how approximation error impacts optimization is shallow.** While Figure 5 shows that loss does not improve with more accurate polar approximations beyond ~6 iterations, the paper does not investigate *why*. A deeper analysis linking the spectral norm error of the polar approximation to training dynamics (e.g., gradient alignment, update norm stability) would significantly strengthen the justification for the "high throughput over high precision" design principle and explain the observed saturation point.

### Minor
- **Limited exploration of the polynomial degree (`d`) trade-off.** The paper focuses on `d=5`, citing prior work. A brief analysis or discussion of the cost-accuracy trade-off between different degrees (e.g., `d=3` vs. `d=7`) and their impact on the number of iterations (`T`) required would provide more comprehensive guidance for practitioners in different scenarios.
- **The experimental variance is unreported.** The improvements in validation loss, while consistent across learning rates, are presented as single-run results. Reporting mean and standard deviation over multiple random seeds would solidify the claim of "consistent improvements" and clarify the statistical significance of the gains.

### Trivial
- **Minor formatting/parser artifacts in the submitted PDF.** Some figure tables contain placeholder labels (e.g., "Col1", "Col2") and repeated text blocks, but the captions and main text provide all necessary numerical results and interpretations. This does not impede understanding.

## Nice-to-Haves
- An experiment applying Polar Express to compute the polar factor in another optimizer (e.g., a variant of Shampoo) would demonstrate broader utility beyond Muon.
- A visualization tracking the singular value distribution of momentum matrices (`M_t`) during training could empirically validate the assumptions about the operating range `[ℓ, u]`.

## Suggestions
- To address the major weakness, design and evaluate a simple, low-cost online heuristic to estimate or adapt the lower bound `ℓ` during the iteration (e.g., using a few steps of power iteration on `X_t^T X_t`). Demonstrating robustness to a wider range of `ℓ` guesses or adaptive selection would significantly strengthen the practical contribution.
- Include a brief analysis or discussion in Section 4.3 on *why* higher polar approximation accuracy does not translate to better loss. Relate the spectral norm error to the alignment of the update direction with the steepest spectral descent direction.
