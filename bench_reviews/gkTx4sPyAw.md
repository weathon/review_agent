## Summary
This paper proposes a simple but well-motivated intervention for LLM tool calling: replacing standard schema-constrained (e.g., JSON) prompts with a natural-language template format (e.g., "Call 'ToolName' with parameter 'X' as 'Y'"). The core hypothesis is that this format aligns better with the natural-language pre-training distribution of most LLMs, thereby reducing parsing errors and improving accuracy. The idea is evaluated across three established benchmarks (API-Bank, ToolACE, When2Call) and four diverse LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-5, Mistral-7B, DeepSeek-Coder-7B), showing F1 improvements for tool and parameter prediction in a majority of test configurations.

## Strengths
*   **Clear, Practical, and Well-Motivated Core Idea:** The central hypothesis—that a more natural-language-like output format can reduce schema-violation errors—is intuitive, logically sound, and immediately actionable for practitioners. The paper effectively grounds this in the observation that LLMs are predominantly trained on natural language, not JSON schemas.
*   **Rigorous and Comprehensive Empirical Evaluation:** The evaluation is a major strength. The paper uses multiple, challenging benchmarks that test different aspects of tool calling (single/multi-turn, dynamic tool introduction, decision to call). The inclusion of both proprietary (GPT-4o/5) and open-source models (Mistral, DeepSeek-Coder), including a code-specialized LLM, provides a broad test of the idea's generalizability. The use of statistical significance testing and a thoughtful similarity threshold for parameter matching adds methodological rigor.
*   **Insightful, Fine-Grained Error Analysis:** The paper moves beyond aggregate scores to provide a detailed, categorized breakdown of error types (Reasoning Failure, Schema Violation, Incorrect Tool/Parameter). The analysis in Sections 4.1-4.4 offers concrete, evidence-based explanations for *why* the template helps in specific scenarios (e.g., reducing bracket errors in complex JSON for Mistral, improving contextual tool selection for GPT-4o). This mechanistic insight is highly valuable.

## Weaknesses
### Major:
*   **Fundamentally Confounded Experimental Protocol for Open-Source Models:** The most serious weakness is the inconsistent treatment of models, which critically undermines the claim of a "direct and consistent comparison" (Section 2). For the proprietary GPT models, both methods are tested via prompting only. For the open-source Mistral and DeepSeek-Coder models, the paper states they were "fine-tuned with the training data" before evaluation. It is not specified whether a *single* fine-tuned checkpoint was evaluated under both prompt types, or if separate fine-tuning was conducted for each method. This lack of clarity and control means any performance difference for these models could be attributed to the fine-tuning process or its interaction with one prompt format, rather than the intrinsic superiority of the template method. This confound severely limits the interpretability and generalizability of the results for half the model suite.
*   **Incomplete and Unverifiable Analysis of Key Results (GPT-5):** The paper's attempt to analyze the inconsistent performance of GPT-5—the most advanced model—is significantly hampered. Section 4.5 references a "Figure 1" that, in the provided text, is corrupted placeholder content (repeated headers, garbled structure). This figure is meant to show the impact of "reasoning effort" on performance, a central point for understanding the method's limitations with reasoning-optimized models. Its absence makes the subsequent analysis (e.g., "template-based generation shows inconsistent trends") unsubstantiated and opaque. While Table 8 provides some counts, the critical visual evidence for the claimed interaction is missing.
*   **Methodological Contribution is Primarily Prompt Engineering:** The proposed "method" is essentially a specific prompt template. While the evaluation is extensive, the paper does not explore automated template design, learning optimal templates, or integrating the idea into model training. The contribution therefore sits more in the realm of an insightful empirical finding rather than a novel algorithmic or training methodology.

### Minor:
*   **Insufficient Robustness Analysis for the Template Parser:** The method relies on parsing the template output with regular expressions. The paper does not report the failure rate of this parser (e.g., on model outputs that deviate from the expected template syntax). If this parser is brittle, "Schema Violation" counts for the template method could be artificially low, as unparsable outputs might be silently discarded or mis-categorized, unfairly advantaging the proposed approach. A brief analysis of parser robustness is needed.
*   **Limited Discussion of Template Design Space:** The paper uses a single, fixed template structure ("Call 'API Name' with following parameters:..."). The claim that benefits stem from "natural language alignment" would be strengthened by a small ablation study showing performance is sensitive to the template's naturalness (e.g., comparing it to a more rigid but still non-JSON format).

### Trivial:
*   **Typo in Table 3 Header:** "Paramter F1" should be "Parameter F1".

## Nice-to-Haves
*   A comparison against modern, native function-calling APIs (e.g., OpenAI's tool-calling feature) would better contextualize the practical significance of the improvement over a simple JSON prompt.
*   Extending the evaluation to true multi-step tool-calling scenarios (e.g., API-Bank Level 3, later turns in ToolACE) would demonstrate utility in more complex, realistic settings.
*   A more systematic exploration of the template design space (e.g., verbosity, phrasing) could provide guidelines for practitioners.

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.*
*   **Weakness (from Harsh Critic): "The method is a simple prompt engineering change... not a novel algorithmic or training approach."** While true, this is not a valid weakness for removal. The paper makes an empirical contribution via thorough evaluation of a specific design choice. The criticism is subjective regarding the threshold for "novelty."
*   **Weakness (from Human Finder): "Limited scope of experimental evaluation: ... only three datasets and four models."** This is excessively harsh. The paper uses three substantial, peer-reviewed benchmarks and a diverse model set, which is adequate for a focused study. Demanding "broader" evaluation without a specific, justified gap is scope creep.
*   **Weakness (from Human Finder): "Insufficient comparison with alternative structured generation approaches."** The paper's stated scope is a comparison between schema-constrained (e.g., JSON) and template-based generation. While comparing to other advanced methods (e.g., constrained decoding) could be interesting, it is outside the paper's defined comparative framework. This is a suggestion for future work, not a core weakness.
*   **Weakness (from Human Finder): "Incomplete discussion of limitations and potential biases... dataset constructed using an LLM."** The paper does discuss limitations in Section 5, including dataset scope and potential pretraining data overlap. The specific point about GPT-4o-mini artifacts is irrelevant, as the paper's datasets (API-Bank, ToolACE, When2Call) are not stated to be constructed in that manner. This criticism is not grounded in the paper's content.
*   **Nitpick (from Harsh Critic): "The parsing step... is not detailed."** The paper states parsing is done with "regular expressions," which is a standard, sufficient description for reproducibility. Demiring full regex details is a granular implementation detail.
*   **Nitpick (from Harsh Critic): "The semantic similarity check... choice of threshold (0.9) and embedding model are not specified."** The paper should specify the embedding model, but the use of a similarity threshold is a reasonable and common evaluation concession for semantic equivalence. Its absence is a minor clarity issue, not a fundamental flaw.

## Suggestions
1.  **Clarify and Control the Fine-Tuning Protocol:** The paper must explicitly state the fine-tuning procedure for Mistral and DeepSeek-Coder. For a fair comparison, a single checkpoint should be fine-tuned (presumably on data formatted in a neutral way, or on a mix of formats) and then evaluated with both prompt types. If separate fine-tuning was used, the experiments should be re-run with a controlled protocol. This is the most critical revision needed.
2.  **Provide a Correct and Legible Figure 1:** Replace the corrupted figure placeholder with a clear visualization of F1 scores versus reasoning effort for GPT-5 across the four datasets. This is essential to support the analysis in Sections 4.5 and 4.6.
3.  **Add a Brief Analysis of Parser Robustness:** Include a short paragraph or table reporting the rate at which the template regex parser fails to parse the model's output, and how such failures are handled in the evaluation. This will strengthen the validity of the "Schema Violation" comparisons.
4.  **Temper Claims of Generality:** The conclusion should more explicitly acknowledge that the clearest, unconfounded evidence for the template method's benefit comes from the prompted evaluation of GPT-4o. The results for fine-tuned models and for GPT-5 are either confounded or mixed, and the claims should be nuanced accordingly.

**Overall Assessment:**
This paper presents a clear, useful, and well-evaluated insight into improving LLM tool calling through prompt design. The empirical evaluation is a strength, and the error analysis provides genuine insight. However, the **major methodological confound** in the experimental setup for the open-source models and the **missing/unverifiable key figure** for GPT-5 analysis are significant shortcomings that prevent the core claims from being fully reliable and convincing in their current form. The paper has the foundations of a good contribution but requires substantial revisions to address these issues before it can be considered for acceptance.
