## Summary
This paper addresses indirect prompt injection attacks in LLMs by proposing Augmented Intermediate Representations (AIR), a defense mechanism that injects Instruction Hierarchy (IH) signals into *all* decoder layers, not just the input layer. The authors hypothesize that input-only injection allows IH signals to degrade, limiting robustness. Experiments across three models (3B, 7B, 8B) and two training methods (SFT, DPO) show AIR reduces attack success rates (ASR) on gradient-based attacks by 1.6× to 9.2× compared to prior IH-injection methods (Delimiters, ISE), with minimal utility degradation.

## Strengths
- **Novel and well-motivated architectural contribution:** The core idea—injecting privilege signals recurrently throughout the network, analogous to advanced positional embeddings like RoPE—is a clear, logical, and novel extension of the Instruction Hierarchy defense paradigm. The intuition that layer-wise injection could better preserve signal separation is compelling.
- **Comprehensive and rigorous empirical evaluation:** The paper evaluates across multiple model families (Llama, Qwen), sizes, training methods (SFT, DPO), attack types (four static, two gradient-based), and two distinct benchmarks (AlpacaFarm, SEP). This breadth strongly supports claims of generalizability.
- **Clear empirical superiority on challenging attacks:** AIR consistently achieves the lowest ASR on the most sophisticated white-box attacks (GCG, Astra), often by a large margin (e.g., 9.2× reduction for Llama-3.2-3B SFT vs. GCG). It also improves the SEP separation score, indicating a stronger ability to distinguish instructions from data.

## Weaknesses
### Major:
- **Unclear and potentially unfair baseline comparisons for the central robustness claim:** The paper's headline result (1.6–9.2× ASR reduction) compares AIR against other IH methods (Delimiters, ISE) using a *shared adversarial training procedure*. Table 1's "None" defense column refers to models with an IH-injection architecture (Delim or ISE) that skipped adversarial training, *not* a properly trained baseline without IH. This confounds the comparison: a Delimiter model trained on data formatted for Delimiters might perform differently. The paper does not demonstrate that AIR outperforms other IH methods when each is trained optimally with its own native data format. This undermines the quantitative claim of superiority over properly configured baselines.
- **Weak mechanistic evidence for the core hypothesis:** Figure 3, which motivates the problem by showing increased representation similarity across layers for baseline methods, is presented without critical details. It is unclear if the models shown have undergone full adversarial training. The y-axis shows high similarity (0.5–1.0) even for AIR, and no statistical analysis is provided. The paper does not follow up with deeper analysis (e.g., attention patterns, probing classifiers) to show *how* AIR mitigates signal degradation or enforces hierarchy differently.
- **Insufficient evaluation against adaptive white-box attacks:** The evaluation uses standard GCG/Astra attacks optimized for a fixed number of steps. The paper does not test attacks that are *adaptive* to AIR's mechanism—for example, by allowing the adversary to optimize through the layer-specific embeddings or by using a substantially larger optimization budget. This leaves open the possibility that the observed robustness is an artifact of the specific attack configuration rather than a fundamental improvement.

### Minor:
- **Limited discussion of computational overhead:** While parameter overhead is quantified as minimal (~0.005%), claims about training overhead being "similar to prior works" and inference being "negligibly small" are not substantiated with measurements (e.g., training time, memory footprint, inference latency). This is a practical concern for adoption.
- **Utility degradation in some configurations:** For certain model/training combinations (e.g., Qwen-2.5-7B with DPO), AIR causes a measurable drop in utility (~2% on AlpacaEval). While the paper correctly notes this is small, a more explicit analysis of the robustness-utility trade-off for the best configurations (e.g., AIR-DPO) would be valuable.

### Trivial:
- **Minor phrasing issues:** The phrase "9.2× lower" in Table 1's caption is ambiguous (it means "reduction to 1/9.2" but could be misread). This does not affect substance.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Ablation studies on layer-specific embeddings:** Testing whether injecting the *same* embedding at all layers, or at only a subset of layers, achieves similar results would help validate the design choice of layer-specific embeddings.
- **Failure analysis:** Analyzing the specific cases where gradient-based attacks still succeed against AIR could reveal residual weaknesses and guide future improvements.
- **Visualization of learned embeddings:** A t-SNE/PCA plot of the learned layer-specific embeddings \(S_j\) could provide intuitive evidence for how privilege separation is maintained across layers.

## Suggestions
- **Conduct a fairer baseline comparison:** For a revision, train separate Delimiter and ISE models using data formatted *specifically for each method* (i.e., using their native tokenization/embedding scheme) with an identical adversarial training budget. Compare AIR's performance against these fairly trained baselines to substantiate the claimed improvement factor.
- **Strengthen the mechanistic analysis:** Provide a clear description of the models used in Figure 3 (were they fully trained?). Supplement with analysis showing how attention to low-privilege tokens is suppressed in AIR across layers, or use probing classifiers to demonstrate better privilege-level decoding from intermediate representations.
- **Test an adaptive attack:** Implement a white-box attack where the adversary can compute gradients through the AIR layer embeddings and optimize adversarial prefixes specifically against the defended model. Report whether AIR's robustness holds under this stronger threat model.

**Overall Assessment:**
The paper presents a novel and promising architectural idea for improving LLM security. The concept of layer-wise IH injection is insightful and could influence future defense designs. However, the current evaluation suffers from a significant methodological issue regarding baseline comparisons, which casts doubt on the precise magnitude of improvement claimed. The mechanistic justification is also underdeveloped. While the empirical results are broadly positive and the technical approach is sound, the paper requires substantial revisions to solidify its central claims. It is not yet ready for acceptance but has strong potential.
