## Summary
This paper introduces an "empiricist" paradigm for exploratory causal inference in randomized controlled trials, where the latent outcomes affected by a treatment are unknown. The method transforms unstructured data (e.g., images) into interpretable neural representations via a foundation model and a sparse autoencoder (SAE). It formalizes a "paradox" where increased statistical power causes significance collapse due to entangled representations, and proposes Neural Effect Search (NES), a recursive stratification algorithm designed to identify distinct causal effects while controlling for entanglement.

## Strengths
- **Clear Problem Formulation and Theoretical Analysis**: The paper crisply defines the rationalist vs. empiricist paradigm and formally characterizes the core challenge—the "Paradox of Exploratory Causal Inference"—where increased statistical power leads to false discoveries under neuron entanglement (Theorems 3.1, 3.2). This is a genuine and valuable theoretical contribution.
- **Novel Algorithm with Theoretical Grounding**: Neural Effect Search (NES) is a creative and principled solution to the identified paradox. The recursive stratification procedure is well-motivated, and Theorem 4.1 provides a consistency guarantee under specified assumptions, strengthening the methodological contribution.
- **Comprehensive Empirical Validation**: The evaluation includes both a controlled semi-synthetic benchmark (CelebA) to quantitatively demonstrate NES's superiority over standard multiple testing corrections, and a real-world application on an experimental ecology dataset (ISTAnt). The alignment of discovered neurons with prior domain knowledge (grooming behavior) and an experimental artifact (background marking) showcases practical utility and diagnostic capability.

## Weaknesses
### Major:
- **Strong and Untestable Assumptions Undermine Causal Discovery Claims**: The method's consistency (Theorem 4.1) and practical success rely on strong assumptions: the foundation model is *sufficient* for the latent outcomes, and the SAE provides an *approximately disentangled* representation where each causal effect has a "principally aligned" neuron (Assumptions A.1-A.3). The authors acknowledge these are the "strongest" limitations. Crucially, **these assumptions are not verifiable in the target exploratory setting where ground-truth latent variables are unknown**. This gap means the method cannot guarantee *causal discovery*; it identifies statistical associations in a learned representation whose faithfulness to true causal factors is unverified. The real-world experiment validates correlation (a discovered neuron is predictive of a known label) but does not provide independent evidence of novel *causal* effect discovery.
- **Real-World Validation Lacks Causal Grounding and Statistical Rigor**: The application to the ISTAnt dataset (n=44) has two critical issues: (1) Validation is correlational, not causal. Showing a discovered neuron is the "most predictive" for a human-annotated behavior (grooming) demonstrates association, not that the treatment causally affects that latent variable. The method essentially recapitulates a known association from prior rationalist analysis. (2) The small sample size forced the removal of Bonferroni correction, drastically increasing the risk of false positives. The paper does not discuss how many other neurons were near-significant or provide adjusted p-values, leaving the robustness of the inference in low-data regimes unclear.

### Minor:
- **Limited Scope and Generality**: The work focuses on binary treatments and binary latent outcomes, arguing continuous concepts in SAEs are "not well understood." This restricts the application domain. Furthermore, the semi-synthetic benchmark evaluates feature *selection* (choosing known ground-truth neurons) rather than feature *discovery* (inferring unknown semantic content), which is the paper's broader claim.
- **Incomplete Empirical Comparison**: The semi-synthetic benchmark compares NES only to basic multiple testing corrections (t-test, Bonferroni, FDR) and a top-k baseline. A comparison to more sophisticated causal representation learning or disentanglement methods (even if adapted) would better situate NES's advantages and limitations.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A sensitivity analysis in the semi-synthetic experiments showing how NES performance degrades as SAE disentanglement quality or foundation model sufficiency is artificially weakened.
- More concrete guidelines for practitioners on diagnosing failure modes (e.g., what if NES returns zero neurons?) and choosing hyperparameters like the SAE sparsity weight.

## Suggestions
- To better support the claim of *causal* discovery, design a validation where ground-truth causal effects are *unknown* to the method and are later verified through a follow-up experiment or expert annotation of held-out data. Alternatively, more clearly position the work as a *hypothesis-generation* tool that finds statistical associations for expert vetting, not a causal discovery engine.
- In the real-world experiment, report the distribution of p-values or effect sizes for all neurons, not just the two selected, to contextualize the decision to forgo Bonferroni correction and assess the risk of false positives.

## Evaluation
- **Novelty**: High. The formalization of the exploratory causal inference paradox and the NES algorithm are novel contributions.
- **Technical Soundness**: The theoretical analysis is sound within its stated assumptions, but these assumptions are strong and unverifiable in practice, limiting the method's guaranteed reliability.
- **Empirical Support**: Mixed. The semi-synthetic benchmark is well-executed and demonstrates NES's advantage over baselines in a controlled setting. The real-world application is intriguing but provides only correlational, post-hoc validation and suffers from small sample size issues.
- **Significance**: Potentially significant for fields generating high-dimensional experimental data, as it offers a principled pipeline for data-driven hypothesis generation. However, the reliance on unverifiable representation assumptions tempers its immediate practical impact.
- **Clarity**: The paper is generally well-written, with clear problem formulation, method description, and theoretical results.
