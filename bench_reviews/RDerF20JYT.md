## Summary
La-Proteina introduces a partially latent flow matching framework for joint generation of protein sequences and fully atomistic structures. The core innovation is representing each residue’s sequence and side-chain details in a fixed-size continuous latent vector while modeling the backbone α‑carbons explicitly. This enables scalable, high-quality generation through a two-stage pipeline: a VAE compresses per‑residue atomistic information, and a flow matching model jointly generates backbones and latents. The method achieves state-of-the-art results on unconditional generation benchmarks, demonstrates unique scalability to proteins of up to 800 residues, and significantly advances conditional tasks such as indexed and unindexed atomistic motif scaffolding.

## Strengths
- **Novel and well-motivated representation.** The partially latent design elegantly sidesteps the mixed discrete‑continuous, variable‑dimensionality problem of all‑atom protein modeling. By keeping backbone coordinates explicit and compressing per‑residue sequence and side‑chain details into a fixed‑size latent space, the method leverages efficient, scalable backbone‑generation architectures without sacrificing atomistic detail. This is a clear conceptual advance over prior purely explicit or fully latent approaches.
- **Comprehensive and convincing empirical evaluation.** The paper establishes new state‑of‑the‑art across multiple axes: unconditional all‑atom co‑designability, diversity, and structural validity (Table 1, Fig. 4‑6); scalability to long sequences (up to 800 residues) where other all‑atom baselines fail; and superior performance on challenging conditional tasks, especially unindexed atomistic motif scaffolding across both all‑atom and tip‑atom settings (Fig. 7). The evaluation includes biophysical quality metrics (MolProbity, rotamer distributions) and thorough comparisons against publicly available baselines.
- **Practical impact through scalability and robustness.** The architecture intentionally omits computationally expensive triangular update layers in its main variant, enabling generation of long, co‑designable proteins while maintaining high performance. This addresses a significant limitation in the field and moves the capability toward real‑world protein lengths.

## Weaknesses
### Major:
- **Heavy reliance on synthetic training data.** The model is trained almost exclusively on the AlphaFold Database (AFDB), which consists of predicted structures rather than experimentally validated ones. While this is common in the field, it raises the concern that the model may learn artifacts of the prediction method rather than true physical constraints. The paper does not ablate or analyze the effect of training on predicted vs. experimental data, leaving uncertainty about generalizability to real‑world protein physics.
- **Static representation omits protein dynamics.** Proteins are dynamic molecules, and functional design often depends on conformational flexibility. La‑Proteina models only static snapshots, which limits its applicability to tasks where dynamics are critical (e.g., enzyme design or allosteric regulation). The paper does not discuss this limitation or propose extensions to incorporate dynamics.

### Minor:
- **Limited analysis of the two‑stage training pipeline.** The method requires training a large VAE (~130M parameters) followed by a flow‑matching model (~160M parameters). While results are excellent, the paper does not quantify potential error accumulation between stages or provide a thorough ablation showing the contribution of each component. A more detailed analysis of how VAE reconstruction fidelity affects final sample quality would strengthen the methodological justification.
- **Latent‑space analysis remains qualitative.** The t‑SNE visualization and local‑perturbation experiment (Fig. 8) show that latents cluster by residue type and exhibit local influence, but a more quantitative assessment—e.g., linear probes to predict biophysical properties (solvent accessibility, secondary structure) or measurement of mutual information with structural features—would better validate that the latent space encodes meaningful, disentangled representations.
- **Motif‑scaffolding evaluation could be deeper.** The success criteria (α‑carbon RMSD < 1 Å, all‑atom RMSD < 2 Å) are stated without biological context. While the tasks are clearly challenging, a brief discussion of how these thresholds relate to functional tolerances or comparison to alternative approaches (e.g., fragment‑based design) would help interpret the results. Additionally, the intriguing observation that the unindexed model sometimes outperforms the indexed one is only briefly hypothesized; a more concrete analysis of the different scaffold types generated in each setting would be insightful.

### Trivial:
- **Formatting and minor exposition issues.** Some figures and captions contain parsing artifacts (e.g., “~~AllAtom~~” in Fig. 5 caption), though these do not affect comprehension. The paper is generally well‑written and clearly organized.

## Nice‑to‑Haves
- **Correlation analysis between co‑designability and structural validity.** Showing that high co‑designability correlates with low MolProbity scores would reinforce the claim that the model generates biophysically valid designs, not merely sequences that happen to fold.
- **Visualization of longest generated proteins.** Side‑by‑side comparisons of generated vs. native structures for 800‑residue samples, along with contact‑map or radius‑of‑gyration analysis, would provide more concrete evidence of plausible long‑range folding.
- **Discussion of computational requirements.** While scalability is a key claim, a brief summary of GPU memory and time for generating different lengths would aid practitioners in deployment.

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.*

- **“Limited Baseline Comparison on Long Sequences” (Harsh Critic).** The paper explicitly notes that P(all‑atom) is computationally prohibitive beyond 500 residues (Fig. 5 caption) and that other all‑atom baselines “collapse or fail to produce valid samples” (Fig. 4). The claim that La‑Proteina succeeds where *feasible* baselines fail is accurate and sufficiently nuanced; the criticism demands an impossible comparison.
- **“Missing evaluation of in‑silico functional properties” (Spark).** The paper’s scope is generative modeling of atomistic structures and sequences; it does not claim to perform functional design or enzyme engineering. Requiring functional metrics is scope creep. The mention of “enzyme design” in the conclusion is a forward‑looking statement, not a core claim.
- **“No systematic ablation of VAE latent dimension and prior” (Spark).** While interesting, such ablations are not required to validate the core contribution. The chosen 8‑dimensional latent with isotropic Gaussian prior works well, and the paper includes latent‑space analysis showing it captures residue‑specific features.
- **“No evaluation on refolded sequences for motif scaffolding” (Spark).** The paper already includes this evaluation in Fig. 33 (referenced in Sec. 4.2) and states that La‑Proteina achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance under that stricter criterion. The main text reasonably focuses on the model’s direct output for clarity.
- **“Lack of experimental validation” (Human Finder).** This is a generic criticism that applies to nearly all computational protein‑design papers. The paper evaluates using standard computational metrics accepted in the field; wet‑lab validation is beyond the scope of a methodological contribution at this stage.
- **“Insufficient analysis of computational requirements” (Human Finder).** The paper demonstrates scalability empirically and notes the architectural choice to omit triangular updates for efficiency. Detailed complexity analysis is not standard in this area and does not affect the validity of the results.

## Suggestions
- **Include a brief discussion of the synthetic‑data limitation.** A paragraph in the limitations or future‑work section acknowledging that training on AFDB may introduce biases and suggesting fine‑tuning on experimental structures (e.g., PDB) as a possible extension would address a legitimate concern without undermining the current contribution.
- **Expand the latent‑space analysis with simple quantitative probes.** Adding a small table or figure showing linear regression accuracy for predicting residue‑level properties (e.g., secondary structure, solvent accessibility) from the latent vectors would strengthen the claim that the representation captures biophysically meaningful information.
- **Clarify the motif‑scaffolding thresholds.** In the caption of Fig. 7 or the corresponding section, add a sentence noting that 1 Å/2 Å RMSD thresholds are commonly used in the literature for motif preservation and correspond to high‑fidelity structural matching.

---
**Overall Assessment:**  
This is a strong paper that makes a significant contribution to protein generative modeling. The partially latent representation is novel and well‑motivated, the empirical evaluation is thorough and convincingly demonstrates state‑of‑the‑art performance across multiple benchmarks, and the scalability to long sequences is a practical advance. The weaknesses are relatively minor and do not undermine the core claims. The paper is technically sound, clearly written, and likely to influence subsequent work in the field.
