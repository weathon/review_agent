**Summary**
This paper investigates whether LLMs can develop human-aligned semantic categories, using color naming as a testbed. The authors conduct two studies: (1) an English color-naming study to measure LLMs' alignment with human data and Information Bottleneck (IB) efficiency, and (2) a cultural evolution simulation via Iterated in-Context Language Learning (IICLL) to test if LLMs exhibit an inductive bias toward IB-efficiency. They find that larger instruction-tuned models are more aligned and efficient, and that only Gemini 2.0 recapitulates the full range of near-optimal IB-tradeoffs observed in humans.

**Strengths**
- The paper is well-written and the topic is important for understanding LLM-human alignment.
- The experiments are extensive, covering many state-of-the-art models.
- The authors provide a novel method (IICLL) for simulating cultural evolution in LLMs.

**Weaknesses**
- The paper's core claim that LLMs "evolve" human-aligned categories is overstated. The IICLL simulation is not evolution; it's just iterative prompting. The authors do not demonstrate any selection pressure, differential fitness, or inheritance of traits. This is a fundamental misrepresentation of the process.
- The authors claim that LLMs "exhibit a human-like inductive bias toward IB-efficiency." However, the IICLL simulation uses the same model at each iteration. This is not an inductive bias; it's a fixed model repeatedly applied. The results are a property of the model's prompt-following behavior, not a learning bias.
- The authors claim that "only a model with strongest in-context capabilities (Gemini 2.0) is able to recapitulate the wide range of near-optimal IB-tradeoffs observed in humans." This is a misleading comparison. The authors are comparing a single model's output to a distribution of human languages. The paper does not show that other models cannot produce a similar range under different conditions.
