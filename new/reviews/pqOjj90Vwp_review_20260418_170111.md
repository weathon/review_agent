## Summary

This paper establishes a logical expressiveness framework for arbitrary Graph Neural Networks expressible as General Aggregate-Combine Neural Networks (GACNNs). The central contribution is Theorem 3 and Proposition 5, which provide a recursive construction mapping any GACNN's computation structure to an equivalent set of FOC (first-order logic with counting quantifiers) formulas. As case studies, the framework is applied to characterize the logical expressiveness of eight prominent GNN families (Proposition 7), deliver a general method for homomorphism expressivity (Theorem 8), and derive WL upper bounds via variable counting (Proposition 9, Corollary 10).

---

## Strengths

- **Genuinely unified framework (Theorem 3, Proposition 5, Section 5):** The GACNN decomposition elegantly reduces arbitrary GNN computation to a DAG of AGG and COM operations, enabling a single recursive construction of equivalent logic sets. Prior work (Barceló et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2024) studied individual GNN classes separately with no common machinery; this paper provides for the first time a general method applicable to any GACNN with a single recipe.

- **Systematic characterization of 8 GNN families (Proposition 7, Section 6.1):** MPNNs, weak/strong Subgraph GNNs, NBFNet, Local 2-GNN, 2-FGNN, SEAL, and 2-GNN are all placed within a single logical framework. This is the first work to jointly cover graph-level, node-level, and link-level prediction models in a unified logical setting, and the derivations are transparent and mechanistic.

- **General homomorphism expressivity construction (Theorem 8, Section 6.2):** The procedure of removing negation and high-order counting from equivalent logic sets, then constructing graph patterns from variable structures, extends the line of Dell et al. (2018) and Zhang et al. (2024) from specific models to arbitrary GACNNs. The explicit claim that this resolves the Zhang et al. (2024) conjecture about the existence of homomorphism expressivity for GACNNs is significant if the proof is correct.

- **Clean WL bounding via variable counting (Proposition 9, Section 6.3):** Checking whether a GNN class is bounded by k-WL reduces to counting the maximum number of variables in the grammar of its equivalent logic set — an elegant and immediately applicable test demonstrated concretely for Subgraph GNN (weak).

- **Mechanistically interpretable expressivity gaps (Section 6.1, Remark):** The logical formulas in Proposition 7 make expressivity differences readable: weak Subgraph GNNs replace edge predicate E with a relational formula ψ(x,y); strong Subgraph GNNs further allow cross-source aggregation. The Bellman-Ford/Floyd analogy for NBFNet and 2-FGNN is a genuinely illuminating connection to classical algorithms.

## Weaknesses

### Minor

- **The cross-order comparison in Corollary 10 is underexplained.** Corollary 10 places MPNNs (order-1, single free variable) and NBFNet (order-2, two free variables) in a single expressiveness hierarchy. Section 3.1 defines expressiveness comparison as Φ₂ ⊂ Φ₁, but formulas of different orders classify different objects (nodes vs. node pairs) and are not directly comparable under subset containment. The paper's definition of "order" in Section 2 is careful, but the paper does not explain how a subset relationship between logic sets of different orders is made meaningful — presumably this comparison is at the graph-distinguishing-power level rather than per-node/per-pair, but this is never spelled out. A single clarifying paragraph would eliminate the ambiguity.

- **The "complete" framing in the title and abstract is slightly overambitious.** The paper itself acknowledges in Section 3.1 that the backward direction of Definition 2 (fourth bullet) is a finite-approximation guarantee rather than exact equivalence: "there does exist GNN models which is not captured by any logic formulas, and thus we relax the statement to be the fourth statement in Definition 2." This is a transparent and reasonable design choice, and the discriminating-power equivalence (fifth bullet) remains exact. However, the title's claim of a "complete" framework and the abstract's framing of the results as providing a one-to-one correspondence between GNNs and logic do not acknowledge this relaxation. The Limitations section (Section 7) discusses the GACNN scope restriction but does not mention this definitional caveat — which matters more conceptually for the "completeness" claim.

- **The scope of GACNN coverage is characterized only through examples.** The paper identifies Graphormer-GD as one architecture that falls outside the GACNN framework, but provides no general characterization of which GNN architectures can or cannot be expressed as GACNNs. GAT with learned attention weights, GNNs with random features, SignNet/BasisNet, and positional-encoding-based GNNs are all left unaddressed. Given that the paper presents GACNNs as a "general" framework, a clearer delineation of its boundary would significantly strengthen the generality claims.

### Trivial

- The `|` operator in the grammar notation (e.g., Theorem 3, Eq. for AGG case) is used without explicit definition of its precedence relative to `∧` and `¬`. The conventions should be stated once up front.

- **Extension to real-valued outputs:** The framework studies binary-output GNNs. A remark on what changes (or what approximate analogue holds) for real-valued outputs would clarify the connection to the empirical GNN expressiveness literature.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation from this consolidated review is the following: by grounding the backward direction of Definition 2 in a finite-approximation guarantee rather than exact equivalence, the paper adopts a definition of "equivalent logic set" that is weaker than the label implies but is nonetheless sufficient to support the discriminating-power hierarchy in Corollary 10 and the homomorphism expressivity in Theorem 8. This design choice, transparent in the paper but not foregrounded as a limitation, is precisely what allows the framework to be truly general — exact logical equivalence would fail for GNN classes containing non-FOC-expressible functions, while the finite-approximation relaxation preserves all the comparisons that matter in practice. This suggests that the right framing for the paper's contribution is not "complete logical equivalence" but "discriminating-power-complete logical characterization," which is arguably a cleaner and more defensible claim.
