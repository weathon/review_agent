## Summary
FaceLinkGen argues that the standard evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR) systems — measuring attack success by pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM) — does not capture identity-level privacy leakage. The authors present a straightforward distillation pipeline that trains a student model to align protected templates with an ArcFace embedding space, then uses Arc2Face to regenerate identity-consistent faces. Applied to three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace/ICCV23, MinusFace/CVPR24, FracFace/NeurIPS25), the method achieves ≥98.5% 1-to-1 verification accuracy and ≥96% regeneration success@5, at a total training cost under $1.60. The paper also provides soft biometric leakage analysis and a transfer evaluation on TIP-IM adversarial de-identification.

---

## Strengths

- **Compelling evaluation critique backed by concrete evidence (Table 1, Table 6):** Table 1 quantitatively demonstrates the PSNR/SSIM–identity decoupling: a CanFG-generated face achieves SSIM=0.841 and FS=0.008 while two different photos of the same person show SSIM=0.235 and FS=0.586. Table 6 directly juxtaposes FracFace's own claimed 68–100% protection rate (frequency-channel metric) against FaceLinkGen's near-zero residual protection (0.0–0.8% under identity-centric evaluation), providing the clearest single-figure argument in the paper.

- **Strong and clean 1-to-1 verification results (Table 3):** Verification accuracy of 0.99/0.98/0.92 on LFW across all three attacked methods requires no generative model and directly demonstrates that identity information passes through the PPFR transformation essentially intact. This is the paper's most unambiguous result.

- **Soft biometric leakage analysis (Section 9/Table 9) is a genuinely novel contribution:** Gender accuracy 92.5% (FracFace) vs. 94.9% (original), race accuracy 67.3% vs. 70.0%, and age MAE 4.74 vs. 4.58, all from a single-epoch fine-tune on FairFace — directly contradicting FracFace's public rebuttal claim of successful soft-biometric obfuscation. This is undervalued in the paper and is relevant to privacy regulators.

- **Minimal-resource stress test (Section 5.1):** 256 training images suffice for 98.7% linkage accuracy and 90.5% regeneration; training completes in 50 seconds. This serves as a tight lower-bound argument that the leakage is structural, not artifact of attacker resources.

- **Cross-API corroboration in the main oracle-access experiment (Table 5):** Agreement between Face++ and Amazon APIs on LFW oracle-access results rules out single-verifier bias for the paper's primary claims.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Unexplained and unresolved Amazon/Face++ discrepancy in Table 7 (constrained attack):** The constrained-attack results show Face++ at 94.6% regeneration success@5 but Amazon at only 44.7–57.0% — roughly a 2× gap. The paper's sole response is: *"The Amazon API is likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images."* This explanation is directly contradicted by the paper's own data: in Table 5 (oracle-access, same Arc2Face-generated images, same LFW set), Amazon and Face++ agree closely. If the Amazon API's strictness were a general property of the API, it would also diverge in Table 5. The more parsimonious interpretation is that the Gaussian high-pass proxy attack produces lower-quality identity embeddings than the oracle-access model, generating images that pass Face++ but are rejected by Amazon at twice the rate — meaning the constrained attack works substantially worse than Section 6 claims. The paper cannot simultaneously invoke Amazon agreement as validation in Section 5.3 and dismiss Amazon disagreement in Section 6 with a single unsupported sentence. Section 6's conclusion that "identity leakage remains strikingly persistent" for the constrained attacker may be an overstatement and should either be investigated with additional evidence or hedged accordingly. This does not affect the oracle-access results but weakens the constrained attacker scenario significantly.

### Minor

- **Missing Arc2Face upper-bound baseline:** The paper never reports what Face++ or Amazon pass rate Arc2Face achieves when given the *original* ArcFace embedding (not the student-model approximation). Without this upper bound, the reader cannot determine how much headroom remains between the attack's performance and perfect identity extraction. This matters for interpreting whether the distilled embeddings are high-quality or whether Arc2Face is simply robust to moderate embedding degradation.

- **"Essentially reaching theoretical maximum performance" claim is imprecise (Section 5.2):** The paper derives the upper bound of 0.88 from dataset noise estimates (9.3–13.0% from [32]) without directly verifying this noise level or reporting confidence intervals. Some attack pairs (e.g., MinusFace-to-MinusFace at 0.72) are noticeably below this bound. The claim should be qualified.

- **MinusFace systematically underperforms but is not analyzed:** MinusFace shows lower linkage rates, lower regeneration success, and lower soft-biometric recovery (race 0.569 vs. 0.663–0.673 for the other two methods). The paper does not investigate why. If certain design choices in MinusFace partially suppress identity leakage, this would be directly useful for future defensive design — the most practically actionable implication of the paper.

- **Abstract metric disclosure:** The abstract states ">96% regeneration success" without disclosing this refers to Success@5 (at least one of five generated images passes). Readers familiar with biometric verification benchmarks will expect a per-image rate. The distinction is meaningful; the per-image pass rate at the strictest threshold drops to 90–99%. The abstract should specify the metric.

### Trivial

- **Conv2D prepend layer under-described:** The paper adds "one additional 3×3 Conv2D layer prepended" for template channel compatibility but does not state how it is initialized or whether it is trained end-to-end. Given how short this description is, one clarifying sentence would suffice.

- **TIP-IM training data source not stated:** Section 8 evaluates TIP-IM but does not specify what paired data is used to train the student model for that method, since TIP-IM produces adversarially perturbed images rather than frequency-domain transforms.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A cosine similarity distribution plot comparing oracle-access vs. constrained-attack extracted embeddings would visually confirm or diagnose the Amazon/Face++ discrepancy and strengthen Section 6 considerably.
- Analysis of failure cases (the ~1–8% that fail regeneration) — whether failures concentrate on specific identities, template values, or image-quality issues — would clarify whether the attack has systematic blind spots.
- A proposed concrete identity-centric benchmark metric (e.g., standardized EER on a fixed evaluation split) would make the evaluation contribution immediately actionable for the PPFR community.
- A brief ablation showing the effect of removing the prepended Conv2D layer would verify the architecture choice is non-cosmetic.

## Removed Points

*These points are flagged for removal; treat them with caution.*

- **"Tautologically broken" framing critique (Harsh Critic, Issue 3):** The critic argues that attacking PPFR systems under oracle access is trivially guaranteed to succeed since the systems are designed to preserve identity. While the critic has a philosophical point, the paper's actual claim is about *evaluation adequacy*, not a hidden vulnerability — and it explicitly frames this in the abstract, methods, and conclusion. The paper is not claiming PPFR systems were secretly broken; it is demonstrating that the *metrics used to evaluate them* allowed authors to declare privacy while identity leaked. This is a legitimate and non-trivial contribution, not a tautology. *Removed as mischaracterization of the paper's claim.*

- **Requested confidence intervals/error bars for minimal-resource attack (Harsh Critic):** While multiple random seeds would strengthen Section 5.1, this is standard empirical practice in systems papers at ACM MM; single-run evaluation is the norm in this community for what is essentially a demonstration experiment. *Moved to nice-to-have.*

- **Query rate limit concern for oracle-access training data collection (Harsh Critic):** The paper notes that oracle access is achievable by reverse-engineering a local client or by controlling the conversion server. The 90K training pairs are accumulated offline — rate limits on verification endpoints are orthogonal to querying the conversion process. *Removed as scope creep.*

- **"Protection is tautological" argument about the distillation being trivial under oracle access:** Factually, distillation under oracle access does leverage the same identity signal the system preserves by design. The paper is transparent about this (Eq. 1–3). The contribution is showing that this suffices for complete identity extraction at trivial cost — important because prior work failed with stronger attacks. Not a valid weakness. *Removed.*

## Novel Insights

The paper's most underemphasized contribution is the soft biometric leakage analysis (Section 9): it turns the evaluation critique into a *regulatory critique*. If race, gender, and age can be inferred from PPFR templates with a single epoch of fine-tuning at near-original accuracy, then systems that have passed pixel-level privacy audits may nonetheless expose data explicitly regulated under frameworks like the Canadian Privacy Act. The combination of the distillation pipeline's negligible cost with this soft-biometric finding implies that the privacy guarantees of deployed frequency-domain PPFR systems need to be reconsidered not just at the academic evaluation level but at the compliance level. The paper treats this as a secondary section, but it may be the most actionable result for practitioners.

## Suggestions

1. **Resolve the Table 7 discrepancy.** Re-run the oracle-access images (same LFW set, same Arc2Face generation) through both APIs side-by-side to establish baseline agreement. If they agree on oracle-access images but diverge on constrained-attack images, that is direct evidence the constrained attack produces lower-quality identity embeddings and the stronger claims of Section 6 should be hedged. If they diverge even on oracle-access images, Face++ is not a reliable verifier for this use case and this affects the main results — though Table 3 (pure embedding-space result) and the Amazon results in Table 5 would remain unaffected.
2. **Add the Arc2Face upper-bound baseline** (Arc2Face on original ArcFace embeddings evaluated by Face++ and Amazon) to calibrate how close the distilled embeddings get to perfect identity extraction.
3. **Elevate the soft biometric leakage finding** to the abstract or a dedicated contribution bullet — it is the most novel part of the paper and the most relevant to the broader privacy community.
4. **Investigate MinusFace's systematically lower leakage rates** to determine whether its design partially mitigates the attack and identify what defensive properties it may have that PartialFace and FracFace lack.
