## Summary

FaceLinkGen demonstrates that frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems, which are typically evaluated using pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR/SSIM), do not prevent identity-level leakage. The paper introduces a distillation-based attack that trains a student model to map protected templates into an ArcFace-compatible embedding space, then leverages Arc2Face to regenerate identity-consistent faces. The attack is evaluated against three recent open-source PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), achieving near-complete regeneration success (Success@5: 97.9–100%) and high 1-to-1 verification accuracy (0.92–0.99) while the targets fail to reconstruct the original pixel content. The central, field-relevant finding is the large gap between claimed protection rates (up to 100% under frequency-channel metrics) and actual identity protection (0–1.5%) under identity-centric evaluation.

## Strengths

- **Table 1 cleanly establishes the core motivating insight.** CanFG-generated faces achieve PSNR 26.81 / SSIM 0.841 relative to the original yet near-zero face similarity (0.008), while two images of the same person have PSNR 10.44 / SSIM 0.235 but face similarity 0.586. This single table concisely invalidates the foundational assumption that pixel similarity implies identity protection.

- **Devastating empirical result in Table 6.** FracFace claims 100% protection under its frequency-channel metric; FaceLinkGen recovers identity with only 0.8–1.5% of images left protected. This is the paper's most striking quantitative finding and directly quantifies the gap between the two evaluation paradigms.

- **Attack simplicity strengthens, not weakens, the negative result.** The pipeline—cosine-similarity distillation loss, Antelopev2 backbone with one extra Conv2D, training on 90K CASIA images in under two hours at USD 0.80–1.60 cost—proves the vulnerability lies in the representations, not in adversarial cleverness. The minimal-resource variant (256 training images, 90.5% regeneration success; Section 5.1) further corroborates this.

- **External commercial API verification (Face++, Amazon) avoids same-model evaluation circularity.** Using Face++ (marketed as "financial-grade") ensures the success metric is not trivially gamed by the same ArcFace/Antelopev2 family used to construct the attack.

- **Cross-system linkage in Table 4** shows that identity leakage is not system-specific noise: MinusFace-to-PartialFace linkage reaches 77.5%, and all cross-system pairs substantially exceed random chance. This compounds the practical threat.

- **Rigorous dataset hygiene.** No identity overlap between CASIA-WebFace training split and test identities; LFW provides a cross-dataset distribution-shift test; TPDNE provides a synthetic contamination-free set. This careful design prevents trivial confounds.

- **Threat model is better motivated than much prior work.** Returning to the original Erkin et al. (2009) insider-adversary framing is appropriate; the paper's attacker assumes strictly less knowledge than Mi et al. [20] or Mi et al. [21], making the results conservative and the claim stronger.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **"Over 98.5% matching accuracy" in the abstract and conclusion is not uniformly supported across all evaluated systems and tasks.** Table 3 (1-to-1 verification on LFW) shows 0.99 / 0.98 / 0.92 for the three methods; FracFace at 0.92 does not clear the 98.5% bar. The more practically relevant 1-to-N closed-set linkage in Table 4 reports top-1 recall of 0.72–0.87, well below the headline number. The 98.5% figure most plausibly aggregates across different metrics (1:1 verification, minimal-resource experiments at 99.5%) without clearly stating this. The distinction between 1:1 verification and 1:N retrieval is not cosmetic—it represents fundamentally different attacker capabilities—and the paper's headline claim should be re-calibrated to be explicit about which task it refers to. This is the most important presentational issue.

- **Amazon API numbers in the constrained-assumption attack (Section 6) are substantially lower than Face++ but are downplayed.** Table 7 shows Amazon API at 0.447–0.570 (44–57%), whereas Face++ shows 0.946–0.963 for the same experiment. The text describes this discrepancy as the Amazon API being "likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images" without investigation. A ~50% success rate on a commercial second verifier significantly weakens the "strikingly persistent" characterization of the constrained attack. The paper should either investigate the source of this discrepancy or moderate its claims for that specific scenario.

### Minor

- **The Section 7 similarity analysis partially addresses, but does not fully resolve, whether the regeneration result is isolated to the template's contribution versus Arc2Face's generative prior.** The paper shows (Section 5.3 and Section 7) that template-derived embeddings have higher cosine similarity to the source image than to another image of the same person, which is meaningful evidence. However, a simple ablation—generating from teacher embeddings of the original images versus student embeddings extracted from templates—would definitively quantify what the distillation adds versus what Arc2Face supplies from any ArcFace-compatible embedding. The current evidence is suggestive but not definitive on this specific point.

- **Soft-biometric leakage (Section 9) is trained for a single epoch on FairFace and is not integrated into the main attack pipeline.** Table 9 results are striking (race 0.673 vs 0.700, gender 0.925 vs 0.949 from original), but one-epoch training on a single dataset leaves open questions about saturation and generalization. This section would benefit from brief ablation over training epochs or from being more clearly framed as a preliminary demonstration.

- **No variance reporting across runs.** Tables 2, 3, and 4 report deterministic numbers without confidence intervals or repeated trials. For near-saturation results this matters less, but for Table 4 (where differences between methods are a few points) and the minimal-resource results in Section 5.1 (where 256-image training outperforms 800-image training on linkage, which is surprising), variance bars would clarify whether the differences are reliable.

### Trivial
- The paper notes the 256-image run outperforms the 800-image run on linkage accuracy (98.7% vs 99.5% reversed?) in Section 5.1 without explanation; this minor inconsistency should be addressed.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Controls comparing template-derived embedding generation to teacher-embedding-derived generation from ordinary images would provide clean isolation of what the protected template contributes to regeneration success, fully addressing any skepticism about Arc2Face's role.
- Analysis of whether attack success varies with identity similarity in the original embedding space (i.e., whether the student mainly captures coarse demographic/lookalike structure or fine-grained person identity) would deepen the contribution.
- Testing whether stochastic or keyed conversion processes (as suggested in Section 10) break the distillation oracle would make the Future Directions section more concrete and actionable.
- Explicit failure-case analysis and nearest-neighbor confusion visualization would provide a more balanced and complete empirical picture.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most genuinely novel insight is meta-evaluative rather than technical: it demonstrates that a field's prevailing success metric (pixel-level reconstruction resistance) can be simultaneously maximized by a defense and largely irrelevant to the actual security property the field cares about (identity non-recoverability). The mechanism is clear—frequency-domain transformations preserve identity-discriminative information while destroying pixel texture—but the *severity* of the gap (100% claimed protection → 0–1.5% actual protection in Table 6) is striking even given the conceptual argument. The secondary insight, that a single generic high-pass filter proxy (without any method-specific knowledge) suffices to train a cross-method student model, concretely limits the design space for frequency-domain PPFR: any defense that preserves recognition utility while operating primarily on frequency channels is structurally vulnerable to this style of attack, independent of the specific channel selection algorithm.

---

## Suggestions

1. Revise the abstract and conclusion to precisely state which metric ("matching accuracy") refers to 1-to-1 verification (Table 3) and distinguish it from 1-to-N closed-set retrieval (Table 4). Quote both numbers clearly rather than conflating them under one headline.
2. Investigate the Amazon API vs. Face++ discrepancy in Table 7—whether it stems from stricter thresholds, sensitivity to synthetic images, or demographic differences—and report this analysis. If Amazon API is genuinely more conservative, this should be reflected in the strength of the constrained-attack claim.
3. Add a brief ablation (even a single table) showing regeneration success from teacher embeddings extracted from original images versus from student embeddings extracted from protected templates. This isolates the template-specific contribution and preempts the strongest residual objection to the regeneration headline.
4. Add repeated runs or bootstrapped confidence intervals for Table 4 and the minimal-resource Section 5.1 results to confirm that small differences across methods are stable.
