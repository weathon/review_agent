## Summary

This paper proposes *Convex Distillation*, a knowledge distillation framework that replaces non-convex blocks in a teacher network with convex student blocks using gated ReLU (GReLU) activations, trained via activation matching on unlabeled data. The central claims are: (1) convex students perform comparably to or better than non-convex students, especially in low-data and high-compression regimes; (2) specialized convex solvers (R-FISTA) converge 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than Adam; and (3) no post-compression fine-tuning on labeled data is needed.

---

## Strengths

- **Novel and well-motivated combination:** Applying convex neural network reformulations (Pilanci & Ergen 2020; Mishkin et al. 2022; Sahiner et al.) to the knowledge distillation setting is a fresh and principled idea not previously explored. The block-by-block "swap-and-infer" pipeline is clean and deployable.

- **Genuine advantage in low-data, high-compression regimes:** Figures 3 and 6 compellingly show that $S_{\text{convex}}$ significantly outperforms $S_{\text{non-convex}}$ when data is very scarce (e.g., 1 sample/class) or compression is aggressive—at 1 sample/class on CIFAR-10 convex distillation reaches ~70% while non-convex stays near ~50%.

- **Strong speed results via convex solvers:** Figure 5 (10-seed, binary classification on TinyImageNet) clearly shows R-FISTA and Approximate Cone Decomposition reaching target accuracy 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than Adam, with appropriate variance bands.

- **Label-free, no fine-tuning:** The activation-matching objective (Eq. 5–6) is genuinely label-free and does not require any post-compression supervised fine-tuning, which is a real practical advantage for edge deployments.

- **Polishing step (Section 4.3):** The Group Elastic Regression post-processing over a shared $W_2$ is a sensible and practical engineering contribution that recovers cross-output information lost by SCNN's one-vs-all decomposition.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None that invalidate the approach in every regime.*

### Major

- **Figure 7 contradicts the headline conclusion:** The conclusion states the method "performs at least as good as prevalent non-convex distillation methods." However, Figure 7—the most complete experiment (SCNN + polishing, CIFAR-10, varying training samples 10–100)—shows $S_{\text{non-convex}}$ consistently outperforming $S_{\text{convex}}$ across all sample counts (non-convex: ~82–88%, convex: ~75–85%). The paper's own explanation is: *"We believe that here convex distillation approach would outperform non-convex distillation if S comprised of CNN layers instead of linear layers."* This is speculative and does not resolve the contradiction. The conclusion must be qualified to reflect that the convex advantage is specific to extreme data-scarcity (≤25 samples/class) and high-compression settings, and that in relaxed settings non-convex catches up and surpasses.

- **Missing ablation: random features vs. convexity:** The convex student's CNN₁ layer is frozen (no gradients flow to it), meaning its gates are fixed random projections. Fixed random projections followed by a linear solver are precisely the *random features / random kitchen sink* family of approximations, which are known to generalize well in low-data settings independent of any convex neural network theory. The paper never ablates a fixed-gate *non-convex* student (CNN₁ fixed random gates, CNN₂ trainable with Adam), which would isolate whether the observed advantage is due to convexity or simply due to the fixed-gate random projection structure. This is a substantial gap: the attributed mechanism (favorable convex optimization landscape) cannot be distinguished from random features + least squares without this ablation.

- **No comparison to established KD baselines:** The paper discusses FitNets, contrastive distillation (Tung & Mori 2019), and label-free distillation (Raikwar & Mishra 2022) in related work but never includes them as experimental baselines. The claim of outperforming "standard non-convex distillation approaches" rests solely on a home-grown Adam-trained ReLU baseline, not any published KD method. This makes the comparative contribution difficult to assess.

### Minor

- **Theory-practice gap for CNN students:** Theorems 1–3 establish convexity for two-layer fully connected GReLU networks. The CNN-based student (Eq. 8) requires citing Theorem 3.3 in Sahiner et al., which the paper handles in a single remark. The convexity guarantee for the specific CNN+BatchNorm+AvgPool composition used in experiments is not derived; it is assumed by citation. For a paper whose central premise is convexity, this gap between the theoretical apparatus and the experimental architecture deserves a more complete treatment (even in an appendix).

- **Restricted to last block(s) only:** All experiments compress Block 4 (and sometimes Block 3) of ResNet-18. Since Block 4 accounts for ~70% of parameters, this special architecture property is doing most of the work. For architectures with more evenly distributed parameters, the practical benefit would be much smaller. The generality of the approach to arbitrary architectures (ViTs, Transformers, earlier/shallower blocks) is not demonstrated.

- **VWW results carry little evidential weight:** Table 2 shows accuracy differences of 0.52% (frozen backbone) and 0.05% (trainable backbone) between convex and non-convex heads. No variance or significance testing is reported. A 0.05% difference from a single run cannot be cited as evidence of anything.

- **Abstract overclaim:** The abstract states the method "achieves performance comparable to the original model without requiring any post-compression fine-tuning." Figures 3 and 4 show meaningful accuracy drops at high compression rates, and Figure 7 shows both distilled models trailing the original model. The abstract's phrasing is too strong.

### Trivial

- Conclusion section's unconditional "at least as good" claim should be qualified (directly fixable).
- No seed variance reported for CIFAR-10 main experiments (Figures 3, 4), while Figure 5 correctly reports 10-seed results.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Applying the method to ResNet-50 on ImageNet-1K or a ViT on a larger benchmark would substantially strengthen the practical impact claim.
- Wall-clock latency / memory measurements on an actual edge device (e.g., ARM Cortex-M, Raspberry Pi) would validate whether parameter savings translate to real inference gains.
- A UMAP or CKA similarity plot showing how closely $f_S(\mathbf{z})$ and $f_T(\mathbf{z})$ align across datasets and compression levels would clarify when activation matching succeeds or fails.

## Removed Points

*These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.*

- **"Wall-clock comparison is unfair (different code paths)"** – The paper already acknowledges in Section 5.2 that "the time budget for Adam is set slightly higher than that used by SCNN for a fair comparison." The asymmetry here actually disadvantages the convex method's reported speedup. Flagging this as a hardware fairness concern misreads the paper's conservative design.
- **"Different random seeds for CNN₁ produce different outputs; reproducibility is unclear"** – This is a trivial reproducibility nitpick. The architecture's use of frozen random gates is no different from standard practice in random feature models and does not undermine the main claims.
- **"Compressing only Block 4 is an unfair advantage"** – This is not an unfair comparison; it is an explicit design choice that the paper is transparent about (Table 1 explains exactly what block is compressed and why it constitutes 70% of the model). It is a scope limitation, not a fairness issue.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel insight from the combined reviews is the observation that the convex student's advantage in extreme low-data settings may be better explained by the *fixed random projection structure* of its frozen CNN₁ gates (the random features hypothesis) rather than by the theoretical properties of convex optimization per se. If this alternative explanation holds, the paper's contribution might be better framed as "random feature approximation + label-free activation matching" rather than as a triumph of convex neural network theory—an important reframing that the authors should address through targeted ablations.

## Suggestions

1. **Run the key missing ablation:** Train a student with fixed random CNN₁ gates but with Adam on CNN₂ (non-convex, random-gate). If this matches $S_{\text{convex}}$ performance, the advantage is attributable to fixed random projections, not convexity. If $S_{\text{convex}}$ still wins, the convex optimization argument is strengthened.
2. **Qualify the conclusion:** Replace "performs at least as good as prevalent non-convex distillation methods" with a regime-specific claim ("in extreme low-data settings [≤25 samples/class] and high-compression settings, convex distillation outperforms non-convex; in relaxed settings they converge or non-convex catches up").
3. **Extend CNN convexity derivation:** Add an appendix deriving (or precisely citing) the convexity guarantee for Eq. 8 with BatchNorm and AvgPool, to close the theory-practice gap.
4. **Include one established KD baseline** (e.g., FitNets) at the same compression factor to establish where the method stands relative to the published literature.
