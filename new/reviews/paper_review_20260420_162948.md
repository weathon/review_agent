# Review of ../paper.md

## Summary

FaceLinkGen demonstrates a critical gap in the evaluation of privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems: current methods measure privacy via pixel-level reconstruction resistance (PSNR/SSIM), yet identity information can be extracted despite the original image remaining "unreconstructable." The paper introduces a simple distillation-based attack that extracts identity embeddings directly from frequency-domain protected templates without pixel-level recovery, achieving over 98.5% linkage accuracy and 96% regeneration success across three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace). The work is validated through cross-dataset evaluation, commercial API verification (Face++, Amazon), and minimal-resource attacks (256 images, 50 seconds training).

## Strengths

- **Conceptual contribution clearly established**: Table 1 provides compelling empirical evidence that pixel-level metrics mismeasure identity privacy—CanFG-generated faces achieve high pixel similarity with originals (SSIM 0.841, PSNR 26.81) but near-zero identity similarity (FS 0.008), while two photos of the same person have low pixel similarity (SSIM 0.235) but high identity similarity (FS 0.586). This validates the paper's core claim that reconstruction resistance ≠ identity privacy.

- **Severe empirical vulnerability demonstrated**: The distillation attack achieves >98.5% linkage accuracy and >96% regeneration success@5 on all three frequency-domain PPFR systems (Section 5, Table 2). Most strikingly, Table 6 shows FracFace claims 100% protection under its frequency-channel disruption metric, yet FaceLinkGen achieves 99.2% regeneration success—demonstrating that channel protection does not prevent identity extraction.

- **Extensive validation**: The evaluation uses: (1) no dataset overlap between attack training (CASIA-WebFace) and target systems (PartialFace/FracFace are training-free; MinusFace trained on MS1Mv2); (2) cross-dataset testing on LFW; (3) independent commercial verification via Face++ and Amazon APIs (Tables 2, 5); (4) similarity distribution analysis independent of Arc2Face (Section 7).

- **Minimal-resource attack proves vulnerability is fundamental**: With only 256 training images and 50 seconds training, the attack achieves 98.7% linkage accuracy and 90.5% regeneration success (Section 5.1), demonstrating the vulnerability resides in the representation rather than attack sophistication.

- **Soft-biometric leakage quantified**: Section 9 shows protected templates leak race, gender, and age at rates approaching original-image accuracy (Table 9: race accuracy 0.673 vs. 0.700 for originals; gender 0.925 vs. 0.949), violating privacy frameworks like Canada's Privacy Act.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper's core claims are supported by substantial empirical evidence.

### Major
None. All identified limitations are explicitly acknowledged or addressed by the authors.

### Minor

- **ArcFace-Arc2Face loop**: The use of ArcFace embeddings with Arc2Face generator creates a favorable alignment (both operate in the same embedding space). While the paper addresses this concern in Section 5.3 and Section 7 by cross-validating with Face++/Amazon APIs and reporting direct embedding similarity, the high (>96%) regeneration rates should be interpreted as potentially inflated relative to settings where the PPFR system uses an incompatible embedding space. However, this does not undermine the core finding—identity information is clearly extractable.

- **Constrained attack theoretical grounding**: Section 6's high-pass filter proxy achieves >92% success but relies on an empirical observation ("they all preserve high-frequency information") rather than rigorous information-theoretic analysis (e.g., mutual information between template components and identity). The paper acknowledges this is specific to frequency-domain methods (line 219), but a stronger theoretical characterization would strengthen the justification.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Have

- **Variance across training subsets**: The minimal-resource attack (256 images) would benefit from reporting variance across multiple random subsets to strengthen claims about robustness.
- **Diversity analysis of regenerated faces**: Quantifying the diversity of Arc2Face outputs from the same embedding (e.g., LPIPS between samples) would clarify whether attackers can generate multiple views of the victim.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treated with caution:

- **Criticism that prior work "misled" simulated attack design**: The paper's framing is that the evaluation paradigm misdirected attack design, but this can be viewed as an emergent property of the field rather than deliberate obfuscation. The paper's claims are appropriately scoped.

- **Demand for different teacher/generator pairs**: The paper explicitly states (line 87) that "Other embedding models like FaceNet can also be used as long as there is a compatible generative model." The choice of ArcFace is justified by its widespread adoption and Arc2Face compatibility, and cross-validation with commercial APIs mitigates concerns about model-specific artifacts.

- **Criticism about CanFG/TIP-IM evaluation depth**: The paper explicitly labels these as "pilot evaluations" (line 34) and the abstract/conclusions appropriately scope claims to "suggest similar vulnerabilities may extend beyond the frequency-domain PPFR family." The preliminary nature is disclosed.

## Novel Insights

The paper exposes a fundamental misalignment between two privacy notions in PPFR: reconstruction privacy (attacker cannot recover original pixels) versus identity privacy (attacker cannot determine or regenerate the person's identity). The striking empirical result that 3-second-per-identity distillation extracts identity embeddings when sophisticated reconstruction attacks fail (Figure 3) suggests the field's evaluation paradigm has systematically underestimated vulnerability to service-provider adversaries. This insight transcends PPFR to any privacy-preserving system where utility requires machine-readable identity while humans perceive privacy in pixel space.

## Suggestions

- Clarify that regeneration success rates represent an upper bound contingent on embedding-space alignment, especially for practitioner audiences.
- Add a figure comparing embedding similarity distributions between (1) same-person different-image pairs, (2) protected template-original image pairs, and (3) different-identity pairs to visualize the information leakage directly.
