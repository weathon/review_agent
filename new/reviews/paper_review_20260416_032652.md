# Review of ../paper.md

## Summary

This paper challenges the prevailing evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR), arguing that pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR/SSIM) fail to capture identity-level privacy leakage. The authors introduce FaceLinkGen, an identity-centric attack using knowledge distillation to extract identity embeddings from protected templates and regenerate faces via Arc2Face. Experiments on three PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace) demonstrate >98.5% matching accuracy and >96% regeneration success, revealing a significant gap between pixel-level metrics and actual identity privacy.

## Strengths

- **Important conceptual reframing with concrete motivation.** The paper clearly articulates why pixel-level metrics misrepresent identity privacy, illustrated powerfully in Table 1 showing high SSIM/PSNR can coexist with low identity similarity while low pixel similarity can accompany high identity similarity. This addresses a genuine blind spot in PPFR evaluation.

- **Deliberate simplicity strengthens the core argument.** The attack uses standard knowledge distillation rather than complex adversarial optimization, demonstrating the vulnerability lies in the representation itself. The minimal-resource experiments (Section 5.1) achieving 90.5% regeneration with only 256 training images and under 50 seconds of training make this point convincingly.

- **Comprehensive multi-method evaluation.** The paper cross-validates results using Face++ and Amazon commercial APIs, embedding-space cosine similarity analysis (Section 7, Table 8), multiple datasets (TPDNE, CASIA-WebFace hold-out, LFW), and extends to constrained attacker scenarios (Section 6), adversarial de-identification (TIP-IM), and soft biometric leakage (Section 9). The cost analysis (~$0.80–1.60 per distillation) demonstrates practical feasibility.

- **Soft-biometric leakage findings have policy implications.** Table 9 shows race, gender, and age can be predicted from protected templates with accuracy near original images (e.g., 92.5% gender accuracy for FracFace vs. 94.9% for originals). This has clear regulatory significance given privacy frameworks like the Canadian Privacy Act explicitly protect such attributes.

- **Constrained-attacker experiment adds meaningful novelty.** Section 6 demonstrates vulnerability extends beyond oracle access using a generic high-pass filter proxy with only 30 paired samples, achieving 92%+ matching and 94%+ regeneration@5 on Face++. This shows the vulnerability is structural to frequency-domain representations, not dependent on full oracle access.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **No human perception study to validate identity-level claims.** The paper's core argument is that identity-level leakage (not pixel-level) is the correct privacy metric, yet regeneration success is measured entirely via commercial APIs and embedding similarity. As one reviewer of a related face reconstruction paper noted, "a perceptual user study should have been included...to quantify whether the reconstructed faces are perceived by humans to have the same identity as the original real faces." Without human judgment of identity consistency, the claim that "identity is recovered" rests on proxy metrics that may not align with actual identity perception. This is particularly problematic given the paper critiques prior work for misaligned metrics while potentially introducing a different misalignment.

- **Limited direct comparison with prior reconstruction attacks under identity-centric metrics.** The paper shows prior U-Net/StyleGAN attacks fail under pixel-level metrics but does not re-evaluate those same attacks using Face++ verification. This leaves ambiguity: is the gap due to the evaluation metric being wrong, or prior attacks being underpowered? A head-to-head comparison where reconstruction-based attacks are evaluated using the proposed identity-centric framework would clarify whether FaceLinkGen's contribution is the attack method or the evaluation paradigm. Without this, the methodological indictment of "reconstruction-centric evaluation" is partially circular.

- **ArcFace-Arc2Face-Face++ coupling raises potential inflation concerns.** The teacher model (ArcFace/Antelopev2), generator (Arc2Face designed for ArcFace embeddings), and primary verifier (Face++) all operate in correlated embedding spaces. While the paper cross-validates with Amazon API and embedding similarity, the Amazon results in constrained settings show substantially lower success (44-57% vs. 94%+ on Face++ per Table 7). The paper notes Amazon "may be stricter with AI-generated images" but does not investigate whether the Face++ results are inflated by embedding-space proximity. This affects confidence in the severity claims.

### Minor:

- **Linkage attack results notably lower than regeneration results.** Table 4 shows closed-set top-1 recall ranging from 72-86%, compared to >96% regeneration success. The paper attributes this to dataset noise (9.3-13.0%), but linkage is arguably more concerning in real-world scenarios (cross-database matching). This gap warrants deeper analysis of failure cases.

- **Limited scope of PPFR systems evaluated.** The primary evaluation covers three frequency-domain methods with pilot studies on TIP-IM and CanFG. Cryptographic/homomorphic-encryption-based PPFR methods (e.g., CryptoFace referenced in Section 10) are not tested, leaving open whether the vulnerability extends to all PPFR designs or is specific to frequency-domain representations.

- **Missing statistical variance measures.** All experiments report point estimates without standard deviation or confidence intervals across runs. Given stochastic sampling in regeneration (Eq. 3) and potential variance in minimal-resource experiments (256 images), reporting uncertainty would strengthen reliability claims.

### Trivial:

- **Some categorical claims in conclusions are overstated.** Statements like "de-ID or reversible face encryption methods *cannot* be used for PPFR tasks" are design choices rather than logical impossibilities; cryptographic schemes can provide PPFR-like properties with key-controlled reversibility.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Information-theoretic analysis of utility-privacy tension.** Even informal reasoning about mutual information between templates and identity, conditional on recognition utility being preserved, would clarify whether the vulnerability is fundamental or contingent on current designs.

- **Pareto analysis of privacy-utility tradeoff.** Sweeping perturbation strength and plotting recognition accuracy vs. attack success rate would directly inform the practical privacy-utility frontier for defense design.

- **Failure case characterization.** Analyzing the ~1-10% of identities where regeneration fails would reveal whether failures are systematic (extreme poses, occlusions) or random, informing both attack limits and defense opportunities.

- **Evaluation with alternative teacher/generator stacks.** Testing with FaceNet or other embedding models (beyond the stated claim that they "can also be used") would substantiate generality beyond the ArcFace ecosystem.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:

- **Threat model inconsistency claim (Harsh Critic #3).** The paper explicitly addresses oracle access in Section 2: "We return to first principles and evaluate PPFR against its intended adversary: a service provider with oracle access to the conversion process." The paper further explains how local conversion can be reverse-engineered or queried through the client. This is not a misreading but the paper's explicit threat model. The criticism misunderstands the paper's deliberate framing.

- **Claim that conclusions are "overstated relative to evidence" regarding narrow evaluation.** The paper consistently qualifies claims: Abstract states "on three recent frequency-domain PPFR systems"; Conclusion states "in the frequency-domain PPFR systems we evaluated." The broader implications about evaluation paradigms are presented as motivations for future work, not proven universal claims. This criticism overstates the overstatement.

- **Nitpick about "no principled argument (e.g., information-theoretic)" supporting generality.** While such analysis would strengthen the paper, its absence doesn't invalidate the empirical findings. This falls under nice-to-have rather than fundamental weakness.

- **Criticism about "circularity risk" being a "structural limitation."** The paper cross-validates with Amazon API and embedding similarity analysis, partially addressing this. The concern is valid but not as severe as portrayed.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most novel contribution is the conceptual reframing of PPFR evaluation from pixel-level reconstruction resistance to identity-level leakage assessment. While the attack pipeline itself combines standard techniques (knowledge distillation + diffusion-based generation), the insight that these methods suffice to break current PPFR systems is significant. The constrained-attacker experiment using a generic high-pass filter proxy demonstrates that frequency-domain PPFR methods share exploitable structural characteristics beyond their specific implementations. The soft-biometric leakage analysis provides empirical evidence that protected templates retain sensitive attribute information at near-original accuracy, which has implications beyond identity recognition alone.

## Suggestions

1. **Add human identity similarity judgments** on a subset of regenerated vs. original faces to validate that API pass rates correspond to actual perceived identity consistency.

2. **Re-evaluate prior reconstruction attacks (U-Net/StyleGAN from FracFace evaluation) using Face++ verification** to directly compare identity-centric attack effectiveness under the same metric.

3. **Investigate the Face++ vs. Amazon API discrepancy** in constrained settings—analyze whether differences stem from threshold calibration, AI-generated face detection, or genuinely different identity matching capabilities.

4. **Report variance measures** (standard deviation across seeds/runs) for key experiments, especially minimal-resource settings where training on 256 images may have high variance.

5. **Clarify scope limitations in abstract/conclusion** to emphasize findings apply to evaluated frequency-domain systems while framing broader paradigm implications as hypotheses for future validation.
