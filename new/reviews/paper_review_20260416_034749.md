# Review of ../paper.md

## Summary
This paper argues, convincingly for the evaluated systems, that privacy evaluation in frequency-domain PPFR should not rely primarily on pixel-level reconstruction metrics such as PSNR/SSIM. It introduces a simple distillation-based attack, FaceLinkGen, that maps protected templates into a standard face-embedding space and then performs linkage or identity-consistent face regeneration, showing very high attack success on three recent PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace). The main contribution is therefore less a novel attack primitive than a strong empirical and conceptual critique of reconstruction-centric privacy evaluation.

## Strengths
- **Important and well-supported core conceptual point:** the paper clearly demonstrates that failure to reconstruct original pixels is not the same as preventing identity leakage. This is argued both intuitively (Table 1, discussion in Sec. 1) and empirically through successful linkage and regeneration attacks.
- **Simple attack pipeline strengthens the paper’s message:** the method is intentionally uncomplicated—student distillation with cosine loss into an ArcFace-compatible space, followed by nearest-neighbor linkage or Arc2Face generation. Because the attack is not highly customized or optimization-heavy, strong success suggests the vulnerability lies in the template representation rather than in attack sophistication.
- **Strong empirical evidence on the tested methods:** across three PPFR systems and multiple datasets, the reported results are striking. In particular, the paper shows high 1-to-1 verification-style matching (Table 3 / nearby text), strong closed-set linkage (Table 4), and very high regeneration verification success with external APIs (Table 2, Table 5).
- **Threat model is thoughtfully argued:** Sec. 2 makes a persuasive case that the service provider/insider adversary with oracle access is a natural and important PPFR threat model, and the paper explains why this is more faithful to the original PPFR motivation than an external eavesdropper-only framing.
- **Good use of external evaluators:** using Face++ and Amazon face-comparison APIs reduces the most obvious same-model evaluation circularity and gives the regeneration claims more credibility than if success had been measured only with the same embedding family used in the attack.
- **Practical severity is demonstrated, not just claimed:** the paper reports low resource requirements, including a minimal-resource attack that still succeeds strongly on FracFace, making the attack more concerning in practice.
- **Useful extensions beyond the main result:** although preliminary, the transfer experiments to TIP-IM / CanFG and the soft-biometric leakage analysis help frame the broader lesson that identity-related information can persist even when the original protection target is not exact reconstruction resistance.

## Weaknesses
###: Fatal
None.

### Major:
- **The paper over-generalizes beyond what is directly established.** The evidence strongly shows that the evaluated systems—PartialFace, MinusFace, and FracFace—leak substantial identity information despite favorable reconstruction-based privacy claims. However, some wording in the abstract, introduction, and conclusion extends this into a broader indictment of the “prevailing evaluation paradigm” or frequency-domain PPFR more generally. The paper itself only evaluates three main PPFR methods plus preliminary extensions to TIP-IM and CanFG. The narrower claim is well supported; the field-wide framing is only partially supported.
- **The strongest regeneration claims are narrower than the presentation suggests because they depend on one particular embedding/generator stack.** The attack uses an ArcFace/Antelopev2-compatible student target and Arc2Face for generation. The paper does partly mitigate this concern by verifying with independent commercial APIs and by also reporting linkage results, so this does **not** invalidate the main identity-leakage claim. Still, claims about broad “identity recovery” or representation-level universality would be stronger if demonstrated with an additional embedding/generator family.
- **The paper does not sufficiently disentangle leakage specific to the PPFR template representation from leakage obtainable from simpler generic surrogates.** Sec. 6 is interesting because a generic high-pass proxy works well, but this cuts both ways: it reinforces that identity leakage exists, yet it also suggests that part of the vulnerability may stem from preserving generic high-frequency face cues rather than some deeper property unique to these PPFR methods. A stronger set of controls in the main experiments—e.g., blur residuals, edge maps, random frequency masks, or comparable simple transforms—would better quantify what is specifically problematic about these template designs.
- **Some headline numerical phrasing is imprecise.** The abstract claims “over 98.5% matching accuracy,” but the strongest linkage numbers in Table 4 are mostly around 0.72–0.88 for closed-set top-1 recall, whereas the near-99% figures correspond to 1-to-1 verification-style accuracy (Table 3 / related text). The paper should state this more carefully, because “matching accuracy” reads more broadly than what is actually shown.

### Minor
- **The constrained-assumption attack in Sec. 6 is promising but somewhat overstated.** The paper says the attacker has little knowledge, yet the proxy is motivated by observing that these methods share a high-pass-like visual essence. That is still a meaningful weaker-assumption result, but it is not a fully assumption-free attacker; the required prior intuition should be described more explicitly.
- **The paper gives limited mechanistic insight into why identity survives the protection transforms so effectively.** It establishes the existence of leakage well, but provides less analysis of which components of the representation carry identity information and why the distortion fails to suppress them.
- **The soft-biometric leakage analysis is suggestive rather than fully mature.** Table 9 is interesting, but the setup is relatively thinly specified and would benefit from stronger baselines or more analysis of whether leakage is inherited mainly through the distilled embedding rather than directly exposed by the templates themselves.
- **The non-frequency / de-identification extensions are preliminary.** The TIP-IM and CanFG experiments are useful auxiliary evidence, but they are not developed to the same standard as the core PPFR study and should be framed as exploratory rather than as equally strong secondary conclusions.
- **Some practical deployment assumptions in the threat-model discussion are asserted somewhat broadly.** For example, claims about reverse engineering, arbitrary query collection, or local-client access being generally realistic are plausible but not universal across all deployments.

### Trivial
- The paper would be easier to interpret with a cleaner separation between 1-to-1 verification, closed-set linkage, and regeneration metrics in the narrative around Tables 2–4.
- Showing more failure cases for regeneration would make the attack boundaries clearer, even though aggregate success rates are already strong.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Add one more embedding/generator pair to show the result is not unusually tied to the ArcFace/Arc2Face ecosystem.
- Add controls using simple surrogate transforms in the main setting, not only in Sec. 6.
- Include a more diagnostic analysis of what frequency/template components preserve identity.
- Clarify the constrained-attack prior knowledge more precisely.
- Report variability over repeated runs for the stochastic generation pipeline, especially for the smaller-data experiments.
- Broaden the non-frequency extension if the authors want to sustain broader claims beyond the three core PPFR methods.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.

- **“The paper needs to report PSNR/SSIM of regenerated faces vs originals or the core thesis is unverified.”** Removed because this misunderstands the paper’s central claim. The whole point is that identity leakage can occur **without** high pixel similarity, and the paper explicitly argues against treating PSNR/SSIM as the main privacy criterion. Reporting them could be a useful extra illustration, but their absence does not undermine the thesis.
- **Pure reproducibility complaints about missing optimizer schedules / hyperparameters / implementation minutiae.** The paper does give the main architecture choice, data split, datasets, and broad setup. More details would help replication, but under the review policy these are not core weaknesses unless they materially affect the conclusions.

## Novel Insights
A useful synthesis emerging from the paper is that Sec. 6 materially changes how one should interpret the core attack: the strongest message is not merely that a student can mimic a specific template transform with oracle access, but that these frequency-domain PPFR methods appear to preserve a fairly generic, visually recoverable identity-bearing subspace that is close to simple high-pass facial information. This means the vulnerability may be less about precise inversion of a proprietary transform and more about a deeper mismatch between “recognition utility preserved” and “identity privacy protected” in this design family.

## Suggestions
- **Tone down the broadest claims** so they precisely match the evidence: emphasize that the paper conclusively breaks the evaluated methods and strongly motivates identity-centric evaluation, rather than claiming to have settled the adequacy of the whole PPFR literature.
- **Clarify metric terminology in the abstract and results section,** distinguishing 1-to-1 verification from 1-to-N closed-set linkage.
- **Add at least one alternative embedding/generator pipeline** to test whether regeneration success remains high outside the ArcFace/Arc2Face pairing.
- **Promote Sec. 6 into a stronger controlled analysis,** by comparing the PPFR templates against simpler image transforms in the same experimental protocol.
- **Deepen the explanatory analysis** of what template properties preserve identity and why simple high-pass cues suffice.
- **Frame TIP-IM/CanFG and soft-biometric results as exploratory extensions** unless more thorough experiments are added.

## Score and Decision
**Assessment by axis:**  
- **Originality:** Good. The attack ingredients are not individually new, but the paper’s reframing—from reconstruction privacy to identity leakage evaluation in PPFR—is genuinely important and timely.  
- **Importance:** High. If the evaluated PPFR systems can be broken this easily under a realistic insider threat model, that matters significantly to the community.  
- **Claims support:** Strong for the evaluated methods; weaker for the broadest field-level claims.  
- **Experimental soundness:** Good overall, with compelling multi-dataset and external-verifier evidence, but lacking some controls and breadth needed for stronger universality claims.  
- **Clarity:** Generally good conceptually, though some numerical phrasing and scope framing should be tightened.  
- **Community value:** High. Even if not the final word, this paper should influence how PPFR privacy is evaluated.
