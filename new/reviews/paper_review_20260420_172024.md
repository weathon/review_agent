## Summary

FaceLinkGen demonstrates that pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM) are inadequate for evaluating identity privacy in frequency-domain PPFR systems. Through a simple knowledge distillation pipeline that extracts identity embeddings from protected templates and feeds them to Arc2Face for regeneration, the paper shows that three recent PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace) achieve >97% regeneration success and >92% 1-to-1 matching accuracy — despite claiming strong protection under pixel-level metrics. The attack works with as few as 256 training images in under 50 seconds, and a constrained variant using only a generic high-pass filter achieves strong results without oracle access to the conversion process.

## Strengths

- **Compelling and important conceptual contribution**: Table 1 crystallizes the disconnect between pixel-level and identity-level privacy with a single, powerful example — CanFG-generated faces achieve SSIM=0.841 with originals but identity similarity FS=0.008, while two photos of the same person have SSIM=0.235 but FS=0.586. This cleanly demonstrates that pixel-distortion metrics can be fundamentally misleading for privacy evaluation.

- **Strong experimental results across multiple settings and systems**: Table 2 shows regeneration Success@5 above 97.9% and Pass@1e-5 above 90% across three PPFR methods and three datasets. Table 4 confirms linkage attacks achieve 72–86% closed-set recall vs. 88% for original embeddings. The constrained attack (Table 7) achieves >92% matching and >94% regeneration Success@5 on Face++ without oracle access, and the cross-method linkage results (Table 4) demonstrate cross-system template linkage.

- **Minimal resource requirements demonstrate fundamental vulnerability**: Section 5.1 shows the attack works with 800 images and 50 seconds of training (97.0% pass rate at FAR=1e-5, 99.5% linkage), and with only 256 images (90.5% regeneration success). At an estimated cost of $0.80–1.60 per method, the vulnerability is clearly structural rather than requiring sophisticated optimization.

- **Well-reasoned threat model**: Section 2 carefully argues for the service-provider-as-adversary model, identifies how prior work drifted toward weaker assumptions, and shows that the paper assumes *less* knowledge than prior attacks (no architecture knowledge). The argument about local model reverse-engineering and the impossibility of separating conversion and recognition trust is well-constructed.

- **Effective cross-verification strategy**: Table 5 cross-verifies regeneration results with Amazon's face comparison API on 700 LFW images showing close agreement with Face++ for the main experiment. Section 7 provides an API-independent analysis via cosine similarity in embedding space, showing extracted embeddings have higher similarity to source images than to other images of the same person.

- **Generalization beyond frequency-domain PPFR**: Table 8 shows the attack recovers identity from TIP-IM (adversarial de-identification, linkage accuracy 0.997 vs. 0.000 under original model) and CanFG (non-frequency, non-adversarial), demonstrating the vulnerability is not limited to the frequency-domain family.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Missing Arc2Face ceiling/baseline for regeneration success**: Table 2 reports regeneration success rates of 90–100%, but without knowing Arc2Face's regeneration success rate from *original* (non-distilled) ArcFace embeddings, it is difficult to calibrate how much identity information is truly lost in the distillation process. If Arc2Face achieves 99.5% from original embeddings, then 90% Pass@1e-5 from student-extracted embeddings reflects meaningful degradation; if Arc2Face achieves 93%, the near-identical performance suggests the student is almost a perfect inverter. Either way, the interpretation changes. This does not invalidate the core argument — the linkage results and absolute success rates already demonstrate extractability — but it does leave the most headline-grabbing numbers (">96% regeneration success") without proper calibration. The paper would be significantly stronger with this ceiling reported.

- **Face++ vs. Amazon API gap in the constrained attack is acknowledged but not investigated**: Table 7 shows the constrained attack achieves 94.6–96.3% regeneration Success@5 on Face++ but only 44.7–57.0% on the Amazon API — a >38 percentage point gap. The paper briefly attributes this to Amazon being "more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images" (Section 6), but does not investigate this further. Since the main experiment (Table 5) shows Face++ and Amazon in close agreement on LFW, the gap may reflect genuinely lower-quality regenerated faces in the constrained setting rather than Face++ being permissive. However, the possibility that Face++ systematically overestimates identity match for AI-generated faces deserves discussion, as it could affect the headline regeneration numbers across all experiments. Section 7's cosine similarity analysis partially addresses this (as an API-independent check), but does not directly resolve the question of whether Face++ is miscalibrated for AI-generated faces.

### Minor

- **Preliminary evaluation on de-identification systems**: Table 8's TIP-IM evaluation uses only 2,082 images from 408 identities, which is small relative to the main experiments. CanFG's vulnerability is also evaluated under oracle access, which CanFG's own authors acknowledge. The paper appropriately frames these as "pilot evaluations" with "preliminary results," but the generalization claims beyond frequency-domain PPFR would be stronger with larger-scale evaluation.

- **Constrained attack lacks sensitivity analysis for hyperparameters**: Section 6 shows strong results with a generic high-pass filter, but does not report how sensitive results are to kernel sizes, blur strengths, or augmentation strategy. Since the paper argues the vulnerability is general (one model attacks all three methods), understanding the robustness of this claim to hyperparameter choices would strengthen the argument.

- **The gap between linkage (72–86%) and regeneration (90–99%) success is not analyzed**: This gap could indicate that Arc2Face compensates for embedding noise by generating identity-consistent faces from imperfect inputs, meaning regeneration metrics may overstate the fidelity of extracted identity information. A brief discussion of why these metrics diverge would improve interpretability.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Reporting Arc2Face's regeneration success rate from original ArcFace embeddings as a ceiling/baseline, which would contextualize the 90–100% rates in Table 2.
- Failure case analysis: showing and analyzing the ~1–10% of regeneration failures (demographic patterns, pose dependence, etc.) to assess whether failures are systematic or random.
- Comparison between direct ArcFace embedding inversion (without any protection) and protected-template extraction, to quantify how much extra difficulty the protection actually imposes on an attacker.

## Novel Insights

The most insightful observation across reviews is the under-appreciated tension between the linkage and regeneration metrics. Linkage (72–86% closed-set recall, Table 4) and regeneration success (90–99%, Table 2) tell quantitatively different stories about how much identity information survives. This gap likely reflects Arc2Face's ability to generate identity-consistent faces even from noisy/imperfect embeddings — meaning regeneration success may somewhat overstate the fidelity of the extracted information. The cosine similarity analysis in Section 7 partially addresses this, but the paper would benefit from explicitly discussing why these two attack vectors yield different success rates and what each reveals about the nature of the information leak.

## Suggestions

- Report Arc2Face regeneration success from original ArcFace embeddings as a ceiling baseline, to calibrate whether the 90–100% rates represent near-perfect extraction or a mix of extraction quality and Arc2Face's robustness to input noise.
- Run at least a subset of the main (oracle-access) regeneration experiment through the Amazon API, not just on LFW but specifically for regeneration (Table 5 appears to cover verification accuracy and regeneration pass rate, but confirming this explicitly on all datasets would resolve the Face++ vs. Amazon question more thoroughly).
- Include a brief analysis of failure cases in the regeneration attack — which identities fail and whether patterns exist — to assess practical severity and potential demographic bias.
