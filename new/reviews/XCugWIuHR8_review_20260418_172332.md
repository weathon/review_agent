## Summary

The paper introduces a knowledge distillation method that compresses deep networks by replacing non-convex blocks (e.g., in ResNet-18) with convex neural network architectures using gated ReLU activations. The convex student blocks are trained via activation matching (no labels required) against the teacher's intermediate representations, and after distillation, the compressed block is swapped in without any fine-tuning. The approach leverages the convex reformulation theory of Pilanci & Ergen, with practical adaptations including a polishing step to address the one-vs-all limitation of existing convex solvers for multi-output settings.

## Strengths

- **Label-free distillation without fine-tuning**: The activation matching objective (Equation 5–6) has no dependency on labels, and all evaluations report test accuracy immediately after swapping the distilled block—no labeled fine-tuning is performed. This is a genuinely useful property for deployment scenarios where labeled data is scarce (Sections 4.1, 5.1–5.4).

- **Convex distillation has clear advantages in extreme data-scarcity regimes**: Figure 3b (CIFAR-10 with 100 samples/class) and Figure 6 (1, 10, 25 samples/class on CIFAR-10) demonstrate that convex distillation meaningfully outperforms non-convex distillation when training data is extremely limited. This is a real, well-demonstrated advantage.

- **Order-of-magnitude speedup from convex solvers**: Figure 5 shows R-FISTA and Approximate Cone Decomposition reach ~75% test accuracy in ~10 seconds on TinyImageNet binary classification, while Adam-based non-convex training requires ~100+ seconds. This directly validates the computational advantage of convex optimization.

- **Polishing step is a practical engineering contribution**: Section 4.3 and Figure 2 describe a creative method to overcome SCNN's one-vs-all limitation for vector outputs, enabling information sharing across output dimensions via group elastic regression (Adelie).

- **Systematic experimental progression**: Experiments progress from convexity validation under the same optimizer (Section 5.1), to exploiting convex-specific solvers (Section 5.2), to improving multi-output solutions via polishing (Section 5.3), to testing beyond distillation (Section 5.4)—each building on limitations identified in the prior section.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Figure 7 directly contradicts the paper's central claim about parity with non-convex distillation**: Section 5.3 states "convex optimization based distillation performs at least as good as with Adam-based non-convex block distillation." The Conclusion echoes this: "distillation via convex architectures performs at least as good as prevalent non-convex distillation methods." However, Figure 7's caption explicitly describes Non-Convex Acc starting at ~82 and reaching ~88, versus Convex Acc starting at ~75 and reaching ~85—a consistent 5–7 percentage point gap at every sample count. The written claim is directly contradicted by the paper's own data. The authors note that SCNN is constrained to 2-layer MLPs (rather than CNN layers) in this experiment, and speculate that CNN-based convex distillation would close the gap—this is speculative and does not justify the categorical "at least as good" claim. This is the paper's most complete experiment under relaxed resource constraints, and it undermines the headline conclusion.

- **The comparison in Section 5.1 is confounded by architectural differences between S_convex and S_non-convex**: The convex student (Eq. 8) is a three-layer network with a boolean mask and a 1×1 projection, while the non-convex student (Eq. 7) is a standard two-layer CNN. The boolean mask acts as structured sparsification/regularization with no counterpart in S_non-convex. The paper also adjusts S_non-convex's filter count to match parameter counts (line 227), which reduces its convolutional features. Any performance difference in Figures 3 and 4 could be partially attributable to (a) the implicit regularization of binary masking, (b) the extra 1×1 projection layer, or (c) the reduced filter count in the non-convex baseline—not solely the optimization landscape. No ablation isolates convexity from architecture. That said, the paper does use the same optimizer (Adam) for both, which partially addresses optimizer confounds.

- **Misleading claim about CNN₁ contributing "no effective parameter to the model size"**: Line 159 states "it does not contribute any effective parameter to the model size." While true that CNN₁ has no gradient flow (its parameters are frozen), these weights still require storage and FLOPS at inference time. Calling them "non-contributing" is physically misleading. The paper also acknowledges "Alternatively, we can mask out CNN₂(z) using fixed boolean masks," which is more honest about the role of CNN₁ as just a source of gating patterns—but this alternative formulation is not what is implemented or evaluated.

### Minor

- **Gap between theoretical convex reformulation and practical implementation**: Theorems 1–3 require gates sampled from the activation-pattern set D_X of the data. In practice, CNN₁ is frozen (likely at random initialization), making the construction closer to a random-features model. The paper cites Kim & Pilanci (2024) showing O(n/d·log n) Gaussian gates suffice for convergence, which partially addresses this—but the connection between the theoretical guarantees and the specific CNN₁ initialization used is never made explicit.

- **Limited scale of empirical validation**: All experiments use ResNet-18 (11M params) on SVHN/CIFAR-10/TinyImageNet, or MobileNet V3 on Visual Wake Words. Modern compression challenges involve much larger models. While the small-scale experiments are sufficient to make the conceptual point, they limit the practical impact of the claims for real-world edge deployment.

- **Table 2 differences are marginal and lack statistical validation**: The Visual Wake Words experiment reports 0.52% (frozen) and 0.05% (trainable) accuracy differences without variance, confidence intervals, or significance tests. The 0.05% difference has no evidential value. The conclusion that "convex NN architectures are good candidates for DNN applications beyond just distillation" is not well-supported by this data.

- **Section 5.2 compares different optimizer classes**: R-FISTA (a specialized convex solver) vs. Adam (a general-purpose optimizer) conflates algorithmic efficiency with the benefit of convexity. While Section 5.1 partially addresses this by using Adam for both, the speed claims in Section 5.2 go beyond what the same-optimizer comparison can support.

### Trivial

- The introduction's citation of NP-hardness for NN training (Yao 1992, Bartlett & Ben-David 1999) concerns worst-case results; in practice SGD on CNNs converges reliably. This oversells the practical value of convexity but is a framing choice rather than an error.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation with matched architectures**: Train S_non-convex with the same three-layer boolean-mask structure as S_convex but allow gradient flow to the mask parameters. This would isolate the effect of convexity from architecture and help clarify the contribution.
- **Larger-scale validation**: Demonstrating on at least one larger teacher model (e.g., ResNet-50 on ImageNet) would substantially strengthen the practical impact.
- **Gate quality analysis**: Comparing random vs. data-driven gating patterns would clarify whether the theoretical convex reformulation connects to the practical implementation.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting insight emerging from cross-reading the reviews and paper is the tension between the paper's strongest empirical result (convex distillation excels in extreme data scarcity, Figures 3b and 6) and its overclaimed generalization (convex distillation matches or exceeds non-convex in all regimes). If the paper had framed its contribution around the data-scarcity advantage—where the convex landscape genuinely appears to help rather than the architecture confound—rather than making a blanket claim, it would be considerably stronger and more honest. The polishing experiment (Figure 7) is particularly revealing: it shows that when resource constraints are relaxed and the convex solver is forced into a limited 2-layer MLP structure, the non-convex approach wins, suggesting the convex advantage in Section 5.1 may be partly architectural rather than purely optimization-theoretic.

## Suggestions

- Revise the main claim in Section 5.3 and the Conclusion to accurately reflect Figure 7's data: convex distillation excels in data-scarce regimes but underperforms non-convex when resources are more available, at least with the current 2-layer MLP convex formulation.
- Clarify what "effective parameter" means when discussing CNN₁—distinguish between trainable parameters and inference-time parameters/FLOPS.
- Add one architectural ablation (e.g., training a non-convex student with the same boolean-mask structure) to isolate convexity from architecture in Section 5.1.
- Report mean ± std over multiple seeds for Table 2 to establish whether the tiny differences are meaningful.
