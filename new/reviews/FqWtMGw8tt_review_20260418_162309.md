## Summary

KnowData is a synthetic image-text data generation framework for fine-tuning CLIP models. It integrates structured knowledge from ConceptNet, unstructured knowledge from Wikipedia via ColBERT-based RAG, and LLM (GPT-3.5) expansion/refinement to produce richer text descriptions, which are then used to prompt text-to-image diffusion models (primarily Stable Diffusion). The resulting synthetic dataset is used to fine-tune CLIP for zero-shot classification across 9 datasets, with the paper claiming state-of-the-art improvements of up to +11.23% on DTD and +11.47% on CIFAR-100 over the strongest prior synthetic-data method.

---

## Strengths

- **Incremental ablation isolating each knowledge source (Table 5):** All ablation variants use the same fine-tuning protocol, so the stepwise contribution of ConceptNet, GPT expansion, Wikipedia RAG, and image diversity tricks is clearly isolable. The gains on DTD (48.11→57.33) and EuroSAT (54.19→63.86) are consistent across all components, directly supporting the core hypothesis that explicit knowledge enrichment helps.

- **Explicit multi-source knowledge outperforms pure LLM generation (Table 5):** The PureGPT baseline (53.84% on DTD) substantially underperforms the full pipeline (57.33%), demonstrating that grounding in external structured and unstructured knowledge sources provides genuine value beyond implicit LLM knowledge — which is the paper's central claim and is internally well-supported.

- **Better data scaling law than base prompts (Figure 3):** The gap between KnowData and BP widens with increasing data size (from ~0.4% to ~6% on DTD at 60K images), indicating that knowledge-enriched data scales better and becomes more valuable at higher data volumes.

- **Qualitative fine-grained discrimination evidence (Figure 2):** The analysis of three similar dog breeds (Weimaraner, Rhodesian Ridgeback, Vizsla) concretely illustrates how knowledge-enriched prompts cause Stable Diffusion to generate visually distinct images, providing a specific and mechanistically believable causal story for why knowledge helps.

- **Benefits transfer to larger models (Table 6):** KnowData improves ViT-L/14 by +5.12% and ViT-G/14 by +1.66% over pretrained CLIP, showing gains are not artifacts of small model capacity.

- **Transparent data generation pipeline (Table 1):** Exact prompt and image counts at every stage are reported, enabling resource estimation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Confounded fine-tuning protocol invalidates headline baseline comparison.** The most consequential flaw is in the comparison between KnowData and He et al. (2023) — the primary same-category competitor. As stated in Section 3.4 and Section 4.1, KnowData fine-tunes the last 31 layers of CLIP-ViT-B/16 and the last 44 layers of CLIP-RN50, while He et al. fine-tunes only the classification head. Table 5 is the smoking gun: the Base Prompt (BP) configuration with KnowData's deep fine-tuning already achieves 48.11% on DTD — already beating He et al.'s 44.92% using head-only fine-tuning (Table 2). On ImageNet-Val, BP+deep (69.04%) nearly matches He et al. (69.16%). This means a trivially generated dataset, under KnowData's deeper fine-tuning, already matches or beats the leading SOTA competitor. The paper's justification — "we believe that knowledge-enhanced data contains more information... and merely fine-tuning the classification head is insufficient" (Section 3.4) — is circular: the protocol choice is justified by the assumption that KnowData data is better, which is the very claim under evaluation. The paper does not run He et al.'s synthetic dataset under the deep fine-tuning setup, making it impossible to cleanly attribute any gains to the knowledge pipeline versus the fine-tuning regime. The headline figures (+11.23% on DTD, +7.99% on DTD RN50) are plausibly mostly due to the fine-tuning depth, not the knowledge pipeline. This missing 2×2 ablation (data quality × fine-tuning depth) is the experiment the paper's central comparison claim requires.

- **CLIP score metric for image quality assessment is tautological (Table 3 / Section 4.2).** The paper explicitly states (Section 4.1): "the text embeddings for the CLIP score here are obtained from our knowledge-enabled prompts, rather than being derived from class names combined with each dataset's CLIP template." This means images are evaluated against the very prompts that generated them. By construction, richer prompts produce images that score higher against those same richer prompts. The metric measures prompt-image consistency within KnowData's own distribution — any method that generates longer, richer prompts would achieve the same improvement in this metric. The conclusion that "knowledge can improve the reliability of synthetic data" (Section 4.2) does not follow from this measurement; only downstream accuracy can serve as a reliable quality proxy.

### Minor

- **Incomplete Shipard et al. (2023) comparison.** Shipard et al. is the only other method that also fine-tunes with synthetic images (same experimental category), yet results are only present for CIFAR100 and EuroSAT in Table 2, with many cells marked "-". The paper attributes this to reproduction difficulty, which is understandable, but it means the comparison to the most methodologically similar baseline cannot be fully assessed. The paper should at a minimum note that Shipard et al. fine-tunes from random initialization while KnowData uses pretrained encoders, which likely explains the large gap.

- **Table 2 vs. Table 5 discrepancy.** Table 2 reports KnowData at 57.51% on DTD (ViT-B), while Table 5 reports 57.33% for CN+WRAG+GPT+Div. Both appear to use 60k DTD images. The small discrepancy (0.18%) is likely from different random seeds or checkpoint selection, but it is unexplained and mildly confusing.

- **VQA evaluation is extremely narrow.** The VQA v2 evaluation in Table 7 covers only yes/no questions ("Are these...") on the mini-eval split (Section 4.2). This is a highly restricted and unrepresentative slice of VQA v2; the claim that "KnowData-finetuned CLIP improves downstream task performance on VQA" should be scoped accordingly. The WinoGround improvements are small in absolute terms (group score: 7→8 over 400 samples) with no variance reporting.

### Trivial

- **Section 3.1 heading-text inconsistency.** The subsection heading reads "Extracting *unstructured* knowledge from knowledge graphs" but the text immediately discusses ConceptNet structured knowledge. The correct heading should read "Extracting *structured* knowledge from knowledge graphs."

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Apply KnowData's deep fine-tuning protocol to He et al.'s synthetic dataset (and apply He et al.'s head-only protocol to KnowData's dataset).** This 2×2 experiment would cleanly disentangle whether the knowledge pipeline or the fine-tuning depth is responsible for performance gains. Without it, the headline comparison is uninterpretable. This is the most actionable path to making the paper's core claim credible.

- **Report compute and dollar cost of the full pipeline.** Generating 480k knowledge-enriched images involves ConceptNet API calls, two rounds of GPT-3.5 API calls per description, ColBERT retrieval, and diffusion model inference. For 1–2% marginal gains on ImageNet-Val over a simpler pipeline, cost-benefit transparency would help practitioners evaluate adoption.

- **Use a neutral, fixed text anchor for CLIP score computation.** Switching to the standard OpenAI CLIP templates (class name templates) as the text anchor for all ablation stages in Table 3 would make the reliability metric meaningful and interpretable across methods.

- **Show side-by-side image comparisons with He et al.'s T5-enhanced images** for the same classes. The qualitative argument is compelling within KnowData's own ablation, but visual evidence against the main baseline would strengthen the claim.

## Removed Points

*These points are flagged for removal — treat them with caution.*

- **Reviewer claim: "KnowData requires CLIP score filtering using the same CLIP that is fine-tuned."** The paper is clear (Section 3.3) that filtering uses a pre-existing CLIP model before fine-tuning, and the fine-tuning is performed after filtering. The feedback loop concern is speculative and not clearly supported.

- **Reviewer claim: Table 4 data generator comparison uses only 60k images while main results use 480k.** This is accurate (stated in Section 4.2), but comparing data generators at 60k is a reasonable ablation choice — the main claim is about relative ranking of generators, which is likely robust to scale. Not a meaningful weakness.

- **Strength Finder: "Substantial accuracy gains of +11.23% on DTD over SOTA" (from Table 2).** This strength is directly undermined by the verified Major weakness showing that the BP baseline with KnowData's own fine-tuning protocol already surpasses He et al. on DTD, making the +11.23% primarily attributable to the fine-tuning regime, not the knowledge pipeline. This strength cannot stand in the face of the confound.

- **Strength Finder: "Image quality metrics corroborate accuracy gains" (Table 3).** This is undermined by the verified tautological CLIP score concern — it does not constitute independent evidence of quality.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation is the data scaling asymmetry shown in Figure 3: knowledge-enriched synthetic data exhibits a widening advantage over base-prompt data as data volume increases, suggesting that richer prompt diversity delays saturation. This is an interesting empirical phenomenon that, if the fine-tuning confound were controlled for, could be a meaningful contribution independent of the headline accuracy numbers. The qualitative mechanism in Figure 2 — that explicit knowledge (coat color, nose color) injected into prompts causes diffusion models to generate visually distinguishable images for semantically similar classes — is a specific, verifiable, and practically useful insight for the synthetic data generation community.
