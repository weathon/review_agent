## Summary

This paper proposes **General Aggregate-Combine Neural Networks (GACNNs)** as a unified abstraction decomposing diverse GNN layers into aggregation (AGG) and combination (COM) operations, then derives a systematic correspondence between arbitrary GACNNs and equivalent first-order logic sets with counting quantifiers (FOC). The central result (Theorem 3 / Proposition 5) constructs these logic sets recursively from the computation graph. As applications, Proposition 7 characterises the equivalent logic sets of seven prominent GNN classes, Theorem 8 provides a general method for homomorphism expressivity, Proposition 9 enables WL upper-bound estimation by variable counting, and Corollary 10 asserts a hierarchy of expressivity among those models. The paper claims to resolve a conjecture from Zhang et al. (2024) on when homomorphism expressivity exists for GACNNs.

---

## Strengths

- **Unifying framework with genuine generality.** Decomposing MPNNs, Subgraph GNNs, NBFNet, 2-FGNNs, and SEAL under one GACNN formalism is a non-trivial organisational contribution. Where prior works (Barceló et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024) each studied individual model families in isolation with incompatible proof techniques, this paper provides a single recursive machinery.

- **Proposition 7 as a practical reference.** The explicit equivalent logic sets for seven GNN classes, derived systematically from Proposition 5, provide the field with a readable, unified "expressivity dictionary." The comparison of NBFNet (single-source pattern) vs. 2-FGNN (multi-source pattern) through their logic set structure is genuinely illuminating.

- **Bridge to homomorphism expressivity (Theorem 8).** The construction procedure converting logic sets to homomorphism expressivity via removal of negations and high-count quantifiers is a natural and elegant bridge between two distinct expressivity languages. Resolving the open conjecture of Zhang et al. (2024) about when homomorphism expressivity exists is a concrete, cited contribution.

- **Proposition 9 (WL upper bounds via variable counting)** is a simple, practical tool. Recovering SEAL < 4-WL with a single variable count, rather than a dedicated proof, demonstrates the framework's utility.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Equality claims in Corollary 10 lack lower-bound justification within the paper's own framework.** Corollary 10 asserts equalities such as "FGNN = 3-WL" and "Subgraph GNNs (weak) = NBFNet." Proposition 9 and variable counting establish *upper bounds only* (model ≤ k-WL). Equalities require a corresponding lower bound—that the model can realise every formula with k variables (i.e., distinguishes every pair that k-WL separates). For "MPNNs = 1-WL" and "FGNN = 3-WL" the lower bounds are classical results from prior work (Xu et al., 2018; Maron et al., 2019), but the paper never explicitly cites them in this context; the text implies these equalities follow from the framework. For "Subgraph GNNs (weak) = NBFNet" the equality appears to be a *new* claim with no lower-bound argument at all in the main text (and the appendix is not available for review). Even if the appendix contains proofs, the main text misrepresents which direction is actually proved by the paper's own tools.

- **Cross-arity comparison in Corollary 10 is undefined as stated.** Definition 2 and Section 3.1 define expressivity comparison via logic-set inclusion: "M₁ is more expressive than M₂ iff Φ₂ ⊂ Φ₁." But Φ is defined over formulas of a fixed order k. Subgraph GNN (weak) produces node representations φ(x) (order 1), while NBFNet produces node-pair representations φ(x,y) (order 2). Their respective Φ sets consist of formulas over different variable tuples; subset comparison is not defined across orders. The paper does not define a common projection or graph-level readout reduction before making these cross-arity comparisons. This is a conceptual gap in the headline result, not merely a missing proof. Corollary 10's chain "Subgraph GNNs (weak) = NBFNet < Subgraph GNNs (strong)" mixes order-1 and order-2 models without explanation.

### Minor

- **FGNN vs. 2-FGNN naming inconsistency.** Proposition 7 characterises "2-FGNN" (a pair-level model), yet Corollary 10 writes "FGNN = 3-WL." Whether FGNN and 2-FGNN denote the same model or different ones is never clarified in the main text. The equivalence of 2-FGNNs to 3-WL is a known result (Maron et al., 2019); if this is what is intended, it should be cited directly.

- **Auxiliary logic set Ψ for Subgraph GNNs and SEAL is incompletely characterised.** Proposition 7 introduces ψ(x,y) and ψ(x,z,y) as auxiliary logic sets but does not state whether these constitute a separate equivalent logic set in the sense of Definition 2, or merely a notational convenience. For SEAL, the ternary ψ(x,z,y) is used to define the primary binary formula φ(x,y); the relationship between ψ and the overall equivalent logic set Φ of SEAL is not established.

- **Limitation section (Section 7) does not bound the gap accurately.** The abstract describes the framework as covering "arbitrary GNN architectures" while Section 7 narrows this to GACNNs only. The Contributions paragraph (Section 1) is more careful ("provided they can be represented through a series of combination and aggregation operations"), but the abstract overclaims. This is a minor presentation issue but risks misleading readers.

- **No experiments.** For a purely theoretical paper this is acceptable by field norms, but the gap between idealised (arbitrary AGG/COM) and practical (sum/mean/max + MLP) settings is not discussed at all. Whether the logical characterisations of Proposition 7 accurately predict practical discriminating behaviour is unaddressed. Synthetic counterexamples demonstrating the strict inequalities in Corollary 10 would partially compensate.

### Trivial

- The paper is from an independent researcher and has no institutional affiliation; presentation is generally readable but could be improved with clearer notation separation (e.g., always distinguishing Φ from Ψ explicitly).

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation synthesised from both reviews is that the GACNN framework yields a *constructive* characterisation of logical expressivity (rather than an existence argument), by assigning each node in the computation graph to a corresponding logic-set fragment via Theorem 3. This constructive character is what enables both the homomorphism expressivity bridge (Theorem 8) and the WL variable-counting method (Proposition 9) to fall out as corollaries—a cleaner cascade than prior piecemeal treatments. However, the framework's headline expressivity-comparison result (Corollary 10) remains underspecified because it conflates models of different output arities without a defined common basis for comparison, which is a structural gap that future work should address by either restricting to a fixed-arity task (e.g., graph classification after readout) or providing an explicit cross-arity reduction.

## Suggestions

1. **For Corollary 10 cross-arity comparisons:** Explicitly define graph-level expressivity via Proposition 6 for all models involved, then state that the hierarchy is over *graph-distinguishing power* (not node-tuple-level discrimination). This would resolve the conceptual ill-definedness of comparing node-level models (Subgraph GNN weak) to node-pair-level models (NBFNet).

2. **For equality claims:** Explicitly cite the lower-bound results from prior work (Xu et al., 2018 for MPNNs = 1-WL; Maron et al., 2019 for FGNN = 3-WL) and provide at least a proof sketch or reference for the new equalities (Subgraph GNNs (weak) = NBFNet).

3. **Resolve the FGNN/2-FGNN naming:** Either consistently use "2-FGNN" throughout, or define "FGNN" separately if it refers to a distinct model.

4. **Clarify the auxiliary set Ψ:** State explicitly whether ψ constitutes its own equivalent logic set in the sense of Definition 2, or whether it is a helper inside Φ.
