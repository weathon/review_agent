## Summary

This paper challenges the prevailing evaluation paradigm in frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR), which equates privacy with resistance to pixel-level reconstruction (PSNR/SSIM). The authors demonstrate that preventing pixel-level reconstruction does not prevent identity leakage by introducing FaceLinkGen, a distillation-based attack that extracts identity embeddings from protected templates and regenerates identity-consistent faces without recovering original pixels. The attack achieves >98.5% matching accuracy and >96% regeneration success on three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), fundamentally exposing a gap between pixel-level metrics and actual identity privacy.

## Strengths

- **Important and well-motivated conceptual reframing.** The paper's central argument — that pixel-level reconstruction failure ≠ identity privacy — is both correct and consequential. Table 1 effectively illustrates this disconnect: a CanFG-generated face has high pixel similarity (SSIM 0.841) but near-zero identity similarity (FS 0.008) to the original, while two photos of the same person have low pixel similarity but high identity similarity. This is a strong, simple demonstration that the community's dominant evaluation practice is flawed.

- **Strong linkage attack evidence.** The linkage attack (Table 4) is the paper's cleanest and most persuasive result. Using cross-image, identity-level matching on disjoint identities, the attack achieves 73–86% top-1 recall across cross-method template matching, approaching the empirical ceiling of 88% for original-to-original matching. This directly demonstrates that identity information persists in protected templates in a form that is learnably extractable, independent of any generative model.

- **Cross-verification design.** Using both Face++ and Amazon commercial APIs for regeneration verification — rather than the same ArcFace model used in the attack pipeline — is a methodologically sound choice that reduces evaluator-dependent bias. The cosine similarity analysis in Section 7 further provides model-independent evidence of identity leakage.

- **Intentional simplicity as a strength.** The attack is a straightforward distillation + generation pipeline. As the authors state: "If a standard procedure suffices, the vulnerability is in the representation." The minimal-resource experiments (Section 5.1, 256 images, <50 seconds training, still achieving 90.5% regeneration) underscore that this is not an attack requiring sophisticated optimization — it reveals a structural vulnerability.

- **Useful extension to soft biometrics and de-identification.** Section 9 shows that gender, race, and age remain recoverable from protected templates, and Section 8 extends the attack to the adversarial de-identification method TIP-IM and non-frequency-domain CanFG, broadening the relevance of the findings beyond frequency-domain PPFR.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **Overgeneralization beyond the oracle threat model.** The paper's headline framing and conclusion claim that "current frequency-domain PPFR systems do not provide the level of identity privacy their pixel-level evaluation results imply" (paraphrased from abstract/conclusion), but the experiments support this primarily under the specific insider/oracle-access threat model. While the constrained-adversary experiments (Section 6) extend this somewhat, they are under-specified. The paper's own future directions section acknowledges that cryptographic/key-based hardening could block the attack, which implicitly concedes that the vulnerability is not inherent to all PPFR but rather to unkeyed, oracle-accessible transformations. The paper should more carefully scope its claims to the tested threat model rather than presenting a universal indictment of PPFR evaluation.

- **Regeneration results are partially entangled with the Arc2Face generator.** The student model is trained to map protected templates into ArcFace-family embeddings, and then Arc2Face (which is specifically designed to invert ArcFace embeddings) regenerates faces. While cross-verification with commercial APIs partially addresses this concern, it does not fully disentangle whether the high regeneration success reflects how well identity is preserved in the template or how effectively Arc2Face can synthesize identity-consistent faces from any embedding. The linkage results (which do not depend on any generator) provide the stronger and cleaner evidence. The paper would be strengthened by re-running prior reconstruction attacks (U-Net, StyleGAN from FracFace/MinusFace) under the same identity-centric (Face++) metric to show that they genuinely fail, rather than relying solely on Figure 3's visual evidence.

- **Insufficient quantitative comparison with prior reconstruction attacks under the proposed identity-centric metric.** The paper's thesis is that pixel-level attacks fail while identity-centric attacks succeed, but it only provides visual evidence (Figure 3) that prior attacks fail, without quantitative identity-level evaluation of those baselines. Directly re-evaluating the U-Net/StyleGAN attacks from FracFace and MinusFace under Face++ verification would make the paradigm comparison definitive rather than illustrative.

### Minor:

- **The constrained-attack experiment (Section 6) is under-specified.** The protocol for selecting the 30 paired samples, hyperparameter selection for the high-pass filter proxy, and threshold tuning specifics are not detailed enough for the claims to carry the same weight as the main experiments. This section reads as a promising extension rather than a rigorous result.

- **Near-ceiling results without variance reporting.** The regeneration success rates are consistently >96% across methods and datasets, making statistical significance less of a concern, but the linkage results (Table 4) show more variation and would benefit from standard deviations across runs or different train/test splits.

- **Minimal-resource experiment lacks clarity on identity separation.** Section 5.1 mentions training with 256 images but does not specify how many identities are represented or confirm that evaluation identities are fully disjoint from training. If identities overlap, the results could partly reflect memorization.

- **The TIP-IM and CanFG evaluations (Table 8) are pilot-level.** These are labeled as "pilot evaluations" by the authors, but the claim that TIP-IM shows a "structural limitation of adversarial de-identification as a privacy mechanism" goes beyond what a single-method pilot evaluation can support.

### Trivial:

- The hashing analogy for linkage attacks (Section 4.1) is rhetorically useful but technically imprecise — face identity spaces are open-set and continuous, unlike finite hash input domains.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Testing simple defenses (e.g., template noise addition, quantization, or distillation-resistant training) against FaceLinkGen would clarify whether the vulnerability is in the specific tested implementations or truly fundamental to any utility-preserving PPFR. The paper's suggestion of cryptographic approaches is forward-looking but untested.
- Failure-case analysis: characterizing the 3–10% of regeneration failures (what makes certain identities harder?) would inform future defenses.
- Evaluating prior reconstruction attacks (U-Net, StyleGAN) under the same Face++ identity metric to provide a direct quantitative comparison between old and new evaluation paradigms.
- A human perception study for regenerated faces would strengthen the claim that identity is truly recoverable, not just machine-verifiable, though commercial APIs are a reasonable proxy.

## Removed Points

- **Claims that the attack "assumes oracle access" is a fundamental limitation.** The paper explicitly and carefully argues in Section 2 that oracle access is the correct and intended threat model for PPFR, and that alternative threat models (external attacker) are weaker and less relevant. The paper's threat model is well-justified and consistent with the original PPFR design intent. This is not a weakness but a deliberate, reasoned design choice.

- **Demands for evaluation against cryptographic/keyed PPFR methods.** The paper's scope is evaluating existing frequency-domain PPFR methods under their stated threat model. Testing against fundamentally different defense paradigms (cryptographic, keyed) is outside scope and would not change the paper's core claim about evaluation inadequacy.

- **Criticism that the paper should include a human perception study.** The paper uses commercial Face++ and Amazon APIs with financial-grade security thresholds — stricter than typical human face matching. The linkage attack establishes identity leakage without requiring any face generation at all. A human study would be a nice addition but is not necessary for the claims.

- **Formatting/style nitpicks** (minor notation issues, section numbering artifacts from PDF parsing) are removed per instructions.

- **Concerns about "missing related works"** (citing specific references not in the paper) are removed per instructions, as independent verification of referenced works is not possible.

- **Demand for error bars/confidence intervals** on near-ceiling results (>96%). For results this close to ceiling, variance is unlikely to change any conclusion. Single-run evaluation is the norm in this community for large-scale benchmarks.

- **Criticism that the method has limited novelty because distillation + generation is standard.** The paper's contribution is precisely that a standard procedure suffices — the novelty is in the evaluation paradigm argument, not the attack sophistication.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most novel insight is the decoupling of pixel-level privacy from identity-level privacy, demonstrated via the concrete observation that frequency-domain PPFR methods preserve high-frequency identity-bearing information that is trivially extractable via distillation. The constrained-attack experiment (Section 6) reveals a shared structural vulnerability — that all three methods essentially perform high-pass filtering — which is a more fundamental observation than any single attack pipeline. This suggests that any PPFR scheme whose template preserves discriminative frequency content is inherently vulnerable, and that evaluating privacy via reconstruction metrics systematically overestimates protection.

## Suggestions

- Re-run the U-Net/StyleGAN reconstruction attacks from FracFace and MinusFace under Face++ verification to provide direct quantitative evidence that pixel-level attacks fail on the same identity metric where FaceLinkGen succeeds. This is the single most impactful addition that would make the paradigm-shift argument watertight.
- Scope the conclusion more precisely: the paper establishes that pixel-level metrics are inadequate for the specific class of unkeyed, oracle-accessible frequency-domain PPFR systems. This is already a significant and actionable result that does not require overgeneralization.
- Add standard deviations for linkage results across multiple train/test splits in Table 4.
