# Review of ../paper.md

## Summary

The paper studies privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) methods that transform face images into frequency-domain “protected templates.” It argues that the dominant evaluation practice—measuring resistance to pixel-level reconstruction (PSNR, SSIM, “channel protection”)—fails to capture identity-level privacy. The authors propose FaceLinkGen, a simple distillation-based attack that learns to map protected templates into a standard face-embedding space and then performs (i) template/face linkage and (ii) face regeneration via Arc2Face; they show very high linkage and regeneration success against three recent frequency-domain PPFR schemes, and provide extensions to one adversarial de-identification method (TIP-IM) and one non-frequency PPFR/de-ID method (CanFG).

## Strengths

- **Clear conceptual message with strong empirical support.**  
  The paper makes a crisp, well-argued point: “no pixel-level reconstruction” does not imply “no identity leakage.” It backs this with multiple forms of evidence:
  - Table 1 shows pixel metrics vs identity similarity can be misaligned.
  - High 1-to-1 verification accuracy between templates and faces on LFW (0.99, 0.98, 0.92 in Table 3) and high closed-set linkage in Table 4 demonstrate that the evaluated PPFR templates behave like strong identity descriptors.
  - Soft-biometric prediction from templates (Table 9) is close to that from raw images, further underscoring that templates retain rich identity-related information.

- **Simple but powerful attack design.**  
  FaceLinkGen is intentionally minimal:
  - A student network is trained on (template, original image) pairs to mimic a frozen teacher (ArcFace) embedding (Eq. 2).
  - The recovered embedding is used directly for linkage, or fed to Arc2Face for regeneration (Eq. 3).
  That such a generic distillation pipeline succeeds with very high accuracy and very low resource cost (under two hours on a single A6000, or even 50 seconds with 800 images in §5.1) strongly suggests the vulnerability lies in the representations, not in attack sophistication.

- **Thorough empirical exploration across systems and datasets.**  
  The core evaluation covers:
  - Three prominent, recent frequency-domain PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), all with released code.
  - Multiple datasets: CASIA-WebFace hold-out (no ID overlap), LFW (distribution shift), and a synthetic “this person does not exist” (TPDNE) set for regeneration.
  - Both identity-linkage (template–template and face–template) and regeneration attacks, using both an open FR model (ArcFace/Antelopev2) and commercial APIs (Face++, Amazon) as verifiers (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5).

- **Compelling evidence of “easiness” of the attack.**  
  The minimal-data experiments on FracFace (§5.1) are particularly damning:
  - With ~800 images, they still get 97.0% regeneration success at FAR 1e−5 and 99.5% linkage.
  - With only 256 images, they obtain 98.7% linkage and 90.5% regeneration success.
  This shows the mapping from templates to ArcFace embeddings is extremely learnable, even in a tiny-data regime, consistent with the claim that identity information is barely obfuscated.

- **Thoughtful threat model discussion focused on the insider.**  
  Section 2 carefully re-anchors PPFR to its original motivation: the main adversary is an untrusted service provider who receives protected templates. The authors:
  - Critique threat models that focus on wiretapping and external attackers instead of insiders, rightly pointing out those concerns are better handled by TLS.
  - Argue that “model secrecy” (e.g., CanFG placing all security in a hidden conversion model) is unrealistic against an adversarial provider, since oracle access via client or reverse engineering is hard to prevent.

- **Extension beyond reconstruction-centric PPFR.**  
  The paper usefully extends the same pipeline to:
  - An adversarial de-ID method (TIP-IM), showing that while TIP-IM fully protects against a particular FR model (ArcFace), a new student model aligned to a different embedding space can undo much of that protection (Table 8).
  - CanFG, a generative PPFR/de-ID hybrid, showing its deterministic, secret-model-reliant scheme is vulnerable if an attacker can get oracle access (§2, §8, Table 8).
  - Soft biometric leakage on FairFace (Table 9), which highlights that race/gender/age are still easily inferable from templates, contradicting some prior claims that such attributes are obfuscated.

- **Low and explicitly quantified attack cost.**  
  The paper is careful to report actual resource/cost estimates (GPU time, approximate dollar cost per identity and per training), which underscores how accessible these attacks are in practice.

Overall, within its chosen ecosystem (ArcFace-like embeddings and commercial verifiers) and threat model (oracle insider with public data), the paper convincingly shows that three influential frequency-domain PPFR methods do not deliver the level of identity privacy suggested by reconstruction- and frequency-metric based evaluations.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The paper is coherent, technically sound within its scope, and the main empirical claims are well supported by the text.

### Major

- **Overstated generality of the conclusions beyond what is empirically tested.**  
  Experiments are on three specific, high-pass-like frequency-domain PPFR systems plus two de-ID schemes. Yet the text often speaks broadly about “frequency-domain PPFR systems” and “current frequency-domain defenses” (e.g., “This paper shows that pixel-level reconstruction metrics may not adequately capture identity-level leakage in the frequency-domain PPFR systems we evaluated.” (Conclusion), “current frequency-domain defenses do not adequately address this” in §6, and generalized language in §8–§9 about de-identification being “almost guaranteed to be attacked”).  
  Given:
  - The tested systems all share a similar design pattern (retain high frequencies, distort or drop low frequencies), and
  - The attack is tailored to mapping these templates into ArcFace space and then leveraging Arc2Face,
  the strongest claims should be explicitly scoped to “the evaluated frequency-domain PPFR methods and closely related designs.” As written, there is a risk of overgeneralizing to all PPFR and de-ID paradigms, including architectures or cryptographic schemes not examined here.

- **Evaluation tightly coupled to one embedding/generation ecosystem.**  
  The core attack and evaluation live in a single ecosystem:
  - Teacher: ArcFace/Antelopev2 (or closely related embedding).
  - Student: trained to mimic that space from templates (Eq. 2).
  - Generator: Arc2Face, specifically designed to invert ArcFace embeddings.
  - Verifiers: commercial systems (Face++, Amazon) whose architectures and training may be correlated with ArcFace-like representations, plus ArcFace itself for cosine similarity (§7).  
  This yields very strong evidence that *ArcFace embeddings are recoverable from templates and inverted effectively by Arc2Face*, but the paper frequently extrapolates to broader identity leakage against “any” strong FR system. There is:
  - No experiment where the student is aligned to one embedding space and evaluation is done with a *different* backbone without retraining, nor
  - Any evaluation of other generative back-ends that aren’t Arc2Face-like.  
  This does not invalidate the result—recovering ArcFace embeddings is already serious—but it narrows its generality: the work most cleanly supports “PPFR templates remain vulnerable so long as they can be aligned to a standard embedding space with an available generator,” rather than “PPFR templates are universally broken for all identity extractors.”

- **Comparative claims about “breaking” prior defenses are somewhat stronger than the evidence.**  
  The paper rightly points out that reconstruction-centric defenses and metrics are misleading, and Table 6 compares FracFace’s reported “Protection (%)” (frequency-channel disruption) versus FaceLinkGen’s regeneration success. However:
  - FracFace’s original protection metric is defined specifically for its own reconstruction attack setup (U-Net, StyleGAN) and its own channel-disruption notion; here the authors overlay an identity-regeneration metric that is significantly stricter and of a different type.
  - The paper does not reproduce FracFace’s own U-Net/StyleGAN attacks side-by-side on the same conditions to show “FracFace’s metric claims near 100% protection under its own U-Net/StyleGAN evaluation, yet we achieve near-total identity regeneration under our evaluation.” Parts of that case are described qualitatively (§1), but the comparative experimental protocol could be clearer and more explicit.  
  Similarly for TIP-IM (§8, Table 8): TIP-IM is designed to dodge one FR model (ArcFace), and the authors show that another model (their student) can still recover identity from protected images. This is a meaningful and important result about model-specificity of adversarial de-ID, but calling TIP-IM “almost completely undermined” in general can be read as broader than what is actually shown: its stated guarantee is “fool a given deployment model,” not “provide generic privacy against arbitrary new models.”

- **Threat-model narrative occasionally blurs insider and external attacker assumptions.**  
  The main threat model (oracle, curious/malicious service provider with ability to generate paired (image, template) data) is clear and very reasonable, and under that setting the results are strong. In §6, the paper examines a “constrained” attacker with:
  - Only 30 paired image–template samples (for validation), but
  - Access to large corpora of unprotected faces and the ability to construct a generic high-pass proxy (via Gaussian blur and augmentations), and
  - The ability to train a single student model and then apply it across all three PPFR schemes.  
  This is an insightful experiment, but it still assumes substantial capabilities—especially possession of unprotected faces drawn from a distribution close to the PPFR input, and the visual observation that PPFR outputs resemble high-pass filtered images. Some parts of the exposition suggest that “identity extraction remains feasible even under constrained attacker assumptions” in a way that may be read as covering generic template-only external adversaries; in reality, the constrained setting still leverages substantial side information. The conclusions around “more realistic external attackers” should be narrowed and clearly tied to these assumptions.

### Minor

- **Technical novelty is incremental; contribution is mainly conceptual and empirical.**  
  The attack uses standard components—distillation to a public embedding, then generative inversion—to make an important conceptual point. There is no fundamentally new learning algorithm or architectural advance. This is acceptable if the positioning is appropriately “we instantiate a simple but powerful attack to highlight a flawed evaluation paradigm,” not “we propose a novel model-inversion technique.”

- **Limited exploration of mitigation strategies.**  
  Section 10 outlines promising ideas: key-based PPFR, formal cryptographic schemes, inversion of de-ID objectives, identity-agnostic geometric cues, etc. However, there is no empirical investigation of even simple defenses (e.g., noise injection or randomized transformations in the template space, adversarial training against such a student). Given how compellingly the paper demonstrates failure of existing schemes, the lack of any practical mitigation exploration slightly weakens the actionable value for practitioners.

- **Constrained-attacker proxy method is structurally tailored to high-pass PPFR.**  
  In §6, the attacker uses a Gaussian-blur-based high-pass filter as a universal proxy because “the output templates of these systems share a common visual essence: they all preserve high-frequency information while obfuscating low-frequency information.” This is a fair observation for the three studied methods, but the text could be clearer that this proxy attack is specific to PPFR schemes with that characteristic (as the authors briefly acknowledge when they note it “does not apply to non-frequency methods such as CanFG”). The generalizability of the proxy to other frequency-domain or learned transformations is not examined.

- **Some claims about soft-biometric leakage are qualitative.**  
  Table 9 quantitatively shows that race and gender accuracy and age MAE from protected templates are close to those from original images, which is a solid result. However, parts of §9 and §10 discuss regulatory implications and design principles in fairly broad strokes. Those are valuable as perspective but could be rephrased as hypotheses or directions rather than as definitive statements about all PPFR/de-ID systems.

### Trivial

- Minor parsing/typo artifacts due to PDF extraction are irrelevant to the actual paper (e.g., stray numbers in the middle of paragraphs); these should not be treated as weaknesses and can be ignored.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Evaluate FaceLinkGen when the teacher and generator are *mismatched* (e.g., student mimics FaceNet; generation via Arc2Face, or vice versa), or with an alternative generative model that accepts a different embedding. This would better characterize how tightly the attack depends on ArcFace–Arc2Face compatibility.

- Run at least one open-set linkage evaluation to complement the closed-set 1-to-N tests: e.g., queries from LFW, gallery from CASIA-WebFace, with a rejection option. This would give a more practical sense of attack performance under realistic database search settings.

- Include small-scale negative controls for commercial APIs: e.g., how often do Face++/Amazon wrongly accept regenerated faces against *wrong* identities, under the same thresholds and data, to validate that the FARs quoted by the services remain meaningful in this setting.

- Provide an explicit, formal definition (even if informal/operational) of “identity-level privacy” and “identity leakage” in this context, which could guide future work on provable guarantees.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution. They either misinterpret the paper or rely on external assumptions we cannot verify here.

- **“PPFR designers assumed ‘no reconstruction ⇒ no identity leakage’ as a core guarantee.”**  
  The harsh review asserts that prior PPFR work *claimed* that blocking pixel reconstruction suffices to prevent identity leakage. The paper here argues that this assumption underlies evaluation practice but does not provide verbatim evidence that earlier works claimed full identity privacy beyond the server. Without reading the original PPFR papers, we cannot treat this as a definitive weakness of those works. The current paper’s framing (“may not adequately capture identity-level leakage”) is already cautious.

- **“The attack is unsurprising to the point of triviality given oracle access.”**  
  While it is information-theoretically plausible that an oracle transform preserving recognition utility is invertible in some embedding space, demonstrating this concretely with >96% commercial-grade regeneration success and near-original linkage in practice is non-trivial. The paper’s value lies exactly in replacing intuition and hand-waving with concrete, quantitative evidence. Calling the attack trivial underestimates that contribution.

- **“Evaluation cannot be trusted because we cannot independently verify the behavior or training data of commercial APIs, or whether Arc2Face exists or is usable.”**  
  The paper treats Face++, Amazon Face Comparison, and Arc2Face as real, deployed or released systems, which by instruction we must as well. Criticisms that hinge on doubting their existence or release status are removed. Concerns about *evaluation design* (e.g., dependence on black-box APIs) are legitimate and already reflected above; doubts about existence are not.

- **“The paper fails because it does not disclose all hyperparameters or low-level implementation details.”**  
  No such lack is apparent in the text we have, and in any case, missing minor implementation minutiae fall under reproducibility nitpicks that we are instructed to discount.

- **Generic claims about missing related work or not citing specific defenses.**  
  Without external access to the full literature beyond the references, we cannot reliably judge omissions in related work and therefore do not treat them as weaknesses.

## Novel Insights

The genuinely novel insight here is empirical and systemic rather than algorithmic: the paper carefully demonstrates that for several contemporary frequency-domain PPFR systems, privacy evaluated via pixel-level non-reconstruction and frequency-channel disruption can be almost orthogonal to the real privacy risk under a realistic insider threat. The combination of (i) extremely simple distillation, (ii) very low resource cost, (iii) success with as few as 256 training images, and (iv) preservation of soft biometrics at near-original accuracy, paints a compelling picture: these templates function essentially as alternate identity embeddings, not as privacy-preserving representations. This shifts the notion of “evaluation” for PPFR from reconstruction- and frequency-space metrics toward identity- and attribute-centric assessments, and it strongly suggests that any transformation-based PPFR that preserves recognition utility for a server is inherently at risk from embedding-alignment attacks unless buttressed by cryptographic or key-based constraints.

## Suggestions

- **Reframe the claims with tighter scope.**  
  Explicitly frame the main result as: “For three recent, high-pass-like frequency-domain PPFR methods, we show that reconstruction-based metrics and frequency-channel disruption substantially overestimate privacy under an insider-oracle threat model. Identity information and soft biometrics remain readily extractable via simple distillation and generative inversion.” Avoid language suggesting that all PPFR or all de-ID schemes are necessarily broken without further evidence.

- **Diversify the evaluation backbone where feasible.**  
  Add at least one experiment where:
  - The student is aligned to ArcFace, but evaluation of linkage and verification uses a *different* FR model (e.g., another open-source backbone trained on different data), or
  - The student is aligned to a different embedding space (e.g., FaceNet) and uses a different generator, even if performance is lower.  
  This would clarify whether the leakage is specific to the ArcFace–Arc2Face ecosystem or more broadly applicable.

- **Clarify the constrained-attacker assumptions and implications.**  
  In §6, more clearly spell out that the attacker still relies on a large public corpus of unprotected faces and on the visually obvious high-pass nature of current PPFR templates, and emphasize that the high-pass proxy is specific to this PPFR family. Present the constrained results as “evidence that vulnerabilities persist even when the attacker cannot query the true conversion directly, as long as they can approximate its high-frequency behavior,” not as a generic template-only break.

- **Strengthen the comparative narrative with prior defenses.**  
  For FracFace in particular, consider:
  - Reproducing their U-Net/StyleGAN reconstruction attack pipeline on the same data, showing that their own metric reports high protection while FaceLinkGen simultaneously achieves high identity regeneration under stricter criteria.
  - More explicitly disentangling “our metric is stronger and more aligned with identity” from “their system is fundamentally insecure,” to keep the comparison fair and convincing.

- **Add small-scale mitigation experiments or at least analytical discussion.**  
  Even if robust defenses are outside scope, a brief empirical investigation of trivial defenses (adding noise, randomizing templates, embedding-space perturbation) would help delineate how deep the problem is. If even crude modifications cannot break the attack without crippling utility, that further reinforces the paper’s message.

- **Clarify the role of commercial APIs.**  
  To increase credibility of the “>96% success” figures:
  - Report, where possible, negative-control collision rates (how often regenerated faces match *other* identities at the same thresholds).
  - Discuss the discrepancy between Face++ and Amazon results (especially in Table 7) and what it suggests about evaluator differences.

### Overall evaluation on key axes

- **Originality:** Moderate. The technical pieces (distillation, embedding inversion, diffusion-based generation) are known; the novel aspect is their application and the systematic critique of reconstruction-centric PPFR evaluation.
- **Importance of research question:** High. Identity leakage in PPFR and de-ID systems deployed or proposed for privacy is a critical problem.
- **Support for claims:** Good within the tested systems and embedding ecosystem, but somewhat overstated when generalized. Narrowing the claims would align them better with the evidence.
- **Soundness of experiments:** Generally strong, with multiple datasets, systems, and evaluation channels, though concentrated around ArcFace and commercial verifiers.
- **Clarity of writing:** Good. Despite PDF artifacts in the provided text, the paper’s structure, threat model, and methods are clear and well explained.
- **Value to the research community:** High, especially for the PPFR and de-ID communities, as it provides concrete evidence that current evaluation standards can give a false sense of privacy.

## Score and Decision

### Calibration

I compared this paper against several human-reviewed works in related areas:

- **KAN See Your Face (razAcpFapu.md, embedding-to-face attacks on PPFR, mostly rejected with scores 1–5).**  
  That paper was criticized for limited novelty and incomplete evaluation. The current submission is conceptually cleaner and more carefully situated in the PPFR privacy context, with stronger, clearer threat modeling and broader experiments (including soft biometrics and constrained attackers). It is substantially stronger than that work.

- **Revealing Unintentional Information Leakage in Low-Dimensional Facial Portrait Representations (48CXLrx7K3.md, reconstruction from feature vectors, scores 3–8, reject).**  
  That work showed leakage from low-dimensional portrait representations but was more narrowly scoped; it did not as directly confront an existing evaluation paradigm nor the PPFR threat model. The present paper is more comprehensive and impactful in terms of implications for an active subfield.

- **On the Inadequacy of Similarity-based Privacy Metrics (g16vmAtJ8x.md, critique of industry privacy metrics with reconstruction attacks, scores 5–8, reject).**  
  This is conceptually similar: revealing flaws in widely used privacy metrics via attacks. The current paper is analogous in the PPFR domain and, like that one, is empirically driven with a strong conceptual message. The topic is narrower but technically more focused; qualitatively, it feels comparable or slightly stronger in tightness of its evaluation within its domain.

- **Does Training with Synthetic Data Truly Protect Privacy? (C8niXBHjfO.md, accepted poster, all scores 6).**  
  That paper also demonstrates that widely believed empirical privacy guarantees (via synthetic data) can fail. It has broader coverage across paradigms and somewhat more diversified experiments. The present paper is somewhat narrower and more ecosystem-specific, but its empirical results on three modern PPFR systems are very compelling. Overall, I see this submission as slightly weaker in breadth but on par in impact within its niche.

Relative to these anchors, this submission is clearly stronger than the rejected attack papers (KAN See Your Face, Targeted Model Inversion), and roughly in the same band as “Does Training with Synthetic Data Truly Protect Privacy?” but with a narrower technical novelty profile and heavy dependence on a single embedding–generator ecosystem. I would place it in the **6.5** range: a solid, impactful paper with some overclaiming and missing diversity in evaluation, but not enough to warrant rejection if the venue welcomes critical empirical work challenging established practices.
