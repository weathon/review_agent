## Summary

FaceLinkGen demonstrates that pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR/SSIM) fundamentally fail to capture identity-level privacy in frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems. By treating the conversion process as a black-box oracle and training a simple distillation pipeline to align protected templates with a standard face embedding space, the authors show that three recent PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace) — which claim strong privacy under pixel-level metrics — are vulnerable to identity linkage and face regeneration attacks. The paper also introduces a constrained attack using a generic high-pass filter proxy and extends the analysis to de-identification systems and soft-biometric leakage.

## Strengths

- **The core thesis is convincingly demonstrated with compelling evidence**: The gap between FracFace's claimed 100% protection rate (under frequency-channel disruption) and FaceLinkGen's near-zero protection rate (0.015 single-trial, Table 6) under identity-centric evaluation is the single most impactful finding, directly falsifying the assumption that pixel-level metrics capture identity privacy.

- **Attack simplicity is itself a finding, not a limitation**: The distillation uses only cosine similarity loss (Eq. 2), trains in under 2 hours on a single GPU for ~$1, and works with as few as 256 training images achieving 98.7% linkage accuracy and 90.5% regeneration success (Section 5.1). This low barrier proves the vulnerability resides in the representation, not in attack sophistication.

- **Table 1 provides a clean, specific falsification of the pixel-identity correlation**: A CanFG-protected face has SSIM=0.841 with the original but identity similarity FS=0.008, while two photos of the same person have SSIM=0.235 but FS=0.586. This directly undermines the implicit assumption underpinning existing evaluation.

- **Threat model analysis (Section 2) is rigorous**: The paper correctly returns to the original insider-adversary framing, systematically dismantles alternative trust assumptions (local models, third-party servers), and shows their threat model assumes less than prior work (no architecture knowledge, no channel parameters).

- **Multi-faceted evaluation breadth**: The paper evaluates linkage (face-to-template, template-to-template, cross-method), regeneration, soft-biometric leakage, transfer to de-identification (TIP-IM, CanFG), and constrained adversaries — providing a comprehensive picture beyond any single metric.

- **Cross-method linkage (Table 4) demonstrates compositional risk**: Templates from different PPFR methods can be linked (e.g., FracFace query to PartialFace key: 0.7537 top-1 recall), meaning an adversary could link identities across independently leaked databases using different protection schemes.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Abstract and body text overstate matching accuracy for FracFace**: The abstract claims "over 98.5% matching accuracy" on three PPFR systems, but Table 3 shows FracFace's 1-to-1 verification accuracy on LFW is 0.92 — well below 98.5%. The body text compounds this by stating verification accuracy "remains near 100%" (Section 5.2). In a paper whose core argument is that prior work's metrics overstate protection, the precision of this paper's own claims matters. The result is still impactful when honestly stated (92–99% matching accuracy, with FracFace being the partial exception), so the overstatement is unnecessary and undermines credibility. The "above 96% regeneration success" claim is correct for Success@5 (minimum 0.979 for FracFace on LFW).

- **Unexplained ~40-point gap between Face++ and Amazon API results for the constrained attack undermines regeneration claims**: Table 7 shows regeneration Success@5 of ~95% on Face++ but only ~45–57% on the Amazon API for the constrained attack. The paper speculates the Amazon API is "likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images" (Section 6) but provides no investigation — no analysis of failure cases, no confidence score comparison, no controlled threshold analysis. Since the matching accuracy (92–96%) is consistent across APIs, the linkage result is robust, but the constrained attack's regeneration claim of "over 94%" is API-dependent. If the Amazon API represents a legitimate commercial verification standard (which the paper endorses by using it for cross-validation in the main experiments), then the constrained attack's regeneration success is closer to a coin flip than the "strikingly persistent" characterization the paper gives it.

### Minor

- **Method-specific adjustment in constrained attack slightly undermines method-agnostic claim**: The constrained attack is presented as a universal, method-agnostic approach, but Section 6 notes that "for MinusFace, it is passed through a Gaussian-blur-based high-pass filter to remove low-frequency noise" during inference. While minor, this system-specific preprocessing step means the attack is not purely method-agnostic as claimed.

- **FracFace's weaker results are not analyzed**: FracFace consistently shows lower matching accuracy (0.92 on LFW vs. 0.98–0.99 for others) and lower constrained-attack performance. Understanding what aspects of its design partially resist the attack would provide actionable guidance for future PPFR design, but the paper does not investigate this.

- **Soft-biometric experiment is trained for only one epoch (Section 9)**: The multi-head prediction MLP is trained for one epoch on FairFace, making it unclear whether the reported accuracies represent lower bounds or are undertrained. This limits the strength of the soft-biometric leakage conclusion.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation testing whether a simpler model (e.g., linear projection) achieves comparable distillation results would strengthen the "vulnerability is in the representation" argument.
- Analysis of failure cases for regeneration (the ~3–10% that fail) would reveal whether the vulnerability is uniform across demographics, poses, or identities.
- A concrete proposal or preliminary test of identity-centric defenses (e.g., adding noise to identity-critical frequency bands) would help move the field forward.

- **Harsh critic: Table 3 lacks standard deviations, confidence intervals** — Standard practice in face recognition evaluation; single-run results without variance are the norm at major venues. Nice-to-have but not a weakness.

- **Harsh critic: StyleGAN attack claim is "unsupported anecdotal"** — The paper references Figure 3 as visual evidence and the claim is in the context of prior work (FracFace's own evaluation), not a new experimental claim of this paper.

- **Harsh critic: missing hyperparameters (optimizer, learning rate, epochs, batch size)** — Nitpick about reproducibility details impractical to fully include in a submission; the core training setup (cosine similarity loss, single A6000, ~2 hours) is described.

- **Harsh critic: demands information-theoretic analysis of the privacy-utility trade-off** — Scope creep; the paper is an empirical demonstration, not a theoretical analysis. This would strengthen the paper but is not its stated contribution.

- **Harsh critic: demands evaluation against PPFR systems designed with identity-centric protection** — All existing frequency-domain PPFR methods use pixel-level evaluation; there is no such system to test against, making this demand unrealistic.

- **Harsh critic: demands proposal and evaluation of a concrete identity-centric defense** — Outside the paper's stated scope; the paper's contribution is identifying the vulnerability and evaluation gap, not proposing defenses.

- **Strength finder: "Cross-verification with independent commercial APIs" as a strength** — While the main experiment cross-validates well (Table 5), the constrained attack shows a massive API discrepancy (Table 7), which partially undercuts this strength. Kept as a moderate strength for the main experiment only.

- **Harsh critic: TIP-IM evaluation sample size is tiny (2,082 images from 408 identities)** — The paper acknowledges this is a pilot/transfer evaluation, not the core contribution. Sufficient for a preliminary demonstration.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most insightful observation is that the PPFR community's evaluation paradigm has been trapped in a circular logic: attacks are designed to optimize pixel-level reconstruction (because that's the evaluation metric), they fail (because recovering discarded nuisance information is genuinely ill-posed), and this failure is then cited as evidence of privacy — when in fact identity information was never the target. The constrained attack's use of a generic high-pass filter as proxy reveals a structural property of this family of methods: they all preserve high-frequency identity information while obfuscating low-frequency visual content, making them vulnerable to any attacker who recognizes this shared characteristic.

## Suggestions

- Qualify the abstract and conclusion to state matching accuracy ranges (e.g., "92–99% matching accuracy") rather than a single over-optimistic threshold, and note FracFace's partial resistance.
- Investigate the Face++ vs. Amazon API discrepancy in the constrained attack: at minimum, report Amazon API confidence score distributions and analyze a sample of failure cases to determine whether the gap reflects threshold differences or genuine attack failure.
- Add a brief discussion of why FracFace is more resistant (e.g., its fractal-based channel selection may disrupt identity-critical frequency components more effectively), as this would provide actionable guidance for future PPFR design.
