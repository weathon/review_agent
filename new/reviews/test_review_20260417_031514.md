## Summary

The paper presents *Calgacus*, a steganographic protocol that uses an LLM to hide an arbitrary text within a different, coherent text of the same token length. The method records the rank of each token in the secret message under the LLM's distribution, then generates a stegotext by following those same ranks under a secret prompt *k*. The paper demonstrates the protocol using Llama 3 8B, provides a public demo, and discusses implications for AI safety, the nature of hallucination, and the decoupling of text from authorial intent.

## Strengths

1. **Elegant and genuinely novel method.** The core idea—preserving token ranks across contexts to achieve stegotext of the same length as the hidden message—is remarkably simple yet non-obvious. The "full capacity" property (same-length encoding) is a meaningful differentiator from prior LLM steganography methods (e.g., Meteor, Wu et al.), which must expand the cover text. The three-step recipe (Section 3) and Figure 3 make the method immediately understandable and reproducible.

2. **Honest analysis of key limitations.** The paper transparently explains why stegotexts are systematically less probable than originals (the "low entropy token choices" analysis), shows concrete failure cases (the hash producing gibberish, the Romanesco dialect failing), and acknowledges the numerical reproducibility challenge (citing Shanmugavelu et al., 2024). This transparency strengthens the paper.

3. **Practical efficiency and accessibility.** The protocol works on consumer hardware with 8B models, encoding/decoding in seconds, and the authors provide an open-source demo. This makes the work verifiable and lowers the barrier to entry.

4. **Thought-provoking conceptual contributions.** The discussion on hallucination as "lack of intention," the Oulipo analogy, and the "Shibbolethian Theatre" scenario for unaligned chatbots are original and intellectually stimulating, adding a philosophical dimension rare in steganography papers.

5. **Compelling illustrative examples.** The Caesar speech hidden in a boar recipe, and the cross-lingual examples (Tamil, Chinese), vividly demonstrate the phenomenon and make the paper unusually engaging.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

1. **The "full capacity" claim is imprecise and potentially misleading.** The paper uses "full capacity" as a term of art meaning "the stegotext and the secret message are of the same length" (in tokens). However, "full capacity" in information theory refers to the maximum rate at which information can be reliably transmitted through a channel. The paper provides no information-theoretic capacity analysis—no bits-per-token accounting under realistic rank distributions, no analysis of effective channel capacity. The method preserves token count, but this is not "full capacity" in the formal sense. More importantly, the paper's own examples demonstrate that the protocol *fails* for out-of-distribution inputs (hashes → gibberish), degrades for texts the LLM predicts poorly, and requires padding and auxiliary prompts (*k'*) to work reliably. The "same length" property holds trivially by construction; the actual question—how often does the method produce *usable* stegotext—is not quantified. The headline claim should be weakened to match what is demonstrated.

2. **Evaluation is too narrow to support the paper's claims.** All quantitative evaluation uses only three original texts (selected at μ, μ−2σ, μ+2σ of a Reddit distribution), each truncated to exactly 85 tokens, with 100 stegotexts per text. There is no human evaluation at all—despite the central claim that stegotexts are "opaque to humans." The only metric used is log-probability under a single model (Llama 3 8B), with a supplementary check under Phi-3 3.8B. There is no systematic study across message lengths, domains, or model sizes. The paper acknowledges that LLMs can statistically distinguish originals from stegotexts, but quantifies this only via visual histogram comparison (Figure 4)—no AUC, no detection error tradeoffs, no ROC curves. Given that the philosophical and safety arguments depend heavily on human undetectability and deniability, this evidential gap is significant.

3. **Security and deniability claims are asserted without adequate analysis.** The paper explicitly declines to provide a formal security model ("we will avoid building a palace on the sand"), yet makes concrete claims about brute-force resistance (O(d^{|k|})), deniability, and the difficulty of key recovery. The brute-force bound is trivial; the more interesting attack—using an LLM to constrain the natural-language key space given *s*—is acknowledged and dismissed in one sentence ("inserting a simple random string in *k* is enough to nip it in the bud") without analysis of how this affects stegotext quality. The deniability argument (Figure 15) is supported by a single anecdotal example, with no quantification of how often random plausible keys decode to high-probability messages. For a paper making security and safety claims, this is insufficient—the paper wants the cachet of security claims without the rigor to support them.

4. **The "unaligned chatbot" scenario is presented as a concrete threat but rests on unrealistic assumptions.** The protocol requires exact logit reproduction across user and company hardware (acknowledged as challenging), users who must know about the decoding trick, run the precise oLLM version locally, and use a custom pipeline. No end-to-end demonstration under realistic deployment conditions is provided. The scenario is thought-provoking as a conceptual argument about knowledge decoupling; it should be explicitly framed as such rather than as an imminent deployment risk.

### Minor:

5. **No comparison with prior LLM steganography methods.** The paper cites Meteor, Wu et al., and Zamir but provides no empirical comparison on stegotext quality, detectability, or effective bit-rate. The claimed novelty of "full capacity" is contextualized only conceptually, not experimentally.

6. **Variations (*k'*, padding) are introduced but never systematically evaluated.** The paper mentions that adding *k'* before *e* can lower ranks and improve stegotext quality, but provides no quantitative study of the trade-off between key size and quality.

7. **The philosophical discussion, while interesting, overreaches based on the empirical evidence.** The argument from "LLMs can encode arbitrary text in plausible-seeming text" to "radical decoupling of text from authorial intent" is a significant inferential leap. Ordinary LLM sampling already decouples surface text from specific authorial intent; Calgacus makes this more visible but does not create a qualitatively new phenomenon. The discussion would be stronger with empirical backing (e.g., a user study showing humans cannot distinguish stegotexts from originals).

### Trivial:

- Tie-breaking for identical probabilities is not discussed, though this is a minor implementation detail.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Human evaluation study (even small-scale) assessing whether readers can distinguish stegotexts from originals.
- Systematic evaluation across message lengths (50, 200, 500+ tokens), domains (code, fiction, technical writing), and model scales.
- Quantified failure rate: what fraction of randomly sampled texts produce incoherent stegotexts?
- Formal or semi-formal information-theoretic analysis of the effective channel capacity of the rank-based encoding.
- Adversarial detectability experiments: training a classifier to distinguish stegotexts from originals, reporting AUC and detection error rates.

## Removed Points

- **"Full capacity" requires identical model/tokenizer on both ends, so capacity is not truly preserved.** The paper is transparent about requiring identical LLM conditions. The capacity claim is about *stegotext length matching secret message length*, which is the same regardless of who runs it. This is a deployment constraint, not a capacity issue. *Removed because it misidentifies the nature of the claim.*

- **Comparison table with prior steganography methods showing capacity/quality trade-offs.** While useful, this is beyond the paper's stated scope, which focuses on introducing a new protocol with a specific property, not benchmarking against all prior work. *Moved to nice-to-have.*

- **Reproducibility concerns about undisclosed hyperparameters (quantization level, temperature, llama.cpp settings).** The paper provides a public demo and uses standard settings; such implementation details are trivially discoverable from the code and not a meaningful weakness. *Removed as a reproducibility nitpick.*

- **Demand for formal cryptographic security proofs.** The paper explicitly and reasonably states it avoids formal security models because current formal steganography models require unrealistic assumptions. Requesting formal proofs is outside the paper's stated scope. *Removed as scope creep; however, the informal security claims that ARE made remain a valid weakness above.*

- **The discussion sections are "overly speculative and digressive."** The philosophical discussion on hallucination, intention, and the Oulipo analogy is arguably the most original contribution and central to the paper's framing. Whether one finds it digressive is a matter of taste, not a substantive weakness. *Removed as subjective.*

## Novel Insights

The paper's most striking observation—one not explicitly highlighted by the reviewers—is the "low entropy token choices" asymmetry: because rank-1 tokens account for ~40% of positions but carry ~95% probability in low-entropy contexts, the stegotext systematically "wastes" high-probability token slots on high-entropy decisions, creating a detectable probability gap even though ranks are preserved. This is a genuine insight into why the method produces plausible but distinguishable text, and it connects information-theoretic properties of LLM distributions directly to steganographic detectability.

## Suggestions

1. **Rewrite the "full capacity" framing.** Replace "full capacity" with "same-length encoding" or "length-preserving steganography" throughout, and add a brief paragraph acknowledging that effective information throughput depends on rank distribution entropy and is not analyzed here.

2. **Add a quantified failure analysis.** Run the protocol on a representative corpus (e.g., 500+ texts of varying lengths and domains) and report the fraction producing coherent stegotexts under reasonable prompt selection. This single experiment would dramatically strengthen the paper.

3. **Constrain the security claims.** Either provide semi-formal security arguments with explicit threat models, or clearly reframe Section 3.1 as "preliminary observations on security" rather than giving the appearance of a security analysis.

4. **Reframe the AI safety scenario as a thought experiment** rather than a concrete deployment threat, acknowledging the practical barriers (logit reproducibility, user coordination) explicitly.

5. **Add at least pairwise scatter plots** of original vs. stegotext log-probability for the evaluation texts, so readers can see the per-pair relationship rather than only aggregate histograms.
