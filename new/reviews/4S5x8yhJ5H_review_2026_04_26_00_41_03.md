## Summary

VIBEFACE introduces a novel facial biometric dataset of 2,250 images and 1,550 videos from 50 demographically balanced subjects, collected under five controlled sessions (varying lighting, eyeglass conditions, multiple devices) and including eKYC-style verification video scenarios (head rotation, blinking, expression change, face touching, etc.). The paper benchmarks face detection (MTCNN, RetinaFace, MediaPipe) and face verification (ArcFace, MagFace) on the dataset, reporting results broken down by scenario, session, gender, age group, and racial category.

## Strengths

- **Careful, principled dataset design**: The five-session protocol systematically varies lighting (artificial, flash, natural, weak natural), eyeglass presence, and acquisition device, producing ecologically valid challenging conditions. The inclusion of zero-lens eyeglasses for non-wearers to standardize occlusion is a thoughtful detail (Section 3.3). Table 2 cleanly enumerates all session/scenario combinations.
- **First dataset explicitly targeting eKYC-style verification workflows with demographic balance**: Table 1 compares VIBEFACE against seven existing datasets, none of which combine eKYC video scenarios with balanced demographic metadata. The seven verification video scenarios (12–18) capturing liveness challenge actions alongside standardized and selfie photos address an identifiable gap.
- **Results reveal real, quantifiable disparities**: MTCNN's detection rate for African subjects drops to 0.610 (off-angle views) vs. 0.984 for East Asian subjects (Table 3), and ArcFace's off-angle verification rate ranges from 0.433 to 0.509 across sessions (Table 4). These non-trivial performance gaps confirm the dataset captures conditions that stress current models.
- **Strong ethical and legal framework**: GDPR and EU AI Act compliance, informed consent, anonymization via randomized identifiers, and controlled-access licensing (Section 3.4–3.5) address legitimate concerns about biometric data governance.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The face verification evaluation uses improper biometric metrics, undermining the "benchmark" claim**: The paper reports verification results using a single fixed similarity threshold of 0.5 and measures "percentage of frames correctly authenticated" (Section 4.2). Standard face verification benchmarks use ROC curves, Equal Error Rate (EER), and True Accept Rate at specified False Accept Rates (TAR@FAR). A single threshold provides no information about the discriminability of genuine vs. impostor score distributions. More critically, **there is no impostor analysis whatsoever** — all comparisons are between a subject and their own reference image (genuine pairs only). Without impostor comparisons, one cannot interpret what a 50% verification rate means: it could reflect a genuinely hard problem (scores clustering near the threshold from below), or simply a threshold that falls in a low-density region, or even that impostor scores are also near 0.5 (meaning the system cannot actually distinguish subjects). The paper's central claim that VIBEFACE "establishes a new benchmark" is not supported by this evaluation. A benchmark requires a defined protocol with proper metrics, which this paper does not provide. This is not a minor methodological choice — it is the difference between a benchmark and a dataset with preliminary evaluation.

- **50 subjects is insufficient for reliable demographic fairness analysis**: With 12–13 subjects per racial category, the effective per-cell sample size for intersectional analysis (e.g., African females aged 51–70) is approximately 3–4, making any demographic comparison unreliable. The paper reports demographic breakdowns (e.g., ArcFace on African subjects: 0.460, Caucasian: 0.509) without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests. With such small groups, observed differences could easily reflect sampling noise rather than systematic bias. While the overall patterns (e.g., MTCNN's lower detection for African subjects) are consistent with known literature and thus plausible, the specific point estimates cannot be treated as reliable fairness measurements. This limits the dataset's credibility for its stated fairness mission.

### Minor

- **The eKYC distinction is under-argued**: The individual actions in the verification scenarios (circular head rotation, blinking, expression changes — scenarios 12–16) overlap substantially with standard liveness detection/PAD challenge actions found in Replay-Mobile, OULU-NPU, and WMCA. The paper's claim of novelty rests on combining these into an "eKYC workflow" alongside still images, but it does not articulate what specifically about the sequencing or operational context differentiates this from existing PAD datasets. This affects framing rather than substance — the dataset still provides genuine value — but the novelty of the overall eKYC framing could be better justified.

- **Detection benchmark is partially uninformative**: RetinaFace achieves perfect (1.000) detection on all image scenarios, all demographic groups, and nearly all video scenarios (Table 3). This leaves no room to evaluate whether the dataset captures challenges for state-of-the-art detection. MTCNN's results do show meaningful variation, but the benchmark offers little discriminative information for modern detectors.

- **Per-frame rather than per-video verification evaluation**: Each video frame is treated as an independent verification trial. Real eKYC systems aggregate scores across frames. This inflates the effective number of comparisons and does not reflect operational practice, though it suffices as a preliminary evaluation.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **ROC curves, EER, and TAR@FAR metrics** with genuine and impostor comparisons — this would transform the paper from a dataset-with-preliminary-evaluation into a genuine benchmark.
- **Confidence intervals or bootstrap-based uncertainty estimates** for the demographic breakdowns, given the small sample sizes.
- **Cross-device evaluation protocol**: The paper collects data from three devices but does not evaluate cross-device generalization, which is one of the stated motivations.
- **Video-level verification evaluation** that aggregates per-frame scores into a per-subject decision.
- **Expand beyond 50 subjects** if the dataset is intended to be a primary fairness evaluation benchmark.

## Novel Insights

The most distinctive insight from this paper is not in its evaluation (which is flawed) but in its **design**: combining eKYC-motivated verification video scenarios with a demographically balanced, multi-session, multi-device protocol produces a dataset where even SOTA models show meaningful performance degradation — ArcFace drops from near-perfect frontal verification (~1.000) to 0.433 off-angle verification (Session C with glasses), and MTCNN shows a 37-point gap between African (0.610) and East Asian (0.984) detection rates. The dataset itself is well-collected and fills a gap; its primary limitation is not in the data but in the evaluation methodology used to characterize it.

## Suggestions

- Replace the fixed-threshold verification metric with proper biometric evaluation: compute genuine and impostor similarity distributions, plot ROC curves, and report EER and TAR@FAR=1%. This single change would dramatically strengthen the paper.
- Report standard deviations or confidence intervals (via bootstrap) for all per-demographic-group results to acknowledge the small sample sizes.
- Consider reframing the title/abstract from "establishes a new benchmark" to "introduces a novel dataset with preliminary evaluation," reserving the "benchmark" designation for when a complete evaluation protocol is defined.
- Add video-level evaluation (score aggregation across frames) as a complementary analysis.
