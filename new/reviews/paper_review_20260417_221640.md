## Summary
FaceLinkGen challenges the dominant evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR), arguing that resistance to pixel-level reconstruction (measured by PSNR/SSIM) does not guarantee identity privacy. The authors present a distillation-based attack that aligns protected templates with a public ArcFace embedding space and regenerates identity-consistent faces via Arc2Face, achieving over 98.5% 1-to-1 matching accuracy and above 96% regeneration success on three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace) while costing ~$1.60 and ~2 hours of compute. The paper advocates for identity-centric evaluation as a necessary complement to pixel-distortion metrics.

---

## Strengths

- **Core evaluation critique is genuinely important.** The paper demonstrates clearly that PSNR/SSIM metrics conflate visual unreconstructibility with identity privacy. Table 1 (CanFG face: SSIM=0.841, FS=0.008; same-person photos: SSIM=0.235, FS=0.586) and the Figure 2 example are effective, hard-to-dispute illustrations of the disconnect.
- **Minimal-resource attack is the most compelling single result.** Training on only 256 images achieves 98.7% linkage accuracy and 90.5% regeneration success; 800 images in 50 seconds yields 97.0% generation pass rate. This operationalizes the paper's core argument—that vulnerability lies in the representation, not attack sophistication—better than any theoretical framing.
- **FracFace falsification (Table 6) is clean and direct.** FracFace claims 100% channel disruption for itself under its own metric; FaceLinkGen achieves near-total identity recovery. This directly refutes a published defensive claim using the authors' own evaluation standard.
- **Multi-API cross-verification.** Using both Face++ and Amazon APIs for the main (oracle-access) attack, and additionally verifying in ArcFace embedding space (Section 7), provides credible triangulation of results independent of any single evaluator.
- **Threat model is carefully grounded.** The discussion of why oracle access is the natural adversary, and why this is actually *weaker* than prior attackers (Mi et al. [20][21]), is more rigorous than typical in this space. The critique of CanFG's security assumption and Zhang et al.'s server-query requirement is appropriately pointed.
- **Soft biometric analysis (Section 9) extends the paper's scope** by showing that gender, age, and race are preserved in templates, corroborating the core identity-leakage argument with a distinct methodology.
- **Cost framing is persuasive.** Positioning the attack as a ~$1.60, standard distillation pipeline is an effective rhetorical and evidential move: it is not an exotic adversarial method, and its success implies that the protection fails against a commodity tool.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Unexplained Amazon API / Face++ discrepancy in the constrained attack.** In Table 7, Face++ regeneration success@5 is 94.6–96.3% across the three systems, while Amazon API success is 44.7–57.0% — a twofold gap on the *same generated images*. The paper's explanation ("the Amazon API is likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images") is unsupported and not investigated further. In the main (oracle-access) experiments, the paper states Amazon results "are close to the Face++ results" (Section 5.3), so this divergence is specific to the constrained setting. If the Amazon API better captures real-world verifier behavior, regeneration success under constrained assumptions is substantially weaker than the headline "strikingly persistent identity leakage" claim. Notably, the 1-to-1 *linkage* accuracy (92–96%) does not depend on commercial APIs and remains robust even in the constrained setting, so the linkage claims survive. However, the constrained-scenario *regeneration* claim needs either a mechanistic explanation for the discrepancy or explicit qualification.

- **DuetFace and FaceObfuscator are named in the introduction but not attacked, without being flagged as a limitation.** Section 1 explicitly lists five PPFR representative systems (DuetFace, PartialFace, MinusFace, FaceObfuscator, FracFace) and implies a systematic evaluation. The paper attacks only three, with the omission of DuetFace (ACM MM 2022) and FaceObfuscator (USENIX Security 2024) noted only implicitly via "methods with accessible source code." FaceObfuscator in particular is a security-venue publication with gradient-descent-resistant features—its resistance (or lack thereof) to a distillation-based attack would be the most informative data point in the paper. The omission should be explicitly framed as a limitation and the scope of the central claim should be accordingly bounded to the three tested methods rather than presented as a property of the full class.

### Minor

- **Constrained attack preprocessing for MinusFace creates a small tension.** The paper states that during inference for the constrained attack, "MinusFace templates are passed through a Gaussian-blur-based high-pass filter to remove low-frequency noise," while simultaneously claiming the attacker has "no knowledge of the system." Even if the high-pass characteristic is "visually observable," selecting a specific filter tailored to MinusFace outputs is system-adaptive. This should be acknowledged rather than subsumed under the "universal proxy task" framing.

- **"98.5% matching accuracy" in the abstract and conclusion is ambiguous.** This figure derives from the 1-to-1 verification accuracy on LFW (Table 3, minimum 0.988). The closed-set 1-to-N linkage accuracy in Table 4 ranges from 72–86%, which has very different operational meaning for a real-world attacker. A one-sentence disambiguation in the abstract would prevent misinterpretation.

- **One-epoch soft-biometric training is not framed as a lower bound.** Section 9 trains the multi-head MLP for only one epoch without justification. Results still show near-original accuracy for FracFace and PartialFace, suggesting the one-epoch result is already a conservative bound. The paper should state this explicitly so readers understand the experiment underestimates leakage rather than being underpowered.

### Trivial

- **Theoretical framing (Equations 1–3) is correct but does not add substantial explanatory value beyond the empirical results.** The formal argument that "any template preserving identity utility retains aligning features for distillation" is nearly definitional. The paper would benefit from specifying what property of a PPFR design would make distillation *hard*—this is touched on but only at the pointer level in Section 10.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Test with an alternative embedding-generator pair** (e.g., FaceNet + compatible generator) to empirically demonstrate that the vulnerability is representation-level rather than ArcFace/Arc2Face-specific. The paper argues this conceptually but doesn't demonstrate it.
- **Ablation on the Amazon vs. Face++ discrepancy in the constrained setting.** Are failures concentrated in specific demographic subsets, specific methods, or correlated with Arc2Face output quality (FID)? This analysis would either resolve or precisely bound the uncertainty about the constrained scenario.
- **Failure case analysis for regeneration.** The 1–10% failure rate across datasets is not characterized. Analysis of whether failures cluster by pose, demographics, or protection aggressiveness would both strengthen the empirical analysis and provide useful signal for defensive design.
- **Information-theoretic framing of identity leakage.** Even an informal mutual-information argument characterizing when distillation succeeds would elevate the theoretical contribution.
- **Explicit discussion of what a resistant system would look like.** The Section 10 future directions is promising, but connecting it to the formal argument (e.g., cryptographic keying makes oracle access insufficient because the attacker cannot generate paired samples without the key) would make the paper self-contained.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation synthesized across both reviewers is the *methodological shift from pixel reconstruction to identity embedding alignment as the attack surface*. Prior attack literature optimized for pixel-level loss functions—an objective that is ill-posed when the PPFR system discards non-identity information. FaceLinkGen's insight is that this misdirected objective explains *why* reconstruction attacks fail while identity leakage persists: the missing nuisance factor is simply replaced by sampling from a generative model, bypassing the ill-posedness entirely. The minimal-resource result (50 seconds, 800 images) operationalizes this elegantly, showing the vulnerability is in the representation rather than any property of the attack. Taken together with the Amazon API discrepancy in the constrained setting—which suggests the *regeneration* attack may be partially confounded by model-specific factors—a careful reader is left with a nuanced picture: *linkage-level* identity extraction is essentially free and robust, while *regeneration-level* identity recovery under constrained assumptions may be more evaluator-dependent than the paper acknowledges.

## Suggestions

1. **Resolve or explicitly bound the Amazon API discrepancy** in the constrained attack. Report whether Amazon API results for the main (oracle-access) regeneration are similarly close to Face++ (Table 5 suggests yes for LFW); if so, explain why the constrained setting diverges. If not resolvable, add a caveat to the "strikingly persistent" claim in Section 6.
2. **Explicitly scope the paper's generalization claims** to the three tested methods. Rename the key conclusion from "these methods fail identity-level evaluation" rather than implying coverage of all five named representative systems.
3. **Formally acknowledge DuetFace and FaceObfuscator omissions** as a limitation, and note that the argument for their vulnerability under oracle access holds theoretically (any recognition-utility-preserving template satisfies Eq. 1), but empirical validation is pending.
4. **Disambiguate the "98.5% matching accuracy" headline** in the abstract to clarify it refers to 1-to-1 LFW verification, and add a sentence on what closed-set 1-to-N linkage accuracy implies operationally.
5. **Frame the one-epoch soft-biometric result as a lower bound** to preempt methodological critiques and strengthen the leakage claim.
