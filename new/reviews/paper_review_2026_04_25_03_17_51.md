## Summary
FaceLinkGen argues that frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems are evaluated inadequately via pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM), and introduces an identity-centric attack that extracts identity information from protected templates through a lightweight distillation pipeline followed by diffusion-based face regeneration. The paper demonstrates high attack success rates (97.9–100% Success@5) across three PPFR systems and multiple datasets, with cross-validation by two independent commercial APIs, and extends the analysis to constrained attackers, de-identification systems, and soft biometric leakage.

---

## Strengths

- **Table 1 directly falsifies the pixel-level evaluation assumption**: A CanFG-protected face achieves SSIM=0.841 with the original but face similarity=0.008; two natural photos of the same person achieve SSIM=0.235 but face similarity=0.586. This crisp quantitative example establishes the core argument without requiring the reader to accept the full attack methodology.

- **Minimal-resource experiments (Section 5.1) are a genuine contribution**: With only 256 training images and ~50 seconds of compute, the attack achieves 98.7% linkage accuracy and 90.5% regeneration success on FracFace. This definitively falsifies any defense of the form "the attack requires privileged data access," and directly supports the paper's thesis that the vulnerability is structural to the template representation.

- **Regeneration attack results are strong across all conditions**: Table 2 reports Success@5 ≥ 97.9% and Pass@1e-5 ≥ 90.4% across all three PPFR methods and all three test datasets (TPDNE, LFW, and CASIA-WebFace hold-out), establishing robustness against dataset shift.

- **Cross-validation with two independent commercial APIs** (Face++ and Amazon via EdenAI, Table 5) substantially reduces concerns about evaluation circularity. On the main experiments, both APIs agree (Table 5 Accuracy all ≥ 0.988), lending credibility to the headline results.

- **Table 6 exposes the evaluation mismatch quantitatively**: FracFace claims 68–100% channel-disruption-based protection rates, but FaceLinkGen achieves near-zero protection rates under identity-centric evaluation. This is direct evidence, not speculative, that the existing evaluation paradigm systematically overstates privacy.

- **Threat model analysis is unusually careful**: Section 2 provides a principled argument for why the service provider is the natural adversary, why oracle access is a realistic assumption (local model reverse-engineering), and why the trust-shift argument against a third-party conversion server does not resolve the privacy problem. This framing is stronger than most attack papers in this area.

- **Soft biometric leakage (Table 9)** demonstrates that race (~67% vs. 70% baseline), gender (~93% vs. 95%), and age (MAE ~4.7 vs. 4.6 baseline) are recoverable at near-original accuracy for FracFace and PartialFace, directly contradicting FracFace's public rebuttal claiming successful obfuscation of these attributes.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Missing Arc2Face oracle baseline undermines the quantitative interpretation of regeneration results.** The paper's regeneration pipeline is: protected template → student model → ArcFace embedding → Arc2Face → generated face → Face++ verification. The paper never reports what Face++ pass rate Arc2Face achieves when fed *directly extracted* (non-PPFR) ArcFace embeddings from LFW or CASIA-WebFace images. Arc2Face is designed to invert ArcFace embeddings faithfully, and if its baseline pass rate from ground-truth embeddings is, say, 92%, then FracFace's 90.4% Pass@1e-5 on TPDNE images indicates templates that are nearly identity-lossless — a much stronger claim. If the baseline is ~99%, the degradation is meaningful. The embedding similarity analysis in Section 7 partially addresses this (showing template embeddings are closer to the source image than same-person images), but does not resolve the quantitative ceiling question for the Face++-reported success rates. An oracle baseline would be a straightforward addition and would substantially strengthen the empirical claim.

### Minor

- **Ambiguous abstract claim and mismatch with Table 3's FracFace result.** The abstract and conclusion both state "over 98.5% matching accuracy on three recent frequency-domain PPFR systems." Table 3 (1-to-1 verification accuracy on LFW) reports FracFace at 0.92, which is well below 98.5%. The 98.5% figure is most plausibly supported by Table 5's Amazon API accuracy values (all ≥ 0.988), but (a) Table 5 is framed as regeneration pass rate, not "matching accuracy," and (b) the abstract does not point the reader to the right table. At a minimum, the abstract should disambiguate which metric and which experiment the 98.5% figure refers to, and either correct the framing for FracFace or add a note that the value is system-specific.

- **Face++/Amazon discrepancy in the constrained attack (Table 7) is unexplained.** In the main results (Tables 2 and 5), both commercial APIs broadly agree (>97% and >98.8% respectively). In Table 7, Face++ reports 94.6–96.3% Success@5 while Amazon reports only 44.7–57.0%. The paper dismisses this as "Amazon likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images," but this explanation was not needed in the main experiments where both APIs agreed. The discrepancy could reflect lower-quality image generation from the proxy-based student model, AI-image detection artifacts, or genuine identity information loss from the high-pass approximation. Without further investigation, it is unclear whether the constrained attack achieves ~95% success (per Face++) or ~50% (per Amazon). This matters because the two conclusions have different practical implications for external-attacker scenarios.

- **Mild overclaim in Section 5.2 about linkage results.** The paper states the attack "essentially reaches the dataset's theoretical maximum performance" given 9–13% dataset noise. However, the FracFace→MinusFace cross-template linkage (0.7537) is noticeably below the original-image ceiling for that pair (0.8241) — a gap of ~7 points. The statement is accurate as a statistical average but not for all configurations, and a more careful phrasing acknowledging variation across method pairs would be appropriate.

### Trivial
- The term "matching accuracy" is used in the abstract without definition or a pointer to the specific table, making the headline figure difficult to interpret without reading the full paper.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most genuinely novel observation is the *structural* argument: that any PPFR system designed to preserve recognition utility must preserve identity-discriminative features, making the utility–privacy tradeoff not a matter of degree but of kind — and that pixel-level metrics cannot detect this because identity similarity and pixel similarity are mathematically orthogonal (as shown concretely in Table 1). The minimal-resource finding (Section 5.1) is the empirical corollary: if 256 training images and 50 seconds of compute suffice to extract near-perfect identity information, the vulnerability is in the template's information content, not in the attacker's capabilities. Together, these observations reframe PPFR privacy not as a reconstruction problem to be made harder, but as a representation problem requiring stronger cryptographic guarantees — a reframing that, if accepted by the community, has broad consequences for how future systems should be evaluated and designed.

## Suggestions

1. Add an Arc2Face oracle baseline: feed ground-truth LFW/CASIA-WebFace images through Antelopev2 → Arc2Face (bypassing any PPFR) and report Face++ Success@5 and Pass@1e-5 at the same FAR thresholds. This single experiment contextualizes all regeneration results.

2. Revise the abstract's "98.5% matching accuracy" claim to specify which table and metric it refers to, and either correct or add a footnote about FracFace's 0.92 in Table 3.

3. Investigate the Face++/Amazon discrepancy in Table 7 by analyzing image quality (e.g., FID score of constrained-attack outputs vs. main-attack outputs) and checking whether Amazon's model has AI-generation detection that explains the divergence.

4. In Section 5.2, qualify "essentially reaching the dataset's theoretical maximum" with the per-method performance; acknowledge that FracFace→MinusFace cross-template linkage (0.75) is noticeably below the original-image ceiling (0.82) for that configuration.
