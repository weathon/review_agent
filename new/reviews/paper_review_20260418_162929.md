## Summary

FaceLinkGen presents an identity-centric attack on frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems, arguing that the prevalent evaluation paradigm — measuring privacy as resistance to pixel-level reconstruction via PSNR/SSIM — fundamentally fails to capture identity-level leakage. The paper proposes a straightforward distillation pipeline in which a student model learns to extract identity embeddings from protected templates, enabling both linkage attacks and diffusion-based face regeneration without recovering original pixels. Evaluated on three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), the attack achieves near-total identity recovery (90.4–99.3% per-image pass rate at the strictest commercial threshold; 97.9–100% Success@5), even with as few as 256 training images and under 50 seconds of training.

## Strengths

- **Quantitative decoupling of pixel-level and identity-level metrics (Table 1)**: The paper operationalizes its central argument with concrete numbers — a CanFG-protected face achieves SSIM=0.841 vs. the original but face similarity FS=0.008, while two different photos of the same person achieve SSIM=0.235 but FS=0.586. This single table is the clearest empirical statement of the evaluation-paradigm problem.

- **Near-total regeneration success across three PPFR systems and three datasets (Table 2)**: Per-image Pass@FAR=1e-5 ranges from 90.4% to 99.3%, and Success@5 from 97.9% to 100%, across PartialFace, MinusFace, and FracFace on TPDNE, CASIA-WebFace hold-out, and LFW. The breadth of evaluation (three systems × three datasets) and the strictness of the commercial verification threshold give these results genuine weight.

- **Minimal-resource experiment (Section 5.1)**: With 256 training images and under 50 seconds of training, the attack still achieves 90.5% regeneration success and 98.7% linkage accuracy against FracFace. This result directly refutes any "data requirements" defense and shows the vulnerability is structural, not computational.

- **Constrained-attacker experiment (Section 6)**: A single student model trained with only a generic Gaussian-blur high-pass filter proxy (no PPFR system internals, only 30 validation pairs) achieves >92% 1-to-1 matching and >94% regeneration Success@5 on Face++ across all three methods. This meaningfully extends the scope beyond the oracle-access insider case.

- **Cross-verification with two independent commercial APIs (Table 5)**: Results are validated with both Face++ and Amazon, avoiding circular evaluation. The Amazon API provides an independent second opinion on the regeneration quality and confirms the main findings.

- **Transfer to de-identification systems (Table 8)**: The same distillation pipeline achieves 0.997 linkage accuracy against TIP-IM (adversarial de-identification that fools ArcFace entirely) and CanFG, demonstrating the vulnerability generalizes beyond the frequency-domain family.

- **Soft biometric analysis (Table 9)**: Race, gender, and age inference from protected templates achieves near-original-image accuracy for FracFace and PartialFace, providing empirical grounding for the regulatory-privacy argument.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None.

### Minor

- **Unexplained Amazon API gap in the constrained-attacker setting**: In Table 7, the constrained attacker achieves 44.7–57.0% Success@5 on Amazon vs. 94.6–96.3% on Face++. The paper dismisses this with "the Amazon API is likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images" — a plausible but unverified claim. The gap is large enough (~40 percentage points) to raise questions about generalizability in the constrained-attacker scenario, which is the setting most relevant to external or low-knowledge adversaries. A control experiment (e.g., running Amazon on Arc2Face outputs from *unprotected* embeddings) would determine whether the gap reflects API calibration against AI-generated imagery or genuine attack degradation. As-is, the paper's claim of "strikingly persistent" identity leakage under constrained assumptions rests primarily on the Face++ numbers.

- **Missing unprotected-image baseline for quantifying marginal identity degradation**: The paper demonstrates that identity information is recoverable from protected templates, but does not establish how much identity information the protections actually remove. Without measuring per-image Pass@FAR=1e-5 for Arc2Face outputs derived from *original* (unprotected) embeddings as a baseline, it is impossible to quantify the marginal degradation provided by each PPFR method. If unprotected images already yield ~92% per-image success and FracFace yields 90.4%, that is a 2% marginal effect — the protection still provides something. If unprotected images yield ~99% and FracFace yields 90.4%, that represents meaningful but incomplete protection. This baseline is necessary to properly characterize the severity of the vulnerability.

### Trivial

- **Success@5 as the headline metric**: The abstract states ">96% regeneration success," which corresponds to Success@5. Per-image Pass@1e-5 goes as low as 90.4%. Both metrics are reported in Table 2 so this is not hidden, but Success@5 is a lenient criterion (1-(1-p)^5 substantially inflates apparent success when p is in the 80–95% range). Clearer framing in the abstract would help readers interpret the headline claim accurately.

- **Citation of FracFace's OpenReview rebuttal** for soft-biometric obfuscation claims (Section 9): The rebuttal is unreviewed, and the paper should either cite FracFace's published claims or note the source limitation. This is a presentation issue, not a scientific one.

- **No variance estimates for minimal-resource experiments**: The 256-image results (90.5% regeneration, 98.7% linkage) are reported as single runs. Given the small dataset size and stochastic training, reporting variance over multiple random seeds would strengthen the claim.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation on the single 3×3 Conv2D prepended to Antelopev2 ("for compatibility with different template formats") would clarify whether this layer performs nontrivial domain-specific learning or is functionally a reshape. This would help characterize how generic vs. system-specific the student model's knowledge is.
- Testing against key-based or cryptographic PPFR methods (e.g., Yuan et al. [41] is cited in Section 10) would sharpen the paper's practical recommendations — if these methods resist the attack, that constitutes an actionable existence proof of a viable defense direction.
- Failure case analysis (e.g., which identities or image conditions cause failures in regeneration) would help practitioners understand when the vulnerability is most and least severe.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most important insight is not that identity information persists in PPFR templates — this is almost guaranteed for any system that retains recognition utility — but rather that the PPFR evaluation literature has systematically conflated "resistance to pixel reconstruction" with "resistance to identity extraction," and that this conflation is self-reinforcing: systems are attacked with pixel-level loss functions because they are evaluated with pixel-level metrics, causing both the attack and the metric to miss the actual privacy-relevant quantity. The distillation pipeline's simplicity is itself the finding: if a generic, inexpensive student model with 256 training images can recover identity information, the vulnerability resides in the representation, not in the sophistication of the attacker. The constrained-attacker extension further shows that all three evaluated frequency-domain systems share a common visual signature (high-frequency component retention) that makes them collectively vulnerable to a single generic proxy attack — a structural property that their independent design choices inadvertently share.

## Suggestions

1. Add a single-row baseline in Table 2: report Arc2Face regeneration pass rates from *original* (unprotected) ArcFace embeddings. This allows readers to quantify marginal identity degradation per method and is the most important missing experiment.
2. In the constrained-attacker section (Section 6), add a control: measure Amazon's acceptance rate on Arc2Face outputs from unprotected embeddings, to determine whether the ~47% Amazon success is due to API calibration against AI-generated imagery or actual attack limitations.
3. Reframe the Success@5 headline in the abstract to also cite the per-image pass rate, so casual readers are not misled.
4. Replace the OpenReview rebuttal citation in Section 9 with a citation to FracFace's published claims or explicitly note the rebuttal is unreviewed.
