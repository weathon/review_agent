## Summary

FaceLinkGen argues that pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR/SSIM) are inadequate for evaluating identity privacy in frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems. The paper introduces a simple distillation-based pipeline that extracts identity information from protected templates, achieving high regeneration success and 1-to-1 verification accuracy across three PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), and extends to constrained adversary settings and de-identification systems.

## Strengths

- **The evaluation paradigm critique is important and convincingly demonstrated.** Table 1 provides a clean counterexample: a CanFG-generated face achieves SSIM=0.841 and PSNR=26.81 relative to the original, yet FaceSimilarity=0.008, while two photos of the same person yield SSIM=0.235 but FaceSimilarity=0.586. This directly shows pixel-level similarity ≠ identity-level similarity.

- **Comprehensive empirical evaluation across three PPFR systems, multiple datasets, and two commercial APIs.** Tables 2–5 demonstrate consistent attack success. Critically, Table 5 cross-verifies regeneration pass rates with the Amazon face comparison API on 700 LFW images, confirming Face++ results (0.988–0.992 pass rates), which rules out dependence on any single commercial verifier for the main attack.

- **The constrained-attack extension (Section 6) is a genuine and novel insight.** The observation that all three methods preserve high-frequency information, enabling a single generic Gaussian-blur high-pass filter as a universal proxy, is non-obvious. Training one model to attack all three systems without system-specific knowledge demonstrates that the vulnerability is structural rather than attack-dependent. The 1-to-1 matching accuracy of 0.925–0.962 under constrained assumptions (Table 7) is strong.

- **Extremely low attack cost strengthens the structural-vulnerability argument.** Under $2 and two hours on a single GPU per method (Section 5), with minimal-resource experiments (Section 5.1) showing 97.0% regeneration pass rate with only ~800 training images and <50 seconds of training. If the vulnerability required sophisticated adversarial optimization, the argument would be weaker; the simplicity proves the point.

- **Careful experimental hygiene — no dataset or architecture overlap.** The student model trains on CASIA-WebFace while PartialFace and FracFace are training-free hardcoded transforms and MinusFace trains on MS1Mv2 (Section 5). This rules out memorization as an explanation.

- **Extensions beyond frequency-domain PPFR broaden impact.** Table 8 shows the same distillation pipeline extracts identity from TIP-IM (adversarial de-identification) with linkage accuracy 0.939 and from CanFG (non-frequency, non-adversarial) with linkage accuracy 0.997, suggesting the vulnerability is not limited to one method family.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Abstract does not mention the substantially lower 1-to-N linkage accuracy (70–85%), presenting only the more favorable numbers.** The abstract states "over 98.5% matching accuracy and above 96% regeneration success." The term "matching accuracy" is ambiguous — it could refer to 1-to-1 verification (Table 3: 0.92–0.99) or regeneration pass rate (Table 5: 0.988–0.992) — but in either interpretation, the abstract omits the 1-to-N linkage numbers (Table 4: 0.72–0.86). Since the paper's own threat model (Section 4.1) identifies linkage as the primary attack vector ("A linkage attack aims to associate a real-world identity with a protected identity"), the absence of these numbers from the abstract creates a misleading impression of near-perfect attack success. The 1-to-N linkage is the more realistic and practically relevant metric (finding a person in a database vs. confirming two templates match), and its omission from the abstract is a significant framing issue. The paper does fairly report these numbers in Table 4 and discusses the ~0.88 dataset noise ceiling, so the data is honest — the problem is the abstract's selective reporting.

### Minor

- **Face++/Amazon API discrepancy in the constrained setting (Table 7) deserves deeper analysis.** In the constrained attack, Face++ reports 94–96% regeneration Success@5 while Amazon API reports only 44–57% on the same attack. The paper attributes this to Amazon being "likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images" in one sentence. This gap matters because it raises the question of whether the constrained-attack regeneration success is robust or API-dependent. Importantly, this does NOT undermine the main attack: Table 5 confirms both APIs agree for the oracle-access setting (0.988–0.992). But for the constrained setting specifically, understanding why the two APIs diverge so sharply would strengthen the paper — is it sensitivity to AI-generated artifacts, different threshold calibrations, or something else? The 1-to-1 matching accuracy (0.925–0.962) is independent of regeneration and remains high regardless.

- **"Over 98.5% matching accuracy" claim is ambiguous and slightly misleading for FracFace under one interpretation.** If "matching accuracy" refers to 1-to-1 verification accuracy (Table 3), FracFace achieves only 0.92 on LFW, not "over 98.5%." If it refers to regeneration pass rate (Table 5), all three methods exceed 98.5%. The paper never explicitly defines which metric the abstract references, creating unnecessary ambiguity. A clarifying phrase (e.g., "regeneration matching accuracy") would resolve this.

- **The oracle-access attack, while valid for the stated threat model, is somewhat expected.** If a PPFR system preserves identity discriminability for recognition utility, and the attacker has oracle access to the conversion process, distillation can extract identity information almost by definition. The paper acknowledges this ("if a standard procedure suffices, the vulnerability is in the representation"), and the conceptual point about the evaluation paradigm is valid regardless. But the practical novelty of the oracle-access results is limited — the constrained attack (Section 6) is where the paper makes its strongest practical contribution.

- **Minimal-resource experiment (Section 5.1) tests only FracFace.** Showing that the attack works with limited data on all three methods would more convincingly demonstrate that the vulnerability is general rather than FracFace-specific. Since FracFace is a training-free hardcoded transform, it may be the easiest target; demonstrating minimal-resource success on MinusFace (which involves a trained model) would be more compelling.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Open-set linkage evaluation with impostor templates and a reject option would better reflect realistic attack scenarios than the current closed-set 1-to-N evaluation.
- Failure-case analysis for regeneration (what do failed cases look like? Do they correlate with specific demographics or poses?) would add depth.
- Evaluation against a cryptographically hardened PPFR system (e.g., CryptoFace [3], cited by the paper itself) would clarify whether the vulnerability is specific to frequency-domain methods or fundamental to any utility-preserving transformation.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:

- **Harsh critic claim #1: "Regeneration success evaluated by a single commercial API (Face++), with known discrepancy unexplained; no Amazon API regeneration results for the main attack."** This is factually incorrect. Table 5 explicitly provides Amazon API regeneration pass rates for the main oracle-access attack (0.988–0.992), and the paper states "The values are close to the Face++ results, validating our claims." The Face++/Amazon discrepancy appears only in the constrained setting (Table 7), not in the main attack.

- **Harsh critic: "Oracle-access threat model makes the attack tautological."** The paper explicitly acknowledges this and frames it as the core insight: the vulnerability is structural to any system that preserves identity for utility. The conceptual contribution (evaluation paradigm critique) is valid regardless, and the constrained attack goes beyond the oracle-access setting.

- **Harsh critic: "Abstract conflates 1-to-1 verification with linkage."** While the abstract's selective reporting of favorable numbers is a valid concern (kept as a Major weakness above), characterizing this as "conflating" overstates the issue. The paper clearly reports both metrics in the body and discusses their differences. The problem is omission from the abstract, not conflation.

- **Harsh critic: "Missing Amazon API regeneration results for the main attack."** Removed as factually wrong — see Table 5.

- **Harsh critic: "TIP-IM evaluated on only 2,082 images from 408 identities—too small for strong generalization."** The paper explicitly labels this as a "pilot evaluation" and uses tentative language ("preliminary results suggesting"). The sample size is adequate for a pilot study.

- **Harsh critic: "CanFG evaluation conflates two different threat models."** The paper separately discusses oracle-access vulnerability and deterministic-transformation vulnerability for CanFG; these are not conflated but presented as two distinct reasons CanFG is vulnerable.

- **Harsh critic: "Soft biometrics finding is known."** The paper cites [18, 23] for the ArcFace embedding property and contributes the specific demonstration that soft biometrics survive the PPFR transformation pipeline, challenging FracFace's rebuttal claims. This is a new empirical finding even if the underlying ArcFace property is known.

- **Strength finder: "Cross-verification using independent commercial APIs avoids self-serving evaluation."** While true, this is partially undermined by the constrained-setting discrepancy (Table 7), which shows the two APIs can disagree substantially. The strength is valid for the main attack but not uniformly across all settings. Kept as a qualified strength.

## Novel Insights

The most important insight that emerges from synthesizing the reviews with the paper is that the Face++/Amazon API discrepancy in the constrained setting (Table 7) may reveal something deeper about PPFR evaluation than the paper explores. The fact that both APIs agree for the oracle-access attack but diverge for the constrained attack suggests that the quality of the regenerated image (not just its identity content) affects verification differently across APIs. Oracle-access regeneration produces higher-fidelity identity embeddings (the student model is well-trained), while constrained-access regeneration via the high-pass proxy produces noisier embeddings, and the resulting AI-generated images may exhibit artifacts that Amazon's API detects but Face++ does not. This implies that attack success under constrained assumptions is more sensitive to the choice of verification oracle, which in turn suggests that the practical threat level of constrained attacks may be lower than the Face++ numbers alone indicate — but still concerning given the consistently high 1-to-1 matching accuracy.

## Suggestions

- Rewrite the abstract to include the 1-to-N linkage accuracy range (70–85%) alongside the regeneration/matching numbers, and clarify what "matching accuracy" refers to. For example: "FaceLinkGen achieves over 98.5% regeneration matching accuracy and above 96% regeneration success, with closed-set 1-to-N linkage accuracy of 70–85% approaching the dataset ceiling of 88%."
- Investigate the Face++/Amazon discrepancy in the constrained setting more thoroughly: test whether Amazon's lower rates are due to sensitivity to AI-generated image artifacts by evaluating with a non-generative verification method (e.g., direct ArcFace cosine similarity thresholds, as partially done in Section 7).
- Extend the minimal-resource experiment (Section 5.1) to all three PPFR methods, not just FracFace, to demonstrate generalizability of the low-resource vulnerability.
