# Review of ../paper.md

## Summary

This paper argues that the prevailing evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR) — which measures privacy as resistance to pixel-level reconstruction (PSNR/SSIM) — fails to capture identity-level leakage. The authors present FaceLinkGen, a distillation-based attack that extracts identity embeddings from protected templates and uses a diffusion model (Arc2Face) to regenerate identity-consistent faces without recovering original pixels. Across three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), the attack achieves over 98.5% matching accuracy and above 96% regeneration success, revealing a fundamental gap between pixel-distortion metrics and actual identity privacy.

## Strengths

- **Important conceptual contribution that challenges an established evaluation paradigm.** The paper clearly demonstrates that preventing pixel-level reconstruction does not prevent identity leakage (Table 1, Figure 2), and that channel-protection metrics used by prior work (e.g., FracFace's "Protection %") are fundamentally misaligned with identity-privacy goals. This is a substantive critique that, if correct, invalidates a core assumption in a significant body of recent PPFR work.

- **Strong, multi-faceted empirical evidence.** Results are consistent across three PPFR methods, three datasets (TPDNE, CASIA-WebFace hold-out, LFW), two commercial verification APIs (Face++ and Amazon), and ArcFace cosine-similarity analysis (Section 7). The minimal-resource experiments (Section 5.1: 256 images, 50 seconds of training; Section 6: generic high-pass proxy with only 30 paired validation samples) make it very hard to dismiss the vulnerability as requiring sophisticated attacks.

- **Deliberate simplicity strengthens the message.** The attack pipeline — standard knowledge distillation plus Arc2Face generation — is intentionally simple. As the paper argues, if a straightforward procedure suffices, "the vulnerability is in the representation," not in attack sophistication. The estimated cost of ~$1 per method and <2 hours training on a single GPU makes this compelling.

- **Well-justified threat model.** The paper returns to the original PPFR adversary (untrusted service provider with oracle access) and explicitly defends this choice (Section 2), including arguments about why locally-running models can be reverse-engineered. The constrained-attacker extension (Section 6) tests weaker assumptions and still shows substantial leakage.

- **Extension to soft biometrics (Section 9).** The FairFace evaluation showing that race (67.3% vs. 70.0%), gender (92.5% vs. 94.9%), and age (4.7 vs. 4.6 MAE) are nearly as recoverable from protected templates as from original images directly challenges published claims that these attributes are adequately obfuscated.

- **Breadth beyond frequency-domain PPFR.** Pilot evaluations on TIP-IM (adversarial de-identification) and CanFG (non-frequency PPFR/de-ID) in Section 8 suggest the vulnerability class extends beyond the primary target methods, even if these extensions are preliminary.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The regeneration attack's dependence on a compatible generative model limits the generality of the regeneration vector.** The pipeline requires that the extracted embedding space be compatible with a generative model — specifically, Arc2Face accepts ArcFace embeddings. The paper briefly notes that "other embedding models like FaceNet can also be used as long as there is a compatible generative model," but does not test this or discuss what happens when no such generative model exists for a given embedding space. The linkage attack remains valid regardless, but the headline "regeneration success" metric is contingent on the ArcFace↔Arc2Face pairing. This matters because the paper's most striking results (96%+ regeneration) depend partly on the maturity of face generation in this particular ecosystem.

- **Generalization claims beyond the three evaluated frequency-domain methods outrun the direct evidence.** The paper's pilot evaluations on TIP-IM and CanFG are conducted on only 2,082 images from 408 identities (Table 8), and the paper states these are "pilot" results. Yet the conclusion claims that "identity information can be extracted from protected templates via a straightforward distillation pipeline" without adequate hedging about the scope. Claims like "the same learning-based pipeline suffices to recover this information in both settings" (Section 8) extend beyond what the experimental evidence for those particular methods supports. The core vulnerability of the three main PPFR systems is convincingly demonstrated; the broader claim needs more evidence.

- **The gap between Face++ and Amazon API results raises questions about the stability of the headline numbers.** Table 5 shows Face++ Success@5 of 98.8–99.2% for LFW but Amazon API pass rates of only 44.7–57.0% (Table 7, constrained setting). While leakage is clear under any reading, this substantial gap means the specific quantitative claims (e.g., ">96% regeneration success") depend on which commercial verifier is used. The ArcFace cosine-similarity analysis (Section 7) helps corroborate the existence of leakage, but the magnitude of regeneration success is less stable than the headline suggests.

### Minor

- **The closed-set 1-to-N linkage results (Table 4), while strong, are bounded by CASIA-WebFace's known 9.3–13.0% label noise.** The paper acknowledges this, noting that "perfect linkage is impossible regardless of method," but does not provide a controlled experiment on a cleaner dataset or directly measure the noise ceiling. A cleaner dataset would more precisely quantify the information loss from the protection transformation itself, separate from dataset quality. That said, the linkage results are far above random and near the theoretical upper bound, so this does not undermine the core conclusion.

- **No evaluation of methods designed with cryptographic or key-based protection (e.g., CryptoFace), which are the most natural defense against this attack class.** The paper's Section 10 suggests cryptographic approaches as future defenses but does not evaluate them. This is understandable given scope — the paper's target is current frequency-domain PPFR — but it means the claim that "templates preserving recognition utility leak identity" is empirically confirmed only for non-cryptographic PPFR methods, not for the entire design space.

- **The "constrained attacker" scenario (Section 6) still assumes the attacker can obtain 30 paired image–template samples.** While this is weaker than full oracle access, it does not cover the pure database-breach scenario where an attacker obtains only leaktemplates with no paired images. The paper's language occasionally implies broader applicability than the experiment strictly supports.

### Trivial

- None worth flagging.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Testing against at least one method with a fundamentally different protection mechanism (e.g., cryptographic PPFR like CryptoFace) to clarify the boundary conditions of the vulnerability claim.
- Expanding the TIP-IM and CanFG evaluations beyond the pilot scale to strengthen generalization claims.
- Adding failure-case analysis: with 2–4% regeneration failure rates, understanding which identities or image conditions resist the attack could inform defense design.
- Formal or empirical analysis bounding the information-theoretic relationship between recognition accuracy and identity extractability, to move the "utility implies leakage" claim from assertion to finding.

## Removed Points

- **Formatting issues from PDF extraction** (e.g., broken equation numbering, misplaced table references): These are parser artifacts, not paper problems.
- **Missing error bars / standard deviations** across tables: The stochastic nature of generation is addressed by reporting Success@5 (5 trials), and the margins are so large that variance reporting would not change any conclusion. This is a standard practice concern, not a substantive weakness.
- **"No comparison with prior reconstruction/inversion attacks using identity-centric metrics":** The paper explicitly compares against FracFace's own evaluation protocol (Table 6) and demonstrates that its identity-centric metric reveals what pixel-level metrics miss. The paper's contribution is the evaluation paradigm shift, not outperforming prior attacks on a shared benchmark.
- **"No empirical evaluation of defenses":** This is scope creep. The paper identifies and validates a vulnerability; designing and testing defenses is a separate and substantial contribution. The paper appropriately points to future directions.
- **"Insufficient novelty of the attack pipeline itself":** The paper is transparent that the simplicity is by design ("if a standard procedure suffices, the vulnerability is in the representation"). The contribution is the evaluation paradigm insight and the empirical demonstration, not the attack mechanism.
- **Criticisms about the practicality of the threat model or claiming the oracle-access assumption is unrealistic**: The paper's Section 2 provides an extensive and explicit justification for the insider threat model, which aligns with the original PPFR motivation (Erkin et al., 2009). The constrained scenario in Section 6 further relaxes assumptions.

## Novel Insights

The paper's central insight — that pixel-level reconstruction resistance and identity-level privacy protection are fundamentally decoupled in frequency-domain PPFR — is both novel and significant. Table 1 and Figure 2 make this viscerally clear: a CanFG-generated face has high PSNR/SSIM (26.81/0.841) but near-zero identity similarity (FS=0.008), while two photos of the same person have low pixel similarity but high identity similarity. This single observation reframes how the community should evaluate PPFR, and the empirical demonstration that >96% regeneration success is achievable with <$2 of compute across three systems makes it impossible to dismiss. The secondary insight that all three frequency-domain methods share a common structural vulnerability — high-frequency information preservation — enabling a single generic proxy attack (Section 6) is also notable and suggests the problem is architectural to this method family.

## Suggestions

- **Hedge generalization claims more carefully.** Change language like "the same pipeline suffices to recover this information in both settings" to accurately reflect that TIP-IM and CanFG are pilot demonstrations with limited evaluation scale.
- **Report similarity distributions, not just thresholds.** For both Face++ and Amazon APIs, reporting confidence score distributions (or ROC curves) would clarify how close to decision boundaries the regenerated identities fall, making the regeneration success metric more informative.
- **Test at least one alternative teacher–generator pair** (e.g., FaceNet + compatible generator) even on a subset, to demonstrate that the regeneration attack is not an artifact of the ArcFace↔Arc2Face ecosystem.

This paper is stronger than "KAN See Your Face" (much more thorough evaluation and a genuine paradigmatic contribution), stronger than "A False Sense of Privacy" (far more compelling empirical results with >96% success rates), and at least comparable to "On the Inadequacy of Similarity-based Privacy Metrics" (which scored up to 8 from some reviewers for the conceptual contribution). The main weaknesses — limited attack novelty, dependence on ArcFace↔Arc2Face for headline regeneration numbers, and pilot-scale generalization evidence — are real but do not undermine the core finding that frequency-domain PPFR methods evaluated under pixel-reconstruction metrics provide far less identity privacy than claimed. The conceptual insight + strong empirical demonstration warrant a score above average but not exceptional due to the technical simplicity and incomplete generalization evidence.
