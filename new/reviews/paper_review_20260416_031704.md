## Summary
This paper argues that pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM) inadequately capture identity-level privacy in frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR) systems, and introduces FaceLinkGen—an identity-centric attack using distillation to extract identity embeddings from protected templates and regenerate identity-consistent faces via a diffusion model. FaceLinkGen achieves >98.5% 1-to-1 matching accuracy and >96% regeneration success on three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), revealing that these methods preserve far more machine-readable identity information than their pixel-level evaluation metrics imply.

## Strengths
- **Compelling conceptual reframing with concrete evidence.** The core insight—that pixel-level similarity ≠ identity-level similarity—is effectively demonstrated through Table 1 (CanFG face: SSIM=0.841, identity FS=0.008 vs. same-person photos: SSIM=0.235, identity FS=0.586). This single example powerfully grounds the entire argument and exposes a genuine evaluation gap in PPFR research.
- **Intentionally simple attack design strengthens the core claim.** By using standard distillation + off-the-shelf Arc2Face rather than complex adversarial optimization, the paper demonstrates the vulnerability resides in the representation itself, not in attack sophistication. The extreme low cost (~$0.80–1.60 for distillation, <$0.001 per identity generation) makes the threat highly practical.
- **Comprehensive multi-faceted evaluation.** The paper evaluates three PPFR methods across three datasets, uses two independent commercial APIs (Face++, Amazon), tests linkage and regeneration attack vectors, examines minimal-resource settings (down to 256 images), constrained-assumption attacks (Section 6), soft-biometric leakage (Section 9), and extends to de-identification (TIP-IM, CanFG). This breadth makes the findings difficult to dismiss.
- **Thoughtful threat model analysis.** Section 2 provides a grounded return to first principles, systematically arguing for the insider/service-provider adversary as the appropriate PPFR threat model and explaining why alternative framings (network eavesdropper, trusted third party) fail or merely shift the trust problem.
- **Constrained-attacker results demonstrate genuine vulnerability beyond oracle access.** Section 6 shows that even without knowledge of the conversion process—using only a generic Gaussian-blur high-pass filter and 30 validation pairs—>92% matching accuracy and >94% regeneration success (Face++) are achieved. This is the most surprising and valuable result, proving the leakage is not merely an artifact of strong attacker assumptions.

## Weaknesses

### Major:
- **The fundamental utility–privacy tension is under-addressed, leading to overclaiming.** PPFR systems must preserve identity-discriminative information for recognition to function. The paper demonstrates that when such information is preserved, it can be extracted via distillation—but this somewhat reflects an inherent design tension rather than a specific flaw in these three methods. The paper correctly shows that pixel-level metrics underestimate leakage, but occasionally overstates its conclusions (e.g., "these methods do not provide the level of identity privacy their pixel-level evaluation results imply" is fair; implying they are fundamentally broken is not). The most consequential claim the paper could make—that any utility-preserving PPFR template must leak identity information to a service provider with oracle access—is hinted at (Section 10 briefly mentions cryptographic hardening) but never rigorously analyzed. Without this analysis, the reader cannot distinguish "these specific methods are poorly designed" from "the entire PPFR paradigm has inherent limitations."

- **Heavy dependence on the ArcFace/Arc2Face ecosystem.** The entire pipeline—teacher model (ArcFace/Antelopev2), student training target, generative model (Arc2Face), and soft-biometric inference—is anchored in one embedding family. Face++ and Amazon APIs provide cross-verification, but their correlation with ArcFace-style embeddings is unknown (likely high, given shared training paradigms and datasets). The paper acknowledges that "other embedding models like FaceNet can also be used as long as there is a compatible generative model" but provides no empirical validation. For a paper fundamentally about *how to measure privacy*, this tight method-evaluation coupling is a meaningful evidential limitation. At minimum, one alternative embedding/generation pipeline would strengthen the generality claim.

- **Abstract conflates 1-to-1 verification (~99%) with linkage accuracy (72–86%).** The abstract's "over 98.5% matching accuracy" refers to 1-to-1 verification (Table 3), not the more practically significant closed-set 1-to-N linkage (Table 4: 72–86%). These address different threat models with different real-world implications. 1-to-1 verification tests whether an attacker can confirm a candidate identity; linkage tests whether they can identify an unknown person from a database. The abstract should report the most operationally significant result accurately, or clearly distinguish both.

### Minor:
- **No head-to-head comparison with prior reconstruction attacks.** The paper argues pixel-level attacks fail while identity-centric attacks succeed, but provides no direct comparison on the same templates. Showing that U-Net/StyleGAN inversion achieves low identity recovery while FaceLinkGen achieves high recovery on identical inputs would make the core argument directly persuasive.

- **Amazon API results show substantially lower success rates (44–57%) than Face++ (94–96%) in the constrained setting (Table 7).** This gap is mentioned only briefly ("likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images") but deserves deeper analysis: if one commercial verifier detects the attack significantly better, understanding why is important for characterizing practical severity.

- **TIP-IM and CanFG extensions are under-specified.** Section 8 reports only cosine similarity and linkage accuracy without the richer regeneration analysis (Face++/Amazon verification, Success@5) used in the main experiments. The claim that adversarial de-identification is "almost completely undermined" is too strong for the evidence presented.

### Trivial:
- The FracFace rebuttal discussion (Section 9) compares their machine-learning inference against FracFace's human-perception claim about soft-biometric obfuscation; while the point is valid, a fair comparison would require a human study on regenerated faces.

## Nice-to-Haves
- An information-theoretic or empirical analysis of how much identity information must be preserved for a given recognition accuracy level, helping clarify whether the vulnerability is method-specific or paradigm-inherent.
- Evaluation with at least one alternative embedding/generation pipeline (e.g., FaceNet + compatible generator) to test ArcFace-specificity.
- Failure case analysis of the 2–10% where the attack fails, which could reveal whether certain identities, image conditions, or protection strategies partially resist extraction.
- A brief discussion of ethical considerations and responsible disclosure, since the paper presents an effective attack on privacy-preserving systems.

## Novel Insights
The most insightful finding is the asymmetry in Section 7: across all three PPFR methods, the cosine similarity between an image and its own protected template *exceeds* the similarity between two different images of the same person. This directly inverts the intended privacy guarantee—rather than anonymizing identity, the template acts as a *better* identity descriptor than a second photo. Combined with the constrained-attacker results showing that a generic high-pass filter (with no system-specific knowledge) suffices for >92% attack success, this reveals that the vulnerability stems from a common structural property of frequency-domain PPFR (high-frequency preservation) rather than from specific implementation flaws.

## Suggestions
- Reframe the abstract to clearly distinguish 1-to-1 verification (~99%) from linkage accuracy (72–86%) and report both, emphasizing the more operationally significant linkage result.
- Add at least one experiment with a different teacher/generator pipeline (even a negative result would clarify whether the vulnerability is ArcFace-specific or general).
- Develop the constrained-attacker section (Section 6) more fully—it is the most surprising and impactful result but is treated as supplementary. Expand with ablations on proxy mismatch, per-identity variation, and deeper quantitative detail.
- Provide a brief analysis (even preliminary) of what formal properties a template would need to resist identity extraction while maintaining utility, connecting to the cryptographic approaches mentioned in Section 10.
