## Summary

The paper proposes "Convex Distillation," a knowledge distillation framework that replaces non-convex blocks in a pre-trained teacher model with Gated ReLU (GReLU) student blocks whose training objective is convex (via fixed gates). The key claims are: (1) label-free, fine-tuning-free block-level compression; (2) superior performance over non-convex distillation in data-scarce and high-compression regimes; and (3) significantly faster convergence via specialized convex solvers. The method is applied block-wise to ResNet-18 on SVHN, CIFAR-10, TinyImageNet, and Visual Wake Words.

---

## Strengths

- **Novel combination of convex NN theory and practical distillation**: To the knowledge of the reviewers, this is the first work to leverage the Pilanci/Ergen/Mishkin convex reformulation of GReLU networks specifically for knowledge distillation, making the bridge between convex optimization theory and practical model compression a genuine contribution.
- **Label-free, fine-tuning-free deployment**: The activation-matching objective (Eq. 6) depends only on intermediate activations—not labels—and the distilled block plugs directly back into the full model without any downstream fine-tuning. This is cleanly demonstrated across datasets.
- **Strong results in extremely constrained regimes**: Figure 6 convincingly shows that at 1–25 samples/class, convex distillation (SCNN/R-FISTA) achieves meaningfully higher test accuracy than Adam-based non-convex distillation within the same time budget. Figure 3 shows a consistent advantage at high compression rates.
- **Speed advantage is real in tested regime**: Figure 5 demonstrates 1–2 orders of magnitude speed advantage for convex solvers over Adam on the TinyImageNet binary task, with 10-seed uncertainty estimates showing this is robust.
- **Polishing step (Section 4.3)**: The group-elastic regression post-processing (using Adelie) to recover cross-output information sharing lost by the SCNN one-vs-all solver is a sensible and novel engineering contribution.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None — the paper's core idea (convex GReLU distillation) is sound and supported by legitimate theory and at least some experiments.

### Major

- **Internal inconsistency between Section 5.3 text and Figure 7**: Section 5.3 explicitly states that "Figure 7 shows that even for relaxed resource constraints, convex optimization based distillation performs *at least as good* as Adam-based non-convex block distillation." However, Figure 7 shows the opposite: Non-Convex Acc consistently and noticeably exceeds Convex Acc at every tested data size (10–100 samples/class), with non-convex reaching ~88% and convex reaching only ~85% at 100 samples/class. The paper then concludes with "we *believe* that convex distillation would outperform non-convex distillation if S comprised CNN layers instead of linear layers" — a speculation explicitly triggered by a result that contradicts the claimed advantage. The abstract's claim of "outperforming standard non-convex distillation approaches" is defensible when limited to Figure 6's highly constrained setting (1–25 samples/class, fixed time budget), but Section 5.3's framing of Figure 7 is an outright misrepresentation of that figure. The paper's claimed performance regime needs to be more carefully scoped.

- **Confound between convex architecture and reduced trainable parameter count**: The paper explicitly acknowledges that for the same number of filters, `#θ_non-convex ≈ 2 × #θ_convex` (Section 5.1), because CNN₁ provides only a boolean mask with no gradient. It then adjusts filter counts so that total parameter counts match across methods. However, this means S_convex has roughly half as many *trainable* parameters as S_non-convex for the same total model size (CNN₁ is frozen in S_convex, while all weights are trainable in S_non-convex). In data-scarce regimes — precisely the regimes where the paper claims convex distillation is superior (Figures 3, 6) — fewer trainable parameters independently reduces overfitting. The paper never ablates this confound (e.g., comparing a non-convex student regularized to have the same effective trainable degrees of freedom, or comparing a non-convex student with matched trainable parameter count). Attributing the observed advantage to *convexity of the optimization landscape* rather than *reduced overfitting from fewer trainable parameters* is therefore unsupported by the experimental design.

- **Speed claim is tied to a dataset regime structurally favorable to full-batch solvers**: R-FISTA is a full-batch algorithm whose per-iteration cost scales with the number of samples. Figure 5 uses 500 samples/class and Figure 6 uses 1–25 samples/class — regimes where full-batch methods have a wall-clock advantage that reverses at realistic scales (e.g., CIFAR-10's 50K training images). No evidence is given that the 1–2 orders of magnitude convergence speed advantage persists at scales beyond the few hundred samples/class tested. This limits the scope of the edge-deployment claims in the abstract and contributions.

### Minor

- **Missing statistical characterization of main results (Figures 3, 4, 7)**: Accuracy differences of 1–3% are asserted as meaningful in Figures 3 and 4, but these are single-run (or unreported-seed) results on SVHN and CIFAR-10. Only Figure 5 reports error bands (10 seeds). Given that ResNet-18 fine-tuning on these datasets has non-trivial variance across seeds, the absence of error bars makes it impossible to verify whether observed differences are signal or noise.

- **Gate initialization for CNN₁ is unspecified**: The convex equivalence theorems (Theorems 2, 3) require gates to be sampled from the data-dependent hyperplane arrangement D_X. The paper never specifies how CNN₁ is initialized in the actual experiments — whether by random initialization, teacher-derived activations, or a data-dependent sampling procedure. This directly affects whether the theoretical convex equivalence applies or whether the architecture is merely an approximation of it. The paper never acknowledges this gap between theory and implementation.

- **Visual Wake Words ablation (Table 2) is below statistical resolution**: Accuracy differences of 0.52% (frozen backbone) and 0.05% (trainable backbone) over ~8K test images are unlikely to be statistically significant without confidence intervals. Presenting this section as evidence that "convex NN architectures are good candidates for DNN applications beyond just distillation" goes beyond what these results support.

### Trivial

- Section 5.3 ends with "we believe that here convex distillation approach would outperform non-convex distillation if S comprised of CNN layers instead of linear layers" — this should either be tested or clearly moved to a "Limitations" or "Future Work" section rather than appearing as the closing statement of an experimental section.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Ablation comparing non-convex student with the same number of *trainable* (not total) parameters as S_convex, to isolate the effect of convexity from regularization-via-fewer-trainable-params.
- Speed comparisons at larger data scales (≥10K samples) to bound the regime of the convergence advantage.
- Characterization of how CNN₁ gate initialization (random vs. teacher-derived vs. data-dependent) affects distillation quality — this would directly connect the empirical approach to the theory.
- Training loss curves (not just test accuracy vs. time) to provide direct evidence about the optimization landscape differences claimed in Section 4.2.
- Application to at least one transformer block as a proof-of-concept for the LLM/VLM claims in the introduction.

## Removed Points

*These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution:*

- **"Convexity of S conflates convex training with convex function class" (Harsh Critic)**: The paper's own Section 4.1 cites Sahiner et al. to ground the claim that all local minima of the Burer-Monteiro factorization are globally optimal (not that f_S is a convex function of its input). The paper is careful to claim convexity of the *training problem*, not of the function class. This criticism misreads what the paper claims.

- **Reviewer concern about non-convex baseline having more parameters than convex (Harsh Critic)**: The paper explicitly adjusts filter counts to equalize total parameter counts, which is the standard fairness criterion in compression benchmarks. The residual trainable-parameter confound is valid and listed above as Major, but the blanket claim that the comparison is unfair is overstated.

- **"Label-free advantage overstated because experiments use training-set images"**: The distillation objective does use training images (without labels), but using unlabeled images is genuine and the paper never claims to use no images. The comparison to synthetic-data-based methods is outside the stated scope.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel contribution is the observation that the fixed-gate GReLU architecture, which makes the distillation problem convex, simultaneously serves as a natural implicit regularizer in data-scarce regimes by reducing the effective number of trainable parameters. This creates a regime-dependent advantage: when labeled data and compute are severely limited, the convex solver finds a better solution faster; when relaxed, non-convex methods (with their larger effective trainable parameter space) catch up. The paper identifies this regime dependence empirically but does not fully theorize or experimentally disentangle it. Making this the explicit framing—rather than the broader claim that convex distillation "outperforms" non-convex distillation—would make the paper's contribution cleaner and more defensible.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix the Section 5.3 / Figure 7 contradiction**: Either revise the text to acknowledge that Figure 7 shows non-convex winning, or add experiments that genuinely demonstrate convex parity/advantage in the Figure 7 regime (e.g., using CNN-based S_convex rather than MLP-based SCNN). Do not describe a figure as showing "at least as good" when it shows the opposite.

2. **Add a trainable-parameter-matched ablation**: Compare S_non-convex trained with only a subset of its parameters active (e.g., freeze a CNN layer analogously to how CNN₁ is frozen in S_convex) to isolate the optimization landscape effect from the regularization effect.

3. **Report standard deviations for Figures 3, 4, 7**: Run at least 3–5 seeds for the main SVHN and CIFAR-10 distillation experiments so that accuracy differences of 1–3% can be assessed for statistical significance.

4. **State CNN₁ initialization explicitly**: Specify whether CNN₁ is randomly initialized or otherwise and discuss how this affects the theoretical connection to the convex equivalence theorems.

5. **Scope the claims more precisely**: Restrict the headline claim to the empirically supported regime (extremely low data: <25 samples/class; time-limited; high compression) rather than making broad superiority claims that the experiments do not universally support.
