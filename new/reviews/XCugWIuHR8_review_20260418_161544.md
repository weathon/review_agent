## Summary

The paper introduces *Convex Distillation*, a knowledge distillation technique that replaces non-convex blocks in a pretrained teacher network with convex gated-ReLU (GReLU) student blocks trained via activation matching. The central appeal is threefold: the student training objective is label-free (depends only on activation matching, not output labels), no post-compression fine-tuning is required, and the convexity of the student opens the door to efficient specialized convex solvers. Experiments span SVHN, CIFAR-10, TinyImageNet, and Visual Wake Words using ResNet-18 and MobileNetV3.

---

## Strengths

- **Label-free, no-fine-tuning distillation (verified, Eq. 6 and Figures 3–7):** The activation-matching objective $\mathcal{L}_\text{distill} = \sum \|f_T(\mathbf{z}) - f_S(\mathbf{z})\|_2^2$ has zero label dependency. All experiments confirm that the student is dropped in directly with frozen surrounding layers — a genuinely useful property for settings where labeled data is scarce or unavailable.

- **Demonstrated advantage in extreme data-scarcity and high-compression regimes (Figures 3a, 3b, 6):** In Section 5.1, $S_\text{convex}$ clearly outperforms $S_\text{non-convex}$ in the low filter-count regime on SVHN (Figure 3a) and with 100 samples/class on CIFAR-10 (Figure 3b). Figure 6 (SCNN solver) further confirms this at 1/10/25 samples/class, constituting meaningful empirical support for the abstract's "can outperform" claim in data-limited settings. These results are backed by the theoretical rationale that the convex landscape avoids spurious local minima.

- **Substantial compression without accuracy drop (Figure 4):** Distilling both Blocks 3 and 4 of ResNet-18 achieves approximately 10× overall model compression with no significant accuracy drop on the full CIFAR-10 test set, validated end-to-end without fine-tuning.

- **Principled regularization path via convex solver (Figure 6):** The use of SCNN's regularization path provides a principled, optimizer-driven mechanism for sweeping the accuracy–compression trade-off curve, which is more principled than manually tuning filter counts.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Speed comparison confounds problem structure with solver choice (Figure 5, Section 5.2):** The "one to two orders of magnitude" speedup shown in Figure 5 compares RFISTA/Approximate Cone Decomposition (convex) against full-batch and mini-batch Adam (non-convex). FISTA-class methods with line search and warm-start are inherently faster than Adam for a wide class of problems regardless of convexity. The paper never establishes that a well-tuned non-convex solver (e.g., L-BFGS, or FISTA applied to the non-convex objective) would be slower. This makes it impossible to attribute the speed advantage to convexity itself rather than to the solver choice. This matters because the entire "efficient edge deployment" and "on-device learning" framing of the paper rests on this speedup being an intrinsic property of the convex formulation.

- **Disconnected experimental streams undermine the central promise (Sections 5.1 vs. 5.2–5.3):** Section 5.1 uses CNN-based GReLU blocks trained with Adam. Sections 5.2–5.3 use 2-layer MLP blocks trained with SCNN. These two streams test categorically different things (convex architecture vs. convex solver), but their results are jointly cited as evidence for "Convex Distillation." The combination that the paper's framing implies — a CNN-block student solved with a fast convex solver — is never tested. The authors themselves acknowledge this directly: "Since SCNN only solves the training of 2-layer MLPs, we are constrained by the types of experiments we can do." This is not a minor limitation; it means the paper's headline claim about efficient compression of CNN blocks via convex solvers has no experimental grounding.

- **Section 5.3 claim contradicted by Figure 7:** The text in Section 5.3 concludes that after the polishing step, "convex optimization based distillation performs at least as good as with Adam-based non-convex block distillation." Figure 7 directly contradicts this: non-convex achieves ~82–88% across all 10–100 samples/class tested, while convex achieves only ~75–85%. The polishing step narrows but does not close the gap. This is a clear internal inconsistency between a stated conclusion and its own figure. (Note: the abstract's weaker claim that the method "CAN outperform" non-convex distillation is supported by Figures 3b and 6, so the abstract is acceptable; the Section 5.3 claim is the problem.)

### Minor

- **CNN1 initialization is unspecified (Section 4.1):** The entire convexity argument for the CNN block relies on CNN1's gates being fixed at initialization (no gradient flows back). The paper never specifies how CNN1 is initialized — random Gaussian, teacher-copied activations, or something else. Given that the quality of random gates directly governs the expressivity of the convex approximation (Kim & Pilanci 2024 requires O(n/d · log n) Gaussian gates), this is a meaningful reproducibility gap and a potential explanation for why results vary across regimes.

- **Parameter accounting for CNN1 in edge deployment context (Section 5.1 / Table 1):** CNN1 holds parameters in memory at inference time even though it receives no gradient during training. The "overall model compression" numbers in Table 1 may not account for this. For edge deployment where memory is the primary constraint, this matters.

- **Table 2 (VWW) results reported without variance estimates:** The differences of 0.52% (frozen backbone) and 0.05% (trainable backbone) between convex and non-convex heads are well within the variance of typical training runs. Without confidence intervals or multi-seed statistics, no reliable conclusion can be drawn from these numbers.

### Trivial

- The paper omits training set experiments at higher sample counts in the MLP/SCNN regime (Figure 6 stops at 25 samples/class), leaving open the question of how performance evolves with more data — a question Figure 7 partially but not cleanly answers.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation over CNN1 initialization strategies (random vs. teacher-guided vs. structured Gaussian) would substantiate the theoretical O(n/d · log n) gate sufficiency condition and improve reproducibility.
- A single experiment applying a convex solver to a CNN-based student block (even at small scale) would close the critical gap between the two experimental streams and directly validate the paper's main framing.
- Experiments on transformer-based architectures (e.g., ViT, DINO) or larger-scale models (ResNet-50, EfficientNet) would substantially strengthen the generalization claim for edge deployment.
- Optimization loss curves for Adam-trained convex vs. non-convex students would make the landscape argument concrete rather than theoretical.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most genuinely interesting observation — often drowned out by the broader methodology claims — is that fixing CNN1 as a random gate generator and training only CNN2 and CNN3 (a GReLU block) via Adam outperforms a fully trainable ReLU student with the same parameter budget. This suggests that the inductive bias of the gated-linear structure, rather than solver choice alone, provides a favorable training geometry. This is a non-obvious finding that supports the convex NN literature's theoretical predictions in a practical compression setting, and it deserves to be highlighted and rigorously ablated as a standalone empirical contribution.

---

## Suggestions

1. **Isolate the solver effect**: Add a baseline that applies L-BFGS or proximal gradient descent to the *non-convex* student under the same time budget as RFISTA. This is the single most important experiment needed to validate the paper's core speed claim.
2. **Fix the Section 5.3 conclusion text**: Either revise the claim about polishing achieving parity, or explain the discrepancy with Figure 7 (e.g., show that convex achieves better compression with the same accuracy, rather than the same accuracy at all sample counts).
3. **Report multi-seed statistics for Table 2**: Run at least 3–5 seeds and report mean ± std. Small differences need variance estimates.
4. **Specify CNN1 initialization explicitly**: Document whether random Gaussian initialization is used, and verify the O(n/d · log n) gate sufficiency condition is approximately met.
