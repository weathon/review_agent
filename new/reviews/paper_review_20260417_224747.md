## Summary

FaceLinkGen argues that the prevailing evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR)—which measures privacy as resistance to pixel-level reconstruction using PSNR/SSIM—fails to capture identity-level leakage. The authors present a knowledge-distillation pipeline that aligns protected templates to a standard ArcFace embedding space, then use Arc2Face to regenerate identity-consistent faces without recovering original pixels. Evaluated on three open-source frequency-domain PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), the attack achieves high linkage accuracy and regeneration success, with pilot results on an adversarial de-identification method (TIP-IM) and a non-frequency-domain method (CanFG) suggesting broader applicability.

---

## Strengths

- **Motivating example (Table 1)** is among the clearest and most persuasive demonstrations of the core problem: a CanFG-protected face achieves SSIM 0.841 with the original yet has face similarity 0.008, while two genuine photos of the same person share SSIM 0.235 but face similarity 0.586. This single example precisely dismantles the proxy assumption.

- **Carefully reasoned threat model (Section 2).** The paper returns PPFR evaluation to its original intent—the service provider as adversary—and shows that the attacker assumed in several prior works (external eavesdropper) contradicts PPFR's motivating privacy concern. The critique of CanFG's reliance on model secrecy when the service provider is the very adversary is particularly incisive.

- **Multi-modal verification convergence.** Results are consistently validated across Face++ (financial-grade), Amazon API, cosine similarity in ArcFace embedding space (Section 7), and soft-biometric prediction (Section 9). The convergence of four independent evaluation modalities substantially reduces concern that results are artifacts of any single tool.

- **Minimal-resource experiment (Section 5.1).** FracFace achieves 97.0% generation pass rate at FAR 1×10⁻⁵ with only 800 training images; with 256 images, linkage accuracy reaches 98.7%. This is a strong, clean result establishing that the vulnerability is intrinsic to the representation rather than dependent on attacker resources.

- **Table 6 comparison** directly juxtaposes FracFace's claimed protection rate (frequency channel disruption, near 100%) against FaceLinkGen's identity-centric pass rate (near 0% resistance). This is the paper's sharpest empirical point and communicates the core critique more compellingly than any single number.

- **Section 7 (similarity distribution)** is model-agnostic evidence: template-to-source embedding similarity consistently exceeds cross-image same-identity similarity, confirming identity leakage at the representation level independently of Arc2Face or external APIs.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **All three evaluated systems share one design family.** PartialFace, MinusFace, and FracFace all operate by selectively transmitting or subtracting frequency-domain channels. The paper itself notes: *"they all preserve high-frequency information while obfuscating low-frequency information"* (Section 6). This shared design characteristic is what makes the constrained attacker's Gaussian-blur high-pass proxy universally applicable. The three systems are not independent data points—they are three instantiations of the same core principle. The paper's broader implication that identity-centric evaluation should be adopted across PPFR research is important, but the evidence base for this claim is a single design family. The pilot experiments on TIP-IM (a different paradigm) and CanFG are on smaller subsets with less detail, and the paper correctly hedges them as "preliminary." The abstract's focus on "frequency-domain PPFR research" is appropriate; however, stronger generalization claims in the introduction should be correspondingly hedged.

### Minor

- **The attack's generalization to non-ArcFace embedding spaces is claimed but undemonstrated.** Section 3 states: *"Other embedding models like FaceNet can also be used as long as there is a compatible generative model,"* but this is offered as assertion rather than empirical evidence. The attack pipeline is specifically calibrated to ArcFace: the teacher/student use Antelopev2 (an ArcFace variant), and Arc2Face was explicitly designed for ArcFace embeddings. PPFR systems designed to preserve recognition under a different embedding model might behave differently under this attack. This does not invalidate the paper's findings for the evaluated systems—all three were designed for ArcFace-compatible recognition—but the paper should be more careful about the generalization claim to other embedding spaces.

- **The abstract's "over 98.5% matching accuracy" is not clearly grounded.** Table 3 reports 1-to-1 LFW verification accuracy of 0.92 for FracFace, which is substantially below 98.5%. The likely intended source for the 98.5% is the cross-verified accuracy in Table 5 (min value 0.988 for FracFace-to-Face), but Table 5 concerns regeneration pass rates against the Amazon API, not "matching accuracy" in the customary sense. The relationship between Tables 3, 4, and 5 and the headline claim should be clarified to avoid confusion about which metric supports the abstract's statement.

### Trivial

- The Future Directions section (Section 10) proposes cryptographic hardening and perception-consistent de-identification as paths forward, but the discussion remains speculative and does not engage concretely with trade-offs. Fleshing this out slightly would strengthen the paper's contribution to the field beyond diagnosis.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Cross-paradigm evaluation with a fully non-frequency method.** Even one thorough evaluation (comparable in detail to the three main systems) of a method with a fundamentally different design (e.g., cryptographic or depth-map-based) would substantially extend the generalization claim. Negative results here (attack fails) would also be highly informative about what actually constitutes a robust defense.

- **Failure-case analysis.** The paper reports consistently high success rates but does not analyze the cases where the attack fails (~1–4% of regeneration attempts, ~10% at the strictest FAR for FracFace). Identifying whether failures correlate with pose, image quality, identity-specific properties, or dataset noise would clarify the attack's scope and help the community identify which defense properties actually matter.

- **Unified identity-leakage metric.** The paper persuasively argues that PSNR/SSIM are inadequate but stops short of proposing a principled replacement. An informal framework—e.g., linkage accuracy at a fixed FAR, or the embedding-distance gap between same-identity and different-identity pairs—would give future PPFR papers a concrete benchmark to adopt.

- **Expand Table 6 to all three systems.** The paper compares FracFace's protection-rate metric against FaceLinkGen's identity-centric pass rate, showing the disconnect clearly. Extending this direct comparison to PartialFace and MinusFace (which presumably have analogous protection metrics) would make the argument more systematic.

## Novel Insights

The paper's deepest insight is not simply that PPFR systems leak identity—it is that the evaluation paradigm itself is structurally misaligned with the privacy guarantee being claimed. By showing that pixel-level distortion is neither necessary nor sufficient for identity protection (via Table 1's CanFG example), and then demonstrating that a $1–2 distillation pipeline extracts identity from "unrecoverable" templates, the paper reframes the question: the problem is not that existing attacks are insufficiently clever, but that the PPFR community has been measuring the wrong thing. The constrained-attacker analysis (Section 6) adds a further insight: the shared high-frequency preservation property across three independently proposed methods is not coincidental—it is a design requirement imposed by the recognition utility constraint, and it is precisely this utility preservation that makes identity leakage structurally inevitable under current designs. This points toward cryptographic or information-theoretic approaches as the only principled path to decoupling utility from identity exposure.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the abstract's 98.5% matching accuracy figure** with an explicit table reference and a sentence explaining what metric it corresponds to. Consistency with Table 3 (FracFace 0.92) should be acknowledged or resolved.
2. **Add a direct empirical check of cross-embedding-space generalization** (even a brief ablation swapping Antelopev2 for FaceNet or ElasticFace) to ground the generalizability claim in Section 3.
3. **Expand the Table 6 analysis to all three PPFR systems**, not only FracFace, to make the pixel-metric-vs-identity-metric disconnect a systematic finding rather than a single case study.
4. **Strengthen the Future Directions section** by testing at least one simple defense (e.g., additive noise to the template post-conversion, or dimensionality reduction) and reporting whether it degrades recognition utility before it degrades FaceLinkGen's success—even a negative result is informative.
