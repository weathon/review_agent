## Summary

This paper challenges the prevailing evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR) systems by demonstrating that pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM) inadequately capture identity-level privacy leakage. The authors present FaceLinkGen, a distillation-based attack that extracts identity information directly from protected templates without pixel reconstruction, achieving over 98.5% 1-to-1 matching accuracy and 96-100% regeneration success on three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace). The paper provides empirical evidence that preventing pixel reconstruction does not prevent identity extraction, and demonstrates cross-system template unlinkability failures alongside soft-biometric attribute leakage.

## Strengths

- **Clear demonstration of evaluation gap**: The paper empirically establishes that pixel-level metrics can be misleading for privacy evaluation. Table 1 shows CanFG achieves high pixel similarity (SSIM 0.841) to the original image but near-zero identity similarity (FS 0.008), while two different images of the same person have low pixel similarity but high identity similarity. This motivates a fundamental shift in how PPFR privacy is measured.

- **Simple yet effective attack methodology**: The distillation approach (Section 3) uses standard cosine similarity loss to train a student network aligning protected templates to identity embeddings. The extreme efficiency demonstrated in Section 5.1—achieving 98.7% linkage accuracy with only 256 training images and 50 seconds of training—is compelling evidence that the vulnerability is structural to the representations rather than an artifact of complex attack optimization.

- **Cross-system unlinkability evaluation**: Table 4 provides empirical evidence that frequency-domain PPFR methods fail to provide unlinkability across different protection schemes (e.g., FracFace-to-MinusFace linkage achieving 75.37% accuracy). This is a genuine privacy violation for cancelable biometrics that the paper correctly identifies.

- **Validation under constrained assumptions**: Section 6 demonstrates that the vulnerability extends beyond oracle-access scenarios. Using only 30 paired samples for validation and a generic high-pass filter as a proxy task, the attack achieves over 92% matching accuracy and 94% regeneration success across all three target systems, showing the vulnerability is not merely an artifact of perfect attacker knowledge.

- **Independent verification via commercial APIs**: The regeneration attack is validated using Face++ (described as having "financial-grade security standards") and Amazon Rekognition (Section 5.3, Table 5), avoiding circularity concerns from using the same model family for both attack and verification.

- **Direct refutation of defensive claims**: Table 6 contrasts FracFace's claimed protection rates (based on frequency channel disruption) against actual identity leakage measured by FaceLinkGen. While FracFace reports 1.000 protection under its metric, FaceLinkGen achieves 0.015 protection (0.008 with 5 trials), empirically establishing that channel disruption does not prevent identity extraction.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **"Model independence" claim lacks validation**: Section 3 states the attack is "independent of the specific face recognition model employed by the PPFR server," but all experiments use ArcFace/Antelopev2 for both the distillation teacher and verification. The paper provides no evidence that templates optimized for a distinct embedding space (e.g., FaceNet, a proprietary commercial model) remain vulnerable to extraction into the ArcFace domain. Given that embedding spaces can differ significantly (angular vs. cosine metrics, different dimensionalities), this claim requires empirical validation to substantiate its generality.

- **TIP-IM extension conflates distinct threat models**: Section 8 tests FaceLinkGen on TIP-IM, an adversarial dodging method designed to prevent FR systems from recognizing a face. The claim that this "does not constitute a meaningful evaluation of TIP-IM's security claims" has merit—TIP-IM aims to prevent recognition, not to protect templates. The paper acknowledges these are "complementary" goals (Section 8), but the framing as a "transfer" attack implies the vulnerability extends to de-identification methods, which needs clearer distinction from the core PPFR contribution.

### Minor

- **Missing utility-privacy trade-off analysis**: The paper does not report the verification accuracy of the PPFR systems on their intended FR model alongside attack success rates. Without this, readers cannot assess whether the attack succeeds when utility is high, or only when systems are configured for maximum privacy (potentially at the cost of utility). This would clarify whether the vulnerability is fundamental to any useful PPFR system or only to specific configurations.

- **Limited evaluation of non-frequency methods**: While Section 8 tests CanFG and TIP-IM, these evaluations are brief compared to the main frequency-domain analysis. A more systematic comparison would clarify whether the vulnerability is specific to frequency-domain designs or applies broadly to transformation-based PPFR.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, as they are based on reviewer misreadings or scope creep:

- **"Conflation of reconstruction resistance with identity anonymity"**: The Harsh Critic claims the paper misunderstands PPFR security goals. This is incorrect—the paper explicitly acknowledges PPFR systems aim to prevent reconstruction while preserving recognition (Section 1, lines 13-17). The paper's contribution is showing that *evaluating* privacy solely via reconstruction resistance is insufficient, not that PPFR systems claim anonymity they don't deliver. The framing of a "privacy gap" refers to the evaluation gap, not a claimed security guarantee.

- **"Circularity in regeneration attack evaluation"**: The claim that the attack succeeds "by construction" misunderstands the validation methodology. The attack is validated via independent commercial APIs (Face++, Amazon Rekognition) that have no relation to the ArcFace teacher used for distillation. The closed pipeline criticism would only apply if verification used ArcFace directly; it does not.

- **"Ambiguity in 'linkage' threat model"**: The paper explicitly distinguishes face-to-template linkage (intended function) from template-to-template linkage (genuine privacy violation) in Section 4.1: "The first case is referred to as face-to-template linkage, while the second is template-to-template linkage. ISO/IEC 24745 explicitly requires resistance against template-to-template search, but does not address face-to-template search."

- **"Section 8 transfer to de-identification" critique as invalidating TIP-IM security**: The paper acknowledges TIP-IM has different goals and frames this as showing the "structural limitation of adversarial de-identification as a privacy mechanism" when identity can be recovered via alignment to a different embedding space. While this extension is less central than the main PPFR contribution, it is not technically incorrect.

## Novel Insights

The paper's core insight—that pixel reconstruction and identity leakage should be treated as distinct evaluation dimensions—has significant implications for the PPFR research community. The observation that high PSNR/SSIM can indicate successful reconstruction of an identity-mismatched image (the CanFG example) while low pixel similarity can accompany successful identity matching suggests the field has been optimizing the wrong objective. This reframing, supported by empirical results across three systems and validation via commercial APIs, represents a genuine methodological contribution that could shift how PPFR systems are evaluated. The demonstration that frequency-domain PPFR systems share common high-frequency characteristics enabling a generic proxy attack (Section 6) is particularly insightful, suggesting structural vulnerabilities in an entire class of approaches.

## Suggestions

- Conduct an ablation study validating cross-embedding-space attack success (e.g., FaceNet teacher attacking ArcFace-optimized templates) to substantiate or refine the model independence claim, or temper the claim to acknowledge the ArcFace-specific nature of the current demonstration
- Include utility metrics for the target PPFR systems to demonstrate attack effectiveness at operating points where recognition utility is preserved
- Clarify the distinction between the core PPFR contribution and the TIP-IM extension in Section 8, potentially reframing the latter as a "pilot exploration" rather than a systematic evaluation
