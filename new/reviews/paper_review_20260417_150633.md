## Summary

This paper argues that the prevailing evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR) — which measures privacy as resistance to pixel-level reconstruction (PSNR/SSIM) — fails to capture identity-level privacy leakage. The authors introduce FaceLinkGen, an attack that uses a simple distillation pipeline to extract identity embeddings from protected templates and then regenerates identity-consistent faces via a diffusion model (Arc2Face). FaceLinkGen achieves >98.5% matching accuracy and >96% regeneration success on three recent frequency-domain PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), revealing that pixel-distortion metrics can severely overestimate privacy.

## Strengths

- **Compelling conceptual contribution**: The central insight — that pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR/SSIM) are fundamentally misaligned with identity-level privacy — is convincingly demonstrated. Table 1 is particularly effective: it shows two real cases where high pixel similarity (SSIM=0.841) coincides with near-zero identity similarity (FS=0.008), while low pixel similarity (SSIM=0.235) coexists with meaningful identity similarity (FS=0.586). This alone is a significant corrective to the field.

- **Intentional simplicity of the attack strengthens the argument**: The deliberate use of a standard distillation pipeline (Section 3) with extremely low cost (~$0.80–1.60 and <2 hours on a single GPU) shows the vulnerability lies in the representation itself, not in sophisticated attack machinery. The minimal-resource attack (§5.1: 256 images yielding 98.7% linkage accuracy) is one of the most persuasive results.

- **Well-reasoned threat model discussion**: Section 2 carefully argues for returning to the original insider adversary framing of PPFR, explaining why local execution, trusted third-party servers, and external-attacker models either shift the trust problem or assume unrealistically constrained adversaries. The argument that a locally running model can be reverse-engineered by the service provider is strong.

- **Comprehensive multi-faceted evaluation**: The paper tests on three PPFR methods (ICCV 2023, CVPR 2024, NeurIPS 2025), three datasets (TPDNE, CASIA-WebFace hold-out, LFW), two commercial verification APIs (Face++, Amazon), and extends to de-identification (TIP-IM) and non-frequency methods (CanFG). The cross-verification with two independent APIs and embedding-level cosine similarity analyses (Section 7) adds reliability.

- **Strong empirical demonstration against FracFace's own metric**: Table 6's direct comparison is powerful — it shows that FracFace's claimed "protection rate" channel percentages (0.68–1.00) correspond to near-zero identity protection (0.000–0.008) under identity-centric evaluation. This is the most concrete evidence that pixel/channel-based metrics are misleading.

- **Constrained adversary experiments extend practical relevance**: Section 6 shows vulnerability persists even without knowledge of the protection mechanism, using only a generic Gaussian-blur high-pass proxy, achieving >92% matching accuracy and >94% regeneration success@5.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **Quantitative claims over-extend from a single embedding/generator stack (ArcFace/Arc2Face) to general conclusions about PPFR**: The headline numbers (>98.5% matching, >96% regeneration) are obtained through a pipeline tightly coupled to ArcFace as the teacher/student and Arc2Face as the generator. The paper claims "Other embedding models like FaceNet can also be used as long as there is a compatible generative model" (§3) but provides no experimental validation. The regeneration success is assessed exclusively via Face++ and Amazon API, both proprietary black boxes. The conceptual result — that these PPFR transforms preserve substantial identity information — is very well supported, but the quantitative extremity (≈100% regeneration success, "near-zero" protection) is not convincingly shown to be model- and verifier-agnostic. For a paper that advocates changing evaluation practice, this matters: the normative claim requires demonstrating that the vulnerability is not an artifact of the ArcFace→Arc2Face synergy or idiosyncrasies of commercial API thresholds with AI-generated faces. The Face++ vs. Amazon API discrepancy (90%+ vs. 44–57% in Table 7) further underscores this concern — the paper notes Amazon may be "stricter with AI-generated images" but does not investigate further, leaving uncertainty about which numbers reflect genuine identity leakage.

- **The threat model's implications are broader than the paper acknowledges, weakening the specificity of its critique**: Under the authors' own fully adversarial insider threat model, *any* high-utility, deterministic PPFR transform without a cryptographic key is likely vulnerable to similar distillation-based inversion. This is noted in the future directions (§10: "suggest stronger protection mechanisms, such as cryptographic approaches"). However, the paper's critique is primarily framed as exposing specific failures of PartialFace/MinusFace/FracFace, when the vulnerability is arguably a consequence of the threat model + information theory, not of these systems' particular design choices. The paper would be stronger if it more explicitly acknowledged that it is demonstrating a *class-level* vulnerability (non-cryptographic PPFR under insider adversary) rather than implying these specific methods are uniquely misguided. The abstract states "frequency-domain PPFR systems" broadly, but the finding likely applies to any utility-preserving, non-keyed template method.

### Minor:

- **Linkage accuracy (70–85%) vs. regeneration success (>96%) gap is under-explained**: Table 4 shows closed-set linkage at 70–85%, substantially lower than the 96%+ regeneration rates. The paper attributes this to CASIA-WebFace noise capping the ceiling at ~88%, but the gap between attack performance and ceiling (e.g., 0.78 vs. 0.88 for FracFace→FracFace) deserves more analysis. If identity information is truly well-preserved (as regeneration results suggest), why doesn't linkage performance approach the ceiling more closely? This gap may reveal important nuances about what aspects of identity are preserved vs. lost, and has implications for whether these attacks scale to large-population identification scenarios.

- **The constrained-attacker scenario (Section 6) is underspecified regarding exactly what the attacker observes and uses**: The paper says the attacker needs "only 30 paired image–template samples for validation (not training)" but also explicitly uses CASIA-WebFace with synthetic high-pass filtering as pseudo-templates for training. This is not merely "30 leaked pairs" — it is a full supervised training pipeline using a proxy task. The key observation (that PPFR outputs share a "high-pass visual essence") is stated but not rigorously justified: how transferable is this visual heuristic to methods outside the frequency-domain family? The section title "Attack Under Constrained Assumptions" implies weaker capabilities than the setup actually assumes, which still requires a labeled facial dataset, an embedding teacher, and structural insight about the protection method.

- **No tested countermeasures or defense analysis**: The paper suggests cryptographic hardening and inverted de-identification as future directions but does not evaluate even simple mitigations. The question of whether these vulnerabilities are irreparable (fundamental to any utility-preserving PPFR) or patchable (specific to current designs) is left entirely open. Even a preliminary test of template noise injection, quantization, or adversarial training against distillation would significantly clarify the nature and depth of the vulnerability.

- **TIP-IM and CanFG evaluations (Table 8) are limited**: Only 2,082 images from 408 identities are used, with only cosine similarity and linkage accuracy reported — no regeneration evaluation. These are described as "pilot" results, but the paper's broader claim that "the issue is structural to template with preserved recognition utility" would be more convincing with full experimental protocols on these out-of-family methods.

### Trivial:

- The constrained attack's proxy task (Gaussian-blur high-pass filter) is specific to frequency-domain methods, which partially contradicts the broader framing that identity leakage is a general problem. The paper acknowledges this limitation ("this particular attack is specific to the frequency-domain family"), so this is noted rather than a true weakness.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Testing at least one alternative embedding space/generator (e.g., FaceNet + compatible generator) to demonstrate the vulnerability is not ArcFace-specific, even if with slightly lower success rates.
- Embedding space visualization (t-SNE/UMAP) showing how student-extracted template embeddings cluster by identity vs. original image embeddings.
- Failure case analysis: examining the 1–10% of cases where the attack fails to identify systematic patterns or boundary conditions.
- Formal discussion of the utility-privacy tradeoff (e.g., mutual information bounds or differential privacy framing) to ground the empirical findings theoretically.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:

- **"The attack requires oracle access — this is a strong assumption limiting practical scope"**: The paper explicitly argues (§2) that oracle access follows from the original PPFR threat model where the service provider is the adversary, and demonstrates that local execution can be reverse-engineered. This is a deliberate and well-justified modeling choice, not an oversight.

- **"Missing comparison with prior attack methods (model inversion, reconstruction)"**: The paper explicitly differentiates its approach from Model Inversion Attacks in §1 (MIAs recover from learned embeddings; this work targets structural vulnerabilities in template generation). The attack objective (identity extraction, not pixel reconstruction) is fundamentally different, making direct comparison with reconstruction-based MIAs inappropriate.

- **"No testing with homomorphic encryption / cryptographic PPFR methods"**: The paper explicitly scopes itself to transformation-based (non-cryptographic) PPFR and recommends cryptographic approaches as the solution (§10). Criticizing the absence of attacks on methods the paper itself identifies as the proper defense is scope creep.

- **"Three attacked methods share a common structural pattern, inflating generality"**: The paper tests three methods spanning ICCV 2023 to NeurIPS 2025 representing the mainstream of open-sourced transformation-based PPFR. That they share frequency-domain design is precisely what makes the findings coherent. The CanFG/TIP-IM extensions (while preliminary) test beyond this family. The paper does not claim universal applicability to all PPFR.

- **"Reproducibility concerns about undisclosed hyperparameters"**: The paper provides substantial implementation detail (dataset composition, 80-20 split, training cost, specific model names). Minor hyperparameter details are standard practice not to include in full and do not affect the core claims.

- **"Soft biometric results missing for CanFG"**: The paper explicitly states this omission and provides a clear rationale ("such leakage is immediately apparent: as illustrated in Figure 2, CanFG-generated faces, by design, retain nearly all soft biometric characteristics"). Excluding an experiment where the outcome is visually obvious is reasonable.

- **"Table 6 compares fundamentally different quantities"**: The paper's entire point is precisely that these different quantities lead to contradictory conclusions — that high channel disruption does not mean identity protection. This comparison is the core contribution, not a methodological error.

## Novel Insights

The most important novel observation is the decoupling between pixel-level distortion and identity preservation: the paper provides concrete evidence (Table 1 and the similarity distribution analysis in §7) that protected templates are *more similar to their originating images than to other images of the same person* in embedding space. This means these PPFR transforms actually encode more identity information than is needed for recognition (which only requires same-ID matching), creating an exploitable surplus. Combined with the finding that standard distillation suffices to extract this surplus at negligible cost, this challenges the fundamental design assumption that "lossy transformation = privacy." The insight that de-identification methods face a symmetric vulnerability (preserving human-perceivable information leaks identity to machines that can extract it) is also novel and suggests that the privacy problem in face recognition is not about which party (human vs. machine) you hide from, but about whether *any* identity-correlated signal survives in the representation.

## Suggestions

1. **Add one experiment with a different embedding/generator pair** (e.g., FaceNet + a compatible conditional generator) even if performance is somewhat lower, to demonstrate the vulnerability is not ArcFace-specific. This would directly address the most substantive concern.

2. **Narrow the abstract/conclusion language** from "frequency-domain PPFR research" to "the evaluated frequency-domain PPFR systems" or "non-cryptographic PPFR under insider threat models," and explicitly state that the finding likely extends to any utility-preserving non-keyed template method rather than being specific to frequency-domain design.

3. **Investigate the Face++/Amazon API discrepancy**: Even a brief analysis (e.g., examining confidence score distributions, or testing on a subset with both APIs using the same images) would clarify whether the Amazon results reflect stricter thresholds, sensitivity to AI-generated faces, or genuinely lower identity fidelity.

4. **Provide at least one preliminary defense experiment**: Simple template perturbation (Gaussian noise, quantization) tested against the distillation pipeline would clarify whether the vulnerability is fundamental or can be mitigated without sacrificing utility.
