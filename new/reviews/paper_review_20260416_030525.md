# Review of ../paper.md

## Summary

This paper critiques the evaluation paradigm of frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR) systems, which traditionally rely on pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR/SSIM) to argue privacy guarantees. The authors present FaceLinkGen, a distillation-based attack that extracts identity embeddings from protected templates and regenerates faces using Arc2Face, without pixel-level reconstruction. Across three recent PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), FaceLinkGen achieves >98.5% linkage accuracy and >96% regeneration success (per Face++ verification), demonstrating that pixel-level resistance does not prevent identity leakage. The paper extends to constrained attackers, soft biometric leakage, and de-identification methods, arguing for identity-centric evaluation standards.

## Strengths

1. **Important conceptual contribution.** The central insight—that pixel-level reconstruction failure does not imply identity privacy—is well-motivated and potentially field-changing. Table 1 effectively illustrates the decoupling between pixel and identity similarity. This reframing addresses a genuine gap in the PPFR literature where reconstruction metrics have been treated as sufficient privacy indicators.

2. **Strong and comprehensive empirical evidence.** The attack achieves high success rates across multiple datasets (TPDNE, CASIA-WebFace, LFW) and three distinct PPFR methods. The minimal-resource experiments (800 and even 256 training images) convincingly demonstrate that the vulnerability is structural rather than an artifact of extensive training data.

3. **Thorough threat model articulation.** Section 2 provides a principled return to the insider adversary (service provider) threat model, clearly distinguishing from network eavesdropper models and critiquing unrealistic assumptions about conversion-model secrecy (e.g., in CanFG).

4. **Broad scope of evaluation.** The paper extends beyond frequency-domain PPFR to adversarial de-identification (TIP-IM) and non-frequency methods (CanFG), and evaluates soft biometric leakage (race, gender, age) with controlled MLP experiments. The constrained-attacker scenario (Section 6) using generic high-pass filtering shows the vulnerability persists even with limited system knowledge.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

1. **Evaluation relies heavily on the ArcFace ecosystem without independence verification.** The attack uses ArcFace embeddings for distillation, Arc2Face for generation, and Face++/Amazon APIs (which may share similar training) for verification—creating potential circularity. The paper claims the attack is independent of the server's FR backbone, but never tests with a fundamentally different embedding space (e.g., FaceNet, proprietary models, or those used by the actual PPFR systems tested). This leaves open whether the high success rates reflect universal identity leakage or ecosystem-specific alignment.

2. **Significant discrepancy between Face++ and Amazon API results undermines generalizability claims.** In the constrained-attacker scenario (Table 7), Face++ reports 94.6–96.3% regeneration success@5, while Amazon API reports only 44.7–57.0%. The paper dismisses this as Amazon being "more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images," but provides no analysis of failure modes, threshold calibration differences, or whether the regenerated faces are actually identity-consistent or merely adversarially aligned to Face++'s specific feature space. This gap substantially weakens the claim of universal "identity recovery" across verification standards.

3. **Lack of comparison with existing model inversion/leakage attacks.** The paper does not benchmark FaceLinkGen against prior face reconstruction or identity leakage attacks (e.g., embedding inversion methods like Wang et al. [33], Arc2Face-based attacks, or the U-Net/StyleGAN attacks used by FracFace's own evaluation). Without head-to-head comparison, it is unclear whether the identity-centric approach represents a qualitatively different threat or merely incremental improvement over existing reconstruction attacks when measured by identity-level metrics.

4. **Conflation of threat models and assumptions.** While the paper correctly identifies the insider/service provider threat model, it generalizes results to critique PPFR systems (e.g., those targeting network eavesdroppers) that explicitly assume weaker adversaries. The threat model shifts between the full oracle-access scenario (Section 5) and the "constrained" scenario (Section 6), but the conclusion presents both as demonstrating fundamental failures without clearly distinguishing which systems fail under which assumptions. The constrained attack still assumes the attacker can train on original face images (just using a high-pass surrogate), which is unrealistic for pure external attackers who only possess leaked templates.

5. **Insufficient engagement with the utility-privacy fundamental tension.** The paper demonstrates that identity information is extractable from utility-preserving templates, but does not adequately analyze whether this is an inherent limitation of non-cryptographic PPFR (any template enabling recognition necessarily preserves identity-discriminative features) or a specific failure of the frequency-domain design. The attack succeeds precisely because the PPFR systems preserve identity information—raising the question of whether any high-utility non-cryptographic template could resist such distillation.

### Minor:

6. **Missing failure case analysis.** While Figure 1 shows successful regenerations, the paper does not analyze cases where linkage or regeneration fails (e.g., specific demographics, poses, or lighting conditions), which would reveal the actual boundaries of the attack.

7. **No ablation studies on distillation components.** The impact of the 3×3 Conv2D layer, different student architectures, or the specific choice of cosine similarity loss versus other alignment objectives is not investigated.

8. **"Pilot" evaluations lack rigor.** The TIP-IM and CanFG evaluations (Section 8) are explicitly preliminary, with CanFG results showing perfect linkage (0.997) but no discussion of why this method is significantly more/less vulnerable than frequency-domain methods despite different design principles.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Template-level defense exploration: Testing whether Gaussian noise, quantization, or adversarial perturbation to templates before release disrupts the distillation would clarify whether the vulnerability is fundamental or merely unaddressed by current systems.
- Human perceptual evaluation of regenerated faces: While automated verification is standard, human judgment would validate whether "passes Face++" corresponds to actual identity recognition.
- Embedding space visualization (t-SNE/UMAP) comparing student embeddings to original ArcFace embeddings to illustrate preservation of identity structure.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution:

- *Claim that prior works only evaluate pixel-level reconstruction:* The paper explicitly acknowledges that FracFace reports identity similarity metrics, though arguing they remain reconstruction-centric. The critique is that these metrics are secondary, not absent.
- *Assertion that 800-image experiments are "unrealistic":* The paper demonstrates success with as few as 256 paired samples, which is within feasible leakage scenarios for determined attackers.
- *Nitpick about missing formal privacy definitions:* While the paper could benefit from formal definitions (unlinkability, indistinguishability), the absence is not a critical flaw for an empirical attack paper.

## Novel Insights

The paper convincingly establishes that frequency-domain PPFR systems evaluated solely on pixel-level reconstruction metrics may provide false assurance of privacy. The critical observation that "preventing pixel-level reconstruction is neither necessary nor sufficient to prevent identity leakage" reframes how the community should evaluate template protection. Furthermore, the demonstration that a simple distillation pipeline suffices to extract usable identity information—rather than requiring complex adversarial optimization—suggests that the vulnerability resides in the representation itself, not in sophisticated attack methodology. The constrained-attacker results, despite their limitations, hint that frequency-domain transformations share structural commonalities (high-frequency preservation) that create transferable vulnerabilities.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the scope of the threat model:** Explicitly separate results for the full oracle-access insider (Section 5) from the constrained attacker (Section 6), and acknowledge which PPFR methods legitimately target weaker adversaries. Do not claim systems "fail" promises they never made under the specific threat model tested.

2. **Explain the Face++/Amazon discrepancy:** Report failure mode analysis, demographic breakdown, or calibration differences to justify why results vary drastically between verification APIs. If the attack is brittle across different FR systems, the claim of universal identity extraction must be qualified.

3. **Compare with prior attacks:** Benchmark FaceLinkGen against the U-Net/StyleGAN reconstruction attacks used in FracFace and other prior MIAs under identical conditions, using identity-level metrics for both.

4. **Acknowledge the utility-privacy tradeoff:** Add discussion clarifying whether any non-cryptographic, high-utility template could resist this class of attacks, or whether the results indicate an inherent impossibility result for the PPFR paradigm itself.
