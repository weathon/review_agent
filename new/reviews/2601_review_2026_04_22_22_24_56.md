## Summary

ScholarPeer is a multi-agent framework for automated peer review that replaces static parametric knowledge with dynamic, web-scale context retrieval. The system decomposes the review process into specialized agents (summary, literature expansion, historian, baseline scout, Q&A engine, and review generator), evaluated on DeepReview-13K (1,286 ICLR 2024/2025 papers). The paper introduces H-Max, a calibration metric comparing AI review quality against the collective set of human reviews, and reports win rates across five review quality dimensions against fourteen baselines.

---

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem formulation with clean theoretical framing**: Section 2 explicitly contrasts *P(R|S_content)* (parametric review) with *P(R|S_content, C_dynamic)* (retrieval-grounded review), providing a crisp conceptual justification for why fine-tuning alone is fundamentally limited by knowledge cutoffs.

- **Broad evaluation scope across heterogeneous baselines**: Table 2 compares against fine-tuned models (CycleReviewer-8B/70B, DeepReviewer-7B/14B), single-agent LLMs (Gemini 3 Flash/Pro, Claude Sonnet 4.5), multi-agent systems (AgentReview, AI Scientist v2), and a closed-source commercial system (Stanford Agent Reviewer), which is more comprehensive than typical framework papers in this space.

- **Informative ablation with meaningful component attribution**: Table 4 demonstrates that each agent contributes measurably, with the Q&A agent being most critical (26% drop when removed), the summary agent surprisingly significant (10% drop), and the baseline scout meaningful (9% drop), validating the architectural decisions.

- **Novel H-Max metric concept**: Calibrating AI review quality against the *collective* strongest points of human reviewers (rather than an average) is a principled design that addresses the anchoring problem in peer review evaluation, even if the specific implementation has weaknesses (see below).

- **Practical saturation analysis**: Figure 5 shows performance saturating at ~10 Q&A pairs and ~3 expansion rounds, with a decline at 15 Q&A pairs, providing actionable tuning guidance and evidence the system is not trivially scalable.

- **Honest disclosure of failure mode**: Figure 4 explicitly acknowledges that AI Scientist v2 occasionally outperforms ScholarPeer on internal mathematical consistency checking (e.g., impossible probability ratios), demonstrating appropriate intellectual candor.

- **Venue-adaptive design without retraining**: Section 3.3's decoupling of investigation from reporting via swappable review guidelines is a practical advantage over fine-tuned systems, allowing ICLR/NeurIPS format variants without additional training.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Search-enabled judge structurally advantages the search-enabled system**: Section 4.2 states the LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4.5) is given web search access "to verify the existence of papers cited in the reviews." ScholarPeer generates citations from live web retrieval, so they are real and verifiable. Fine-tuned baselines (CycleReviewer, DeepReviewer) are parametric and may cite papers imprecisely or hallucinate them; the judge's citation-verification tool would systematically penalize these systems' *Technical Accuracy* scores regardless of the quality of their substantive critique. The 98.7–99.7% Technical Accuracy win rates against fine-tuned models (Table 2) cannot be attributed solely to ScholarPeer's reasoning architecture under this confounded design. The paper provides no experiment isolating this effect.

- **Potential data contamination from live web search is unaddressed**: DeepReview-13K consists of ICLR 2024/2025 papers whose human reviews are publicly indexed on OpenReview. ScholarPeer performs live Google Search during inference for literature expansion, historian contextualization, and baseline scouting. The paper contains no analysis of what fraction of retrieved search results are OpenReview pages for the evaluated papers, no filtering to exclude review-hosting domains, and no experiment comparing performance with search restricted to pre-submission sources. While the search queries are designed for related-work and baseline discovery (not review retrieval), the absence of any analysis or acknowledgment of this risk is a significant omission in a system evaluated against the same papers whose reviews are publicly web-indexed. An experiment blocking OpenReview-domain results would be essential to establish that performance gains are architectural rather than information-retrieval artifacts.

- **H-Max "superhuman" headline claim is not statistically supported**: Section 4.3.1 reports that the LLM judge's calibration achieved a Pearson correlation of r = 0.53 with human experts, which the paper describes as "strong alignment with human judgment." This is moderate calibration (explaining ~28% of variance) and is insufficient to reliably locate a system above or below the human anchor with confidence. The ScholarPeer H-Max score of 6.14 vs. the human anchor of 5.0 is a ~1-point gap on a 10-point scale; with r = 0.53 calibration, this difference could plausibly fall within measurement error. The paper provides no confidence intervals and no statistical significance test for this gap. The claim that ScholarPeer produces reviews "clearly superior" to human expert reviewers is not adequately supported by the calibration evidence. Furthermore, the human calibration study used two annotators per paper with no reported inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Cohen's κ), weakening the reliability of the human ground truth itself.

### Minor

- **Factual error in Table 3 caption**: The caption states "SCHOLARPEER achieves state-of-the-art performance across all metrics," but the table itself shows Stanford Agent Reviewer achieves an Analytical Depth H-Max score of 6.22 vs. ScholarPeer's 5.86. This is a concrete factual inaccuracy that the paper does not discuss.

- **Ablation win-rate discrepancy unexplained**: Table 4 reports ScholarPeer vs. AI Scientist v2 (Gemini Pro) at 85% on 200 papers, while Table 2 reports the same comparison at 80% on 1,286 papers. This 5-point gap is not addressed. If the 200-paper ablation subset is unrepresentative, the component-drop comparisons (e.g., "9% drop for baseline scout" vs. "4% drop for historian") inherit this sampling uncertainty.

- **Human evaluation underpowered for the key competitive comparison**: Section 4.3.1 reports human evaluation on 100 papers (20 per baseline). A 54% win rate against Stanford Agent Reviewer on 20 pairwise comparisons is not statistically distinguishable from chance (50%). No confidence intervals are reported for any of the human win-rate figures.

- **Figure 1's 100% aggregate win-rate is misleading**: Figure 1 presents a headline "100%" win rate, but this excludes Stanford Agent Reviewer. Table 2 shows win rates well below 100% against several agentic baselines, and Table 3 shows Stanford Agent Reviewer outperforming ScholarPeer on one H-Max dimension. The abstract's "significant win-rates" framing, juxtaposed with the 100% figure, overstates the headline result.

### Trivial

- The paper describes r = 0.53 as a "high Pearson correlation" (Section 4.3.1); this is moderate by standard social science conventions and the language should be adjusted.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Search leakage ablation**: Run ScholarPeer with OpenReview domain blocked from web search, or with search restricted to sources predating paper submission dates, and compare results to the full-search version. This would be the most actionable experiment to address the contamination concern.

- **Controlled search-access ablation for baselines**: Instantiate AgentReview and AI Scientist v2 with equivalent Google Search capability as ScholarPeer, then re-measure win rates. This would cleanly attribute performance gains to architecture vs. information access mode.

- **Statistical reporting for ablation**: Add bootstrap confidence intervals to Table 4's win-rate estimates; with N=200 and win rates of 59–85%, the uncertainty on per-component differences is non-trivial.

- **Case studies with source traces**: Show 2–3 specific ScholarPeer reviews alongside their web-retrieved sources and the human reviews, allowing readers to verify that high H-Max scores reflect genuine novel insights rather than aggregated external commentary.

Testing a competing architecture without its own native search access is a legitimate system-level comparison, not necessarily an unfair one. What would be needed is the search-access-matched ablation as a nice-to-have, but the absence of this experiment does not invalidate the existing results against Stanford Agent Reviewer, which also uses search.

- **Strength Finder — "Rigorous judge calibration" strength**: Dropped because r=0.53 is moderate, not rigorous, and the paper's own description of it as "high correlation" is an overclaim. This contradicts the verified Major weakness and loses as a strength.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most genuinely novel methodological insight is the H-Max calibration design — evaluating AI reviews not against an average human reviewer but against the *strongest individual points made by any reviewer in the pool*. This "collective ceiling" anchoring avoids regression-to-mediocrity in calibration and provides a more ambitious benchmark for AI review quality. The Review Diversity Score (RDS) quantifying mode collapse in fine-tuned systems is a second useful contribution: the finding that fine-tuned models score 0.01–0.02 on RDS vs. humans at 0.43 makes the diversity failure of parametric systems concrete and measurable, which is a genuinely informative empirical result independent of the H-Max calibration concerns.

## Suggestions

1. **Address contamination directly**: Provide a domain-blocking experiment (exclude OpenReview URLs from search results) and report the performance delta. Even if contamination is minimal, its absence of acknowledgment is a credibility gap.
2. **Decouple judge's search from citation verification**: Consider a judge variant that uses search for citation existence checks only *after* scoring, or report Technical Accuracy win rates separately with and without judge search enabled. This would cleanly decompose the methodology.
3. **Fix the Table 3 caption**: Accurately reflect that ScholarPeer is SOTA on most metrics, with Stanford Agent Reviewer leading on Analytical Depth.
4. **Report uncertainty in H-Max gaps**: Given r=0.53 calibration, a bootstrap or permutation test on the 6.14 vs. 5.0 difference is needed to make the "superhuman" framing defensible.
5. **Report inter-annotator agreement for human calibration**: Cohen's κ or similar is essential for 100-paper, 2-annotator calibration studies.

## Evaluation on Key Axes

- **Originality**: Moderate-high. The search-grounded multi-agent design is a natural but well-executed extension; H-Max is a genuinely novel metric design. Not a conceptual breakthrough, but meaningfully novel.
- **Importance of research question**: High. Automated peer review at scale is a pressing and underserved problem; the "parametric vacuum" framing correctly identifies a fundamental limitation of prior approaches.
- **Whether claims are well-supported**: Weak on the central claim. The "superhuman" headline claim (H-Max > 5.0) rests on a moderately-calibrated judge with unaddressed contamination risk. The advantages over fine-tuned baselines are credible; the superhuman framing is not.
- **Soundness of experiments**: Mixed. The evaluation scale (1,286 papers, 14 baselines) is impressive, but structural confounds in judge design and unaddressed contamination undermine the strongest claims.
- **Clarity of writing**: Good overall; the framework is clearly described. Some overclaiming in abstract and table captions.
- **Value to the research community**: Moderate. The framework concept and H-Max metric are useful contributions; the contamination and judge-bias issues limit how much practitioners can trust the specific performance numbers.
