## Summary

FaceLinkGen challenges the prevailing evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR), which uses pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM) to claim privacy protection. The paper introduces an identity-centric attack that uses teacher-student knowledge distillation to extract ArcFace-compatible embeddings directly from protected templates, enabling both identity linkage and face regeneration via Arc2Face—without ever recovering the original pixels. The core argument is empirically demonstrated on three recent frequency-domain PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), achieving high linkage and regeneration success verified by third-party commercial APIs (Face++, Amazon).

## Claims and Support

**Claim 1: Pixel-level metrics don't adequately capture identity leakage.**
*Partially supported.* Table 1 provides a compelling illustration. The substantive empirical evidence (Sections 5.2–5.3, Tables 2–5) is convincing for the three evaluated systems. The conclusion itself appropriately scopes this to "the frequency-domain PPFR systems we evaluated." Some passages in the introduction overstate breadth, but the core framing is valid.

**Claim 2: "Over 98.5% matching accuracy on three recent frequency-domain PPFR systems."**
*Partially supported, with a writing problem.* The abstract uses "matching accuracy" for what are actually two distinct tasks. Table 3 (1-to-1 verification on LFW) shows PartialFace=0.99, MinusFace=0.98, FracFace=0.92—meaning 98.5%+ does not hold for FracFace. Table 4 (closed-set 1-to-N linkage on CASIA hold-out, the more directly relevant task) shows 0.72–0.86, well below the headline number. The paper conflates these into one "matching accuracy" claim, which is technically misleading even if neither result is fabricated.

**Claim 3: Above 96% regeneration success on three PPFR systems.**
*Well-supported.* Table 2 shows Success@5 consistently ≥97.9% across all three methods and all three test datasets. The use of Face++ and Amazon API as independent verifiers rather than ArcFace itself is a genuine methodological strength.

**Claim 4: Vulnerability is structural, demonstrated even with minimal data.**
*Supported for the evaluated systems.* Section 5.1 shows FracFace attacked with 256 images achieves 98.7% linkage and 90.5% regeneration success. This is persuasive evidence, though the claim is not ablated across attacker ingredients or extended to all three methods in the minimal-resource setting.

**Claim 5: Current frequency-domain PPFR systems don't provide the identity privacy their evaluations imply.**
*Well-supported for the three evaluated systems.* Table 6's comparison against FracFace's own protection metric is rhetorically compelling. The conclusion correctly limits this to "the frequency-domain PPFR systems we evaluated."

**Claim 6: The framework transfers to de-identification methods (TIP-IM, CanFG).**
*Partially supported as a pilot.* Table 8 shows compelling numbers (e.g., distilled student recovers identity from TIP-IM despite perfect ArcFace-space protection), but the subset is small (2,082 images / 408 identities) and the authors correctly label this "pilot evaluation" in most places.

**Claim 7: Soft biometric attributes recoverable from protected templates.**
*Partially supported.* Table 9 shows near-original accuracy for FracFace and PartialFace; MinusFace is weaker. The experimental protocol (FairFace training, unspecified 10K evaluation set, no comparison of direct prediction from protected templates without distillation) is underspecified.

## Strengths

- **Important conceptual correction with good evidence.** The argument that reconstruction-based metrics are insufficient for identity privacy is well-motivated by Table 1 and substantiated by strong empirical results across three systems and three datasets. The insight challenges a widely-adopted evaluation convention in a consequential application area.

- **Simple, cheap, convincing attack pipeline.** Completing distillation for under $2 on consumer hardware, and still achieving near-ceiling performance with only 256 images, is powerful evidence that the vulnerability resides in the representation itself rather than requiring a sophisticated attacker.

- **Third-party verification eliminates within-ecosystem circularity concern.** Using Face++ and Amazon APIs to verify regenerated identities—rather than ArcFace itself—is methodologically sound. The paper explicitly acknowledges the ArcFace/Arc2Face pairing and defends why it does not invalidate the finding. The paper also notes FaceNet is a viable alternative teacher.

- **Well-motivated insider threat model.** The paper correctly identifies the curious/malicious service provider as the primary adversary and explains clearly why architecturally separate conversion doesn't resolve the trust problem. This is more rigorous than prior work, which sometimes adopted an external-wiretapper framing inconsistent with PPFR's original design intent.

- **Constrained-assumption extension.** Section 6 demonstrates that even without oracle access, a generic high-pass proxy suffices for >92% linkage and >94% regeneration@5 on Face++, broadening the threat surface beyond the insider model.

- **Disjoint identities for train/test.** CASIA-WebFace hold-out linkage evaluation uses identities with no overlap with distillation training data—an important and correctly observed experimental control.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None.*

### Major

- **Headline "over 98.5% matching accuracy" conflates two distinct tasks and slightly misfires for FracFace.** The abstract compounds 1-to-1 verification accuracy (Table 3) with closed-set 1-to-N linkage (Table 4) under the single label "matching accuracy." These are materially different in difficulty and privacy implication. For the more relevant linkage task, the numbers are 72–86%, not 98.5%+. Moreover, Table 3 shows FracFace at 0.92—below the "98.5%" threshold—so the abstract's "three recent frequency-domain PPFR systems" claim is too strong for that specific number. The paper's core result is still valid, but the abstract overpromises in a way that undermines credibility.

- **Large unexplained discrepancy between Face++ and Amazon API in the constrained-attack (Section 6).** Table 7 reports Face++ regeneration success of 94–96%, but Amazon API success of only 44–57%—roughly half. The paper's one-line explanation ("The Amazon API is likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images") is insufficient. This gap is material because it raises the question of which commercial verifier's judgement is more realistic. The authors should have investigated whether Amazon rejects AI-generated images regardless of identity match, and what the false-accept rate is on non-generated pairs.

### Minor

- **All three primary targets are from the same research group (Mi et al.)**, all sharing similar frequency-domain design philosophy. DuetFace and FaceObfuscator are mentioned but not attacked. This limits the diversity of the empirical evidence base and slightly constrains the generality of the claim about "frequency-domain PPFR." The paper acknowledges this implicitly ("these represent the mainstream of currently open-sourced transformation-based PPFR work"), which is reasonable, but the limitation should be explicitly acknowledged.

- **Soft biometric section (Section 9) is underspecified and thin.** The evaluation dataset (10K images) origin is not stated, the split between training and evaluation for the MLP head is unclear, and there is no baseline showing direct prediction from protected templates without the distillation pipeline (which would isolate what the distillation specifically adds). MinusFace performance is notably worse but receives little analysis.

- **Constrained-attack proxy model training uses only FracFace-like architecture but evaluates all three methods, relying on a "common visual essence" observation.** The paper would benefit from more explicit description of how the single-model works across all three systems and whether hyperparameters were tuned on the evaluation set (mentioned as 30 validation pairs, but threshold selection is unclear).

### Trivial

- **Section 5.1 minimal-resource ablation is conducted only on FracFace.** Extending it to PartialFace and MinusFace would strengthen the "structural" claim, though the FracFace result alone is already persuasive.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Empirical exploration of simple defenses** (noise injection, quantization, dropout on channels) to characterize whether the vulnerability is truly irreducible or merely unaddressed; even negative results would be informative.

- **Cross-generator evaluation** (e.g., replacing Arc2Face with PuLID or FaceID IP-Adapter) to confirm that regeneration success is not sensitive to the specific ArcFace/Arc2Face pairing.

- **Formalize a concrete identity-centric evaluation protocol** as a positive recommendation. The paper argues convincingly against PSNR/SSIM but does not propose a standardized replacement (e.g., distillation-based linkage accuracy + commercial API verification at specified FAR thresholds).

- **Analysis of failure cases** (~1–10% of non-successes): whether failures concentrate on specific identities, image conditions, or are random, would illuminate the attack's robustness and hint at defensive directions.

- **Failure case visualization:** Figure 1 shows only successful examples; including a small systematic failure analysis would make the empirical picture complete.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most genuinely novel observation is not the attack itself (distillation from protected templates to an accessible embedding space is a logical extension of known techniques) but rather the **diagnostic framing**: if a PPFR system preserves recognition utility, it necessarily preserves enough mutual information about identity to be distillable into a standard embedding—and that embedding, once obtained, renders pixel-level obfuscation irrelevant. This makes reconstruction-based evaluation a category error rather than merely an insufficient metric. The corollary—that designing for utility against machine recognition *guarantees* identity leakage unless the representation is cryptographically separated from the embedding—is a useful formal argument that motivates the paper's suggested future directions (key-based hardening, homomorphic encryption). The constrained-attack result further suggests that identity leakage is not just an insider risk: the common "high-pass nature" shared by this family of methods creates an additional attack surface for outsiders.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix the abstract headline.** Either lower "over 98.5% matching accuracy" to a number accurate for all three systems, or split into separate claims: "over 99%/98%/92% 1-to-1 verification accuracy, and 72–87% closed-set linkage recall, approaching the dataset's theoretical ceiling given 9–13% label noise."
2. **Investigate the Face++ vs. Amazon API discrepancy in Section 6 in depth.** Test whether Amazon rejects AI-generated faces per se (by submitting Arc2Face outputs verified manually), and characterize what the Amazon API's FAR is at the operating threshold used.
3. **Specify the soft biometric evaluation protocol in full:** training/validation/test split for the FairFace MLP head, whether identities overlap with the distillation training set, the origin and composition of the 10K evaluation images, and add a baseline of direct attribute prediction from the protected template without distillation.
4. **Add a minimal-resource ablation for PartialFace and MinusFace** (analogous to Section 5.1 on FracFace) to generalize the structural vulnerability argument.
