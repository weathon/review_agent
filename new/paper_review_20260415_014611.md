## Summary

FaceLinkGen challenges the dominant evaluation paradigm in frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR), which measures privacy as resistance to pixel-level reconstruction (PSNR/SSIM). The paper presents a distillation-based attack that maps protected templates into a standard identity embedding space (ArcFace), enabling both identity linkage and face regeneration without recovering original pixels. Applied to three recent frequency-domain PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), the attack achieves high linkage and regeneration success, demonstrating a structural gap between pixel-distortion metrics and identity-level privacy in these systems.

---

## Claims and Support

**Claim 1: Pixel-level metrics (PSNR/SSIM) do not adequately capture identity-level privacy in frequency-domain PPFR.**
*Well-supported.* Table 1 provides a concrete motivating example (CanFG face scores SSIM=0.841 yet identity similarity=0.008; two photos of the same person score low SSIM yet share identity). The main attack results confirm systems that score well under pixel-level evaluation still leak substantial identity information.

**Claim 2: "Over 98.5% matching accuracy and above 96% regeneration success" on three PPFR systems.**
*Partially supported, with precision concerns.* Table 3 (1-to-1 verification, LFW) shows PartialFace=0.99, MinusFace=0.98, FracFace=0.92. FracFace's 0.92 does not meet the "98.5%" threshold. The 98.5% figure appears derived from the best-case/top-2 methods or from the minimal-resource FracFace experiment (Section 5.1: "99.5% linkage accuracy"). The body is transparent about per-method breakdowns, but the abstract consolidates unevenly. For regeneration, Success@5 ranges 0.979–1.000, supporting the "96%" floor. The harder closed-set 1:N linkage numbers (Table 4: 0.72–0.87) receive less headline emphasis, though the paper notes these approach the dataset's theoretical maximum (~0.88 due to label noise).

**Claim 3: The vulnerability lies in the representation itself, evidenced by the attack's simplicity and low data requirements.**
*Partially supported.* Minimal-resource experiments (800 images, 50s training; 256 images) still achieve high success, strongly suggesting that identity information is not meaningfully disrupted by the transformations. However, there is no ablation varying student architecture capacity, teacher backbone choice, or comparing against simpler non-learned baselines. The claim is plausible and the evidence is suggestive, but causal isolation of the vulnerability mechanism is incomplete.

**Claim 4: The vulnerability extends to constrained attackers via a high-pass proxy (Section 6).**
*Partially supported.* The setup is clearly described (high-pass filter proxy, 30 validation pairs only, one student model for all three methods), and results are strong (Face++: 0.946–0.963; Matching: 0.925–0.962). However, there is no ablation on proxy design sensitivity (kernel size, strength). The Amazon API results (0.447–0.570) show significant divergence from Face++ results, raising questions about overestimation. The paper explicitly scopes this to frequency-domain methods, which is appropriate.

**Claim 5: Similar vulnerabilities extend to adversarial de-identification (TIP-IM) and non-frequency methods (CanFG).**
*Supported as pilot findings.* Table 8 shows strong identity leakage for both TIP-IM (student model: 0.648 cosine similarity, 0.939 cross-image linkage) and CanFG (0.520 cosine, 0.825 cross-image linkage). The paper consistently frames these as "preliminary" and "pilot" evaluations, which is appropriate given only one representative of each type is tested.

**Claim 6: Soft biometric attributes remain recoverable from protected templates.**
*Partially supported as pilot.* Table 9 shows FracFace and PartialFace near original-image baselines for race, gender, and age. The one-epoch training and single evaluation run leave protocol details incomplete, but the signal is clear and consistent with prior literature on ArcFace embedding leakage.

## Strengths

- **Identifies a specific and demonstrable failure mode in PPFR evaluation:** The paper shows concretely (Table 1, Tables 2–4) that high pixel-level distortion does not prevent identity extraction in the evaluated systems. This is a targeted, falsifiable critique rather than a generic claim about evaluation metrics.

- **Well-motivated insider threat model:** Section 2 makes a rigorous argument for why the "secrecy of the conversion process" is not a sound defense assumption when the service provider is the adversary — the provider can reverse-engineer a locally running model or use attacker-controlled accounts to collect paired samples. This threat model is more realistic than external-eavesdropper framing used in some prior PPFR work, and the paper backs up the threat model argument carefully.

- **Deliberately simple attack strengthening the core argument:** The distillation pipeline (Eq. 2, standard cosine-similarity loss) is intentionally minimal. The minimal-resource variants (256 images, 50-second training) demonstrate that the identity leakage is not a byproduct of an elaborate, finely tuned pipeline but rather an intrinsic property of the representations.

- **Cross-verification with independent commercial APIs:** Using Face++ and Amazon APIs for regeneration evaluation (rather than the attack's own ArcFace model) meaningfully reduces the circularity concern in regeneration assessment. Counting failed face detection as failure is also an honest evaluation choice.

- **Quantified gap between PPFR's claimed protection and actual identity leakage:** Table 6 directly compares FracFace's own "protection rate" metric (based on frequency-channel disruption) against FaceLinkGen's identity-centric measure, showing 100% claimed channel protection corresponds to near-zero identity protection. This is a crisp and compelling demonstration.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None identified. The core claim — that identity leakage remains high in evaluated PPFR methods despite pixel-level distortion — is supported by the experimental evidence.*

### Major

- **Abstract headline metrics are imprecise and somewhat misleading.** The abstract states "over 98.5% matching accuracy" across three systems, but Table 3 shows FracFace at 0.92, not ≥0.985. The 0.985 figure appears to apply at most to PartialFace (0.99) and MinusFace (0.98). The 1:N closed-set linkage (Table 4: 0.72–0.87) receives minimal headline emphasis despite being the most operationally meaningful measure of cross-instance identity leakage. While the paper is internally transparent about per-method, per-task breakdowns, the aggregate framing in the abstract and conclusion overstates uniformity of results. This matters because the abstract is what readers trust for a fair summary of attack capability.

- **Regeneration is evaluated against the source image only, not cross-image.** Section 5.3 evaluates regeneration by asking whether Face++ matches generated images to the *original image used to create the template*. This demonstrates that generated faces pass a verifier against that one enrollment image, but not that a person's identity is recoverable across *other* photos of the same individual. Since the linkage attack (Section 5.2) already demonstrates cross-image identity matching, the regeneration evaluation could straightforwardly be extended to check generated images against held-out photos of the same identity — which would be a more robust claim of "identity recovery." As-is, the regeneration result is necessary but not sufficient for the strongest version of the claim.

- **ArcFace ecosystem dependency is asserted but not tested.** Section 3 explicitly notes "Other embedding models like FaceNet can also be used as long as there is a compatible generative model" and frames the attack as independent of the server's backbone. However, every attacked system and the teacher model use ArcFace-compatible embeddings. If a PPFR server used FaceNet, AdaFace, or a proprietary backbone with different embedding geometry, it is unclear whether distillation-to-ArcFace would capture equivalent identity information. This is a testable claim and the one experiment that would most strongly validate the generality argument.

- **No comparison with prior face embedding inversion or model inversion attack methods under the same evaluation protocol.** The paper compares against reconstruction attacks from original PPFR papers (U-Net, StyleGAN), which fail as the paper predicts, but does not compare with more recent diffusion-based model inversion attacks or embedding-to-face generation methods. Without at least one comparison with existing inversion-style attacks under the same datasets and metrics, it is unclear how much of FaceLinkGen's success owes to the specific distillation formulation versus the underlying generative capability of Arc2Face.

### Minor

- **Linkage evaluation uses the same embedding space as training.** Table 4 reports closed-set 1:N linkage via cosine similarity in ArcFace space — the same space the student is explicitly trained to reproduce. While this is not purely circular (the student learns a *mapping* from templates, which is non-trivial), a stronger validation would report linkage accuracy using an independent verifier (e.g., Face++ pairwise comparison, as is done for regeneration). This would parallel the regeneration evaluation's use of independent APIs.

- **The Amazon API vs. Face++ disparity (44–57% vs. 95%+ in constrained setting) is noted but not adequately explained.** The paper attributes this to API strictness or sensitivity to AI-generated images, which is plausible, but the gap is large enough to cast some ambiguity on what "regeneration success" operationally means across verifiers. A brief analysis of whether the discrepancy is systematic (e.g., correlated with image quality or specific identities) would clarify whether Face++ is more permissive to AI-generated faces.

- **Constrained attacker section (Section 6) lacks ablation on proxy design.** The high-pass filter is presented as a single configuration without sensitivity analysis (kernel size, strength, or alternative proxy choices). Without this, it is unclear whether the reported results reflect a generally transferable proxy or one that was effectively tuned to match the shared visual characteristic of the three tested methods.

- **Soft biometric leakage section (Section 9) lacks protocol detail.** The one-epoch training regime on FairFace, with no convergence curve or variance reporting, makes it difficult to assess whether the results are saturated or represent an underestimate/overestimate. The comparison to original-image baselines is directionally meaningful, but the experimental setup is underdeveloped for a formal result.

### Trivial

- The "face-to-template linkage analogy to hash inversion" (Section 4.1) is rhetorically useful but technically loose; the paper would benefit from a brief note acknowledging this is an analogy, not a technical equivalence.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Test with at least one non-ArcFace-compatible PPFR system or backbone** (e.g., FaceNet-based or a proprietary substitute) to substantiate the backbone-independence claim empirically rather than argumentatively.
- **Evaluate simple defenses** (e.g., additive Gaussian noise on templates, quantization, or embedding perturbation) to map the privacy-utility tradeoff boundary, even approximately. This would sharpen the "fundamentally in the representation" argument and give practitioners a clearer direction.
- **Add cross-image regeneration verification:** verify generated faces against held-out photos of the same identity, not just the source image.
- **Embedding-space visualization (t-SNE/UMAP):** visualize alignment between original ArcFace embeddings and student-extracted embeddings to give intuitive confirmation of the distillation quality.
- **Run original reconstruction attacks through Face++ verification** under identical protocols to directly and numerically quantify the pixel-level vs. identity-level evaluation gap — the paper argues this gap exists but doesn't put a number on it in a controlled head-to-head.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most genuinely novel contribution is not the attack technique itself (distillation-style alignment to a public embedding space is established) but the *diagnostic reframing* it applies to a specific family of methods: it shows that the property PPFR systems are *designed* to preserve (recognition utility via identity-discriminative features) is precisely the property that makes them vulnerable to the proposed attack. The simpler the distillation that succeeds, the stronger the indictment of the representation. This creates a direct and uncomfortable tradeoff for frequency-domain PPFR: any design that preserves enough mutual information for a service provider's recognition task also preserves enough for an adversarial distillation pipeline. The minimal-resource results (256 images, 50 seconds of training) sharpen this into a concrete lower bound on how easy identity extraction is. The paper implicitly motivates a shift toward formal cryptographic methods (Section 10) as the only designs that can break this tradeoff structurally — a conclusion that is not stated as a main result but follows naturally from the evidence.

## Suggestions

1. **Revise the abstract** to accurately reflect per-method metric ranges. State the 1:N closed-set linkage numbers (0.72–0.87, approaching dataset theoretical maximum) alongside 1:1 verification and regeneration, so readers can assess attack severity across all task types without ambiguity.

2. **Add a cross-image regeneration check:** for each generated face, verify against 1–2 held-out photos of the same identity not used to create the template. This would convert the regeneration claim from "passes a verifier against the enrollment image" to "recovers a person-level identity representation."

3. **Test on one PPFR system with a non-ArcFace backbone.** Even a single experiment with FaceNet or AdaFace as the teacher (and a compatible generative model) would validate or qualify the backbone-independence claim, which is central to generality.

4. **Add an ablation in Section 6** varying high-pass filter kernel size and strength to show the proxy-based attack is not sensitive to a single tuned configuration.

5. **Clarify the Amazon vs. Face++ discrepancy** with at least a brief analysis: does the gap correlate with image quality metrics, specific PPFR methods, or particular identity/demographic subgroups? This determines whether the results should be interpreted conservatively (Amazon as upper bound on attacker difficulty) or expansively (Face++ as the appropriate benchmark).

6. **Expand Section 9 protocol:** report multiple training epochs and convergence behavior for the soft-biometric experiment, and add a direct baseline of a classifier trained on the protected templates without the distillation stage.

## Score and Decision

**Originality:** High. The conceptual reframing (pixel-distortion ≠ identity privacy) is clear, well-motivated, and specifically demonstrated rather than argued abstractly.

**Importance of research question:** High. The result that three recent, published PPFR systems offer false privacy guarantees under a realistic insider threat model has direct implications for system deployment and for evaluation standards in the field.

**Whether claims are well-supported:** Moderate-high. The main insider/oracle results are convincing. The headline abstract framing conflates metrics across tasks. Some claims about generality (non-ArcFace backbones, constrained attacker) rest on partially evidenced assumptions.

**Soundness of experiments:** Good. Multi-dataset, multi-API cross-verification, honest failure accounting, and minimal-resource stress tests are all positives. Missing: cross-image regeneration, ablations on student/proxy, comparison with prior inversion methods.

**Clarity of writing:** Good. The argument structure is clean and the threat model section is rigorous. The abstract overpromises slightly relative to the per-method breakdowns in the body.

**Value to the research community:** High. This paper is likely to influence how PPFR researchers design evaluations and motivate a shift toward formal privacy guarantees.

The weaknesses identified are real but do not undermine the core contribution. The paper makes a specific, demonstrable, and important point about the evaluation of frequency-domain PPFR systems. The strengths outweigh the weaknesses; this is an above-threshold paper that would benefit from modest revisions around metric precision, cross-image regeneration evaluation, and backbone generality.
