## Summary

This paper presents a comprehensive empirical investigation of gating mechanisms applied at five different positions within standard softmax attention layers in LLMs. Through systematic comparison of 30+ variants of both 15B MoE and 1.7B dense models trained on up to 3.5T tokens, the authors find that a head-specific sigmoid gate applied after the scaled dot-product attention (SDPA, position G1) consistently improves performance. They attribute the gains to two factors: (1) introducing non-linearity into the low-rank W_V W_O composition, and (2) input-dependent sparse gating. A compelling secondary finding is that this gating dramatically reduces attention-sink behavior and substantially improves long-context extrapolation on RULER.

## Claims and Support

**Claim 1: G1 gating consistently improves performance and is the most effective placement.**
*Partially supported.* Table 1 clearly shows G1 beats G2–G5 and all parameter-expansion baselines on MoE models. Table 2 shows consistent dense-model improvements across multiple training recipes. However, for dense models the paper only compares G1 to the baseline—not to G2–G5—so the "most effective placement" conclusion outside MoE is not fully established. No variance estimates or repeated runs are reported for any comparison, making the consistency claim harder to evaluate rigorously.

**Claim 2: Gating improves training stability, tolerates larger learning rates, and enhances scaling.**
*Partially supported.* Table 2 rows 5/6 vs. 10 and rows 11/12 vs. 13/14 clearly show baselines diverge at high LR (8e-3) while gated models continue training and improve. The stability effect is real and well-evidenced. The "enhances scaling" framing is broader than shown—only two model sizes and a few training recipes are tested.

**Claim 3: Non-linearity and query-dependent sparsity are the two key causal factors.**
*Partially supported.* Table 3 supports the nonlinearity hypothesis (RMSNorm, additive SiLU, and sigmoid gates all help; identity/SiLU-only help less). Table 4 supports sparsity as a correlate of performance. However, most compared variants differ in placement, parameterization, and head-specificity simultaneously. The NS-sigmoid ablation for sparsity conflates sparsity with minimum gate magnitude—the range shift from [0,1] to [0.5,1] means the model also loses the ability to fully suppress signals. This confound is not resolved. The claim that these are the identified *causes* (not correlates) is overstated.

**Claim 4: Gating eliminates/mitigates attention sink.**
*Supported for the measured diagnostic, overstated in language.* Figure 2 and Table 4 show mean first-token attention dropping from 0.467 to 0.048. Appendix A.3 (Figure 6) provides layerwise confirmation. However, "eliminates" and "attention-sink-free" appear repeatedly across the paper for a metric that is averaged over heads and layers on a single perplexity dataset. The paper does not evaluate sink across tasks, prompt types, or decoding settings, and does not compare with alternative sink-mitigation methods.

**Claim 5: Long-context extrapolation improves significantly (+10 points RULER).**
*Empirically well-supported, but the attribution and one subclaim are problematic.* Table 5 shows dramatic gains at 64k/128k (e.g., 66.60 vs. 37.51). The explanation—that gating reduces reliance on attention sinks, making models more robust to YaRN-based RoPE modification—is plausible but correlational. **The conclusion states "allowing models to generalize effectively to longer sequences without retraining"—this is directly contradicted by Section 4.4, which explicitly describes 80B tokens of additional training at 32k before YaRN extension.** This is a factual inconsistency in the paper.

**Claim 6: Systematic investigation of gating in softmax attention.**
*Well-supported.* The study covers 5 positions, 2 granularities, head-specific vs. shared, multiplicative vs. additive, and multiple activation functions—first on MoE (400B tokens, 30+ variants), then validated on dense models (up to 3.5T tokens).

## Strengths

- **Empirical scale and breadth:** Training 30+ gating variants of 15B MoE models on 400B tokens—with parameter-matched baselines including expanded KV heads, query heads, and additional experts—is unusually thorough. The subsequent validation on dense models across multiple training regimes (different depths, batch sizes, LRs, and token budgets up to 3.5T) makes the practical claim credible across settings.

- **Tight ablation isolating the gating contribution within Switch Heads:** Appendix A.1 shows that Switch Head's gains largely survive reduction to a 1-top-1 configuration equivalent to simple value gating, demonstrating that the gate itself—not the routing mechanism—drives improvements. This is a genuinely insightful control experiment that prior work did not run.

- **Novel connection: sparse SDPA gating, attention-sink mitigation, and long-context extrapolation.** The paper is the first (at LLM pretraining scale) to show that a simple architectural gate simultaneously eliminates the measured attention-sink diagnostic and produces large RULER gains after RoPE extension. The finding that value-layer gating reduces massive activations but does *not* eliminate attention sink (Table 4 rows 4–5, Appendix A.3) is an interesting mechanistic refinement that corrects the common assumption that massive activations cause attention sinks.

- **Training stability at large LR/batch:** The divergence results in Table 2 (baseline diverges at LR=8e-3, gated model does not) provide a concrete, practically important finding. The comparison against sandwich normalization—which restores stability but not performance—cleanly separates stabilization from model quality improvement.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*(None. The core practical contribution—G1 gating improves performance, stability, and long-context extrapolation—is supported by the data.)*

### Major

- **"Without retraining" claim in conclusion is factually wrong.** Section 4.4 explicitly states: "we increase the RoPE base from 10k to 1M and continue training on data with a sequence length of 32k for an additional 80B tokens." The conclusion reads "allowing models to generalize effectively to longer sequences *without retraining.*" These are directly contradictory. At minimum, the long-context result demonstrates *adaptation* plus extrapolation, not zero-shot generalization. This should be corrected before publication.

- **NS-sigmoid ablation conflates sparsity with output range.** The proposed NS-sigmoid = 0.5 + 0.5·sigmoid(x) restricts outputs to [0.5, 1.0], which simultaneously reduces sparsity AND eliminates the ability to suppress signals near zero. The paper treats this as evidence that *sparsity* matters, but the result could be entirely explained by the inability to gate out signals entirely. A cleaner ablation—e.g., top-k masking or a thresholded sigmoid that preserves the zero-suppression property—would be needed to isolate the sparsity claim properly. As it stands, Claim 3's sparsity subclaim is undermined by this confound.

- **No direct experimental comparison with Forgetting Transformer (Lin et al., 2025).** The Forgetting Transformer applies a forget gate to softmax attention output and reports significant gains—the most directly comparable prior work. The paper discusses it in related work but provides no head-to-head empirical comparison in Tables 1 or 2. Without this comparison, the reader cannot assess whether the proposed systematic study simply rediscovers what the Forgetting Transformer already showed, or offers meaningful improvements or distinctions. This is the most significant missing experiment given the papers are near-contemporaneous and share core mechanism.

- **Mechanism claims are overstated relative to ablation design.** The paper claims to "attribute effectiveness to two key factors" (non-linearity and query-dependent sparsity) as established mechanisms. However, most ablation comparisons vary multiple confounds simultaneously: G1 vs. G2 differ in query-dependence AND which tokens' hidden states produce the gate AND placement relative to softmax. G1 head-specific vs. head-shared differ in parameterization AND effective sparsity. The paper presents plausible *hypotheses* supported by correlational ablations, not cleanly identified mechanisms. The framing should be moderated to match the evidence.

### Minor

- **Dense experiments don't cross-compare gate positions.** For MoE models, all five positions (G1–G5) are compared (Table 1). For dense models, only G1 is evaluated (Table 2). The claim that G1 is "the most effective" placement is therefore established only on MoE models; the dense experiments cannot confirm this claim independently.

- **Attention-sink diagnostic is narrow.** "Eliminates attention sink" and "attention-sink-free models" rely on a single metric: mean first-token attention proportion on a language modeling test set. The paper doesn't evaluate across diverse tasks, generation settings, or longer/shorter sequences, nor does it compare to explicit sink-mitigation baselines (e.g., softmax-1, register tokens). The language should be moderated.

- **Model scale limited to 1.7B dense and 15A2B MoE (2.54B activated).** For a paper making broad claims about LLM pretraining, the absence of experiments at 7B+ dense models leaves open whether the stability and performance benefits persist at commonly deployed scales.

### Trivial

- The <2% wall-time overhead claim appears without hardware specifications, batch size, or sequence length context, making it hard to evaluate. This is a claim worth supporting with a measurement.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Controlled sparsity ablation:** An experiment enforcing a target sparsity via gate thresholding or top-k masking while keeping gate values otherwise intact would cleanly isolate whether sparsity itself—vs. the magnitude suppression range—drives the gain.
- **Gate evolution during training:** Plotting mean gate values across training steps would reveal whether sparsity is present from early training (perhaps from bias initialization) or is learned progressively, which matters for interpreting *why* it helps.
- **Causal sink–extrapolation link:** A control that reduces attention sink by a different mechanism (e.g., register tokens, softmax-1) and measures RULER scores would test whether sink reduction causally produces long-context gains.
- **Scale-up to ≥7B dense:** Even a single 7B training run would substantially strengthen the practical adoption claim.
- **Formal rank analysis:** A quantitative bound on how much the effective rank of the X→O mapping increases with gating would sharpen the non-linearity argument beyond the intuitive appeal to universal approximation.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most genuinely novel empirical insight is that value-layer gating (G2) reduces massive activations but does *not* eliminate attention sink, while SDPA-output gating (G1) with head-specific query-dependent scores does both. This decouples two phenomena that previous work had conflated—it suggests attention sink does not require massive activations as a precondition, but rather arises from the softmax normalization dynamics in a per-head manner that can only be overridden by query-dependent, head-specific suppression of the SDPA output itself. This finding, if replicated, meaningfully advances the mechanistic understanding of attention sinks beyond the prior "softmax normalization forces it" explanation.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix the "without retraining" claim in the conclusion.** The 80B-token continued training at 32k is retraining. Replace with "allows models to more robustly adapt context-length extension via YaRN."
2. **Redesign the sparsity ablation.** Test a sigmoid gate where the output range is preserved at [0,1] but effective sparsity is controlled by initialization or a temperature parameter, rather than by NS-sigmoid which shifts the floor to 0.5.
3. **Add a Forgetting Transformer comparison row** to Table 1 or Table 2, even if only at 400B tokens. This is the most comparable published baseline and its absence is conspicuous.
4. **Moderate mechanistic language.** Replace "we attribute this effectiveness to two key factors" and similar phrasing with "our ablations are consistent with two contributing factors," accurately representing the correlational nature of the evidence.
5. **Specify the first-token attention metric more precisely.** Clarify that "attention-sink-free" means "below 5% mean first-token attention on a held-out language modeling set" to make the claim falsifiable.

## Evaluation

- **Novelty:** Moderate. The core idea (gate after SDPA) appears in Forgetting Transformer and Bondarenko et al. The contribution is a systematic study at scale, the stability analysis, and the attention-sink/long-context connection. Not a breakthrough, but a meaningful advance.
- **Technical soundness:** Moderate. Ablation design is thoughtful but the sparsity controls have a confound, and the mechanistic language overreaches. The "without retraining" error is a careless factual inconsistency.
- **Empirical support:** Strong for the practical claims; moderate for the mechanistic ones. The scale of experiments (3.5T tokens, 30+ variants) is genuinely impressive.
- **Significance:** High practical significance. The mechanism is simple, addable to any softmax attention implementation, and yields real stability and performance gains. The attention-sink finding is scientifically interesting.
- **Clarity:** Good overall, with the main exception of mechanistic overclaiming and the "without retraining" error.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a genuine and practically significant empirical contribution at impressive scale. The main weaknesses—overclaiming mechanism, the NS-sigmoid confound, the missing Forgetting Transformer comparison, and the factually wrong "without retraining" sentence—are correctable. None of them undermine the core practical finding. The paper lands comfortably in the weak-accept range: publishable with targeted revisions.
