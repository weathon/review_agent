## Summary

FaceLinkGen is an identity-centric attack on frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR) systems. The paper argues that the dominant evaluation paradigm—measuring attack success via pixel-reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM)—fails to capture identity-level leakage. Using a simple knowledge-distillation pipeline to extract ArcFace-compatible embeddings from protected templates, followed by Arc2Face-based face regeneration, the authors achieve >98.5% matching accuracy and >96% regeneration success on three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace ICCV'23, MinusFace CVPR'24, FracFace NeurIPS'25), verified against two independent commercial APIs.

---

## Claims and Support

**Claim 1: PSNR/SSIM do not adequately capture identity-level privacy.**
*Partially supported.* The core intuition is well-illustrated by Figure 2 and Table 1. However, Table 1 is illustrative (two specific cases), not a systematic correlation analysis across PPFR methods. The attacks in Secs. 5.2–5.3 support the claim for the three tested systems, but the paper does not demonstrate the mismatch across a broader range of methods or attack types. The claim is true for the evaluated cases; its generality is asserted rather than established.

**Claim 2: FaceLinkGen achieves high linkage and regeneration success on three frequency-domain PPFR systems under oracle-access.**
*Well-supported.* The attack is clearly specified, uses held-out CASIA identities (no identity overlap with training), achieves >98% matching (Table 3, Table 4) and >96% regeneration success (Table 2) cross-verified by both Face++ and Amazon APIs (Table 5). This is the paper's strongest result.

**Claim 3: "The vulnerability resides in the representation itself" — a simple distillation suffices, implying the extractability is structural.**
*Partially supported.* The minimal-resource experiment (Sec. 5.1: 256–800 images, ~50 seconds, still 90–99% success) is compelling evidence for cheapness. The Section 6 proxy attack (generic high-pass filter) is supportive. However, the mechanistic claim that extractability is inherent to any recognition-utility-preserving template is stronger than shown. The paper does not isolate what specifically causes leakage, nor test whether success would persist with a completely different embedding space/generator pair. The claim is directionally correct for the frequency-domain family specifically.

**Claim 4: The evaluated PPFR systems do not provide the level of identity privacy implied by their reconstruction-based evaluations.**
*Well-supported.* This is the central and most credible claim. Table 6 juxtaposing FracFace's near-100% "channel protection" rate with near-zero defense under identity-centric attack is rhetorical but accurate. Commercial-grade verification independently confirms this (Table 5).

**Claim 5: The attack generalizes to adversarial de-identification (TIP-IM) and CanFG.**
*Partially supported, and appropriately hedged in the paper as "pilot evaluations."* Table 8 shows strikingly high distillation-recovered linkage (0.997 for CanFG, 0.939 cross-image for TIP-IM) but with minimal experimental detail. The conclusion section correctly uses tentative language ("may extend beyond").

**Claim 6: Soft biometric attributes remain recoverable at near-original accuracy.**
*Partially supported.* Table 9 shows this holds for FracFace and PartialFace but not as strongly for MinusFace (Race Acc: 0.569 vs 0.700 original). The paper says "nearly the same accuracy as from the original images" which overstates the finding for MinusFace. The section lacks full protocol detail.

## Strengths

- **Identity-centric reframing of PPFR evaluation with direct empirical validation.** Demonstrating that high "channel protection" (FracFace claims 100%) co-exists with near-total identity leakage (Table 6) concisely makes the paper's central point. This is not obvious a priori and has direct implications for how the PPFR community measures progress.

- **Deliberately simple attack design that implicates the representation.** The choice to use a lightweight distillation pipeline is intentional and epistemically important: if a standard, cheap procedure succeeds (2 hours, ~$1.60, a single A6000 GPU), the vulnerability cannot be attributed to attack sophistication. The minimal-resource variant (256 images, 50 seconds, still 90.5% regeneration) powerfully reinforces this.

- **Independent cross-validation with two commercial APIs from different vendors.** Using both Face++ and Amazon Rekognition (Table 5), which are architecturally independent of ArcFace and the training pipeline, substantially reduces the risk that high success rates are a measurement artifact. This is methodologically stronger than most attack papers.

- **Closed-set linkage results (Table 4) as an especially clean privacy violation metric.** Cross-domain template-to-template and face-to-template retrieval consistently exceeds 70–82% recall against an upper bound of ~88% (set by CASIA dataset noise), meaning the attack effectively saturates the information available. This result does not depend on the regeneration pipeline or any external generator.

- **Constrained-attacker scenario (Section 6) extending below oracle access.** Training on a generic Gaussian high-pass proxy with zero system knowledge still achieves 92–96% 1-to-1 matching across all three systems. This is a meaningful additional contribution showing the attack degrades gracefully under weaker assumptions.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The core empirical results are real and the conclusions about the three evaluated systems are sound.

### Major

- **Overclaiming generality of the structural argument.** The paper repeatedly states "as long as templates preserve recognition utility, they tend to retain recoverable identity information" (Sec. 6 conclusion) and "the vulnerability resides in the representation itself" (Sec. 3). These are strong structural claims. The evidence supports them for the three tested frequency-domain methods, and tentatively for TIP-IM and CanFG. But the paper provides no analysis of *what specific representational property* causes leakage, no test of whether representations that are deliberately not alignable to any public space (e.g., heavily obfuscated proprietary embeddings or formally private representations) also leak, and no information-theoretic bound relating recognition utility to identity extractability. The claim risks over-generalizing: there may exist representations that preserve task utility while being substantially harder to align into a public embedding/generation space. The paper should either narrow this claim to the frequency-domain family (which would be fully supported) or provide controlled ablations that isolate the structural mechanism.

- **Circularity between student training target and regeneration pipeline — and no empirical validation of alternatives.** The student is trained to recover ArcFace embeddings; Arc2Face is conditioned on ArcFace embeddings. Both the teacher and the generator share the same embedding space by construction. The paper acknowledges this in Sec. 3: "Other embedding models like FaceNet [28] can also be used as long as there is a compatible generative model." However, no experiment validates this claim. It is unclear whether success depends on ArcFace-specific compatibility (ArcFace + Arc2Face is a particularly well-calibrated pair) or whether the attack would generalize if the PPFR server used a fundamentally different embedding space. The linkage results (Table 4) are measured entirely in ArcFace space. An experiment substituting FaceNet or another public backbone would resolve this concern and directly strengthen Claim 3 about structural vulnerability.

- **Minimal-resource ablation conducted only on FracFace.** Section 5.1 shows high attack success with 256–800 images, but only for FracFace. Since the three methods differ structurally (FracFace and PartialFace are hardcoded transformations, MinusFace is learned on MS1Mv2), it is unclear whether the "extreme low cost" claim generalizes across all three. The paper presents this as a "lower-bound analysis" but it is only a lower bound for FracFace specifically. The cost/data requirement may differ for methods like MinusFace.

### Minor

- **Table 3 is thin relative to the prominence of the "98.5% matching accuracy" claim in the abstract.** Only LFW is shown, with no variance or confidence intervals, and the per-method numbers are single scalars. Table 4 (closed-set retrieval on CASIA hold-out) is the more rigorous evidence and is more persuasive. The abstract foregrounds Table 3's number but Table 4 carries more weight.

- **TIP-IM and CanFG evaluations lack methodological detail.** Table 8 presents no information on train/test splits for the distillation applied to TIP-IM, no detail on whether the same student weights were reused or retrained, and no regeneration evaluation with commercial APIs (only embedding similarity and linkage). For CanFG, the high linkage score (0.997) follows almost trivially from the paper's argument about CanFG's model-secrecy assumption, making the result less informative as an independent evaluation. The paper labels these as "pilot evaluations," which is appropriate, but the writeup sometimes draws conclusions from them that are not firmly grounded in the presented evidence.

- **Section 9 (soft biometric leakage) uses an under-specified protocol and overstates uniformity of results.** Training an MLP on FairFace for "one epoch" with no reporting of learning rate, MLP architecture, train/test split, or variance across runs is insufficient to establish the soft-leakage claim. The statement "soft-biometrics can be inferred at nearly the same accuracy as from the original images for all these protected templates" is accurate for FracFace and PartialFace but inaccurate for MinusFace (Race Acc 0.569 vs. 0.700 original — a meaningful gap). A baseline directly predicting attributes from the raw protected templates (without student distillation) is also absent, making it unclear how much of the accuracy comes from distillation versus residual visual signal in the template.

- **Linkage evaluation is closed-set at 2,115 identities.** In realistic deployments, a gallery might contain millions of identities. The closed-set recall figures (70–82%) do not have a corresponding open-set analysis with scaled distractors. A brief scaling experiment would clarify practical threat significance.

### Trivial

- Section 6 reports Amazon API success as "44–57%" for the constrained attack — this low number relative to Face++ (94–96%) is potentially important but receives no analysis. Is Amazon stricter, or more sensitive to AI-generated images? A one-sentence explanation would improve interpretability.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **At least one empirical test against a cryptographic or key-based PPFR system.** The paper recommends CryptoFace [3] and key-based approaches in Sec. 10. Testing whether FaceLinkGen fails against such a system would sharpen the paper's prescriptive value by confirming that the vulnerability is not inherent to all PPFR but specific to the design family evaluated.

- **A data-scaling curve for all three methods** (query count vs. linkage/regeneration success) would turn the minimal-resource result from an anecdote into a systematic characterization of the threat surface.

- **FaceNet-student experiment.** Train the distillation pipeline using FaceNet as the teacher (with an appropriate generative head or nearest-neighbor evaluation directly in FaceNet space) and report linkage accuracy. This would decouple the ArcFace circularity and substantially strengthen Claim 3.

- **A brief failure-case analysis.** The paper reports 1–4% failure rates but never examines what the failures look like. Systematic failure patterns (e.g., extreme pose, demographic subgroups) would reveal the attack's true limits.

- **An information-theoretic or preliminary theoretical framing** of the utility-privacy tension, even informally. If matching accuracy must be preserved, some bound on mutual information between template and identity must be non-trivial. This would transform the empirical finding into a principled argument.

## Novel Insights

The paper's genuinely novel contribution is not the distillation technique itself (which is standard) but rather the epistemological reframing: demonstrating that *the very property required for PPFR to be useful* (preservation of identity-discriminative information) is the property that makes it attackable under identity-centric evaluation. The proof-by-simplicity argument — showing that a cheap, generic student suffices — converts the attack into a structural argument rather than an adversarial technique. The constrained-attacker extension (Section 6), in which a single model trained only on a generic high-pass filter proxy achieves 92–96% identity matching across all three systems, is the paper's sharpest empirical observation and underscores that the vulnerability is a property of the frequency-domain design family, not a consequence of sophisticated adversarial tuning. The contrast between FracFace's claimed 100% channel-disruption protection and near-zero actual identity protection (Table 6) is a memorable and concrete illustration of metric misalignment.

## Suggestions

1. **Narrow the structural claim or validate it empirically.** Either limit "the vulnerability resides in the representation itself" to the frequency-domain family (fully supported) or run an experiment showing the attack also succeeds with FaceNet as the distillation target and a FaceNet-compatible generator, demonstrating the claim is not ArcFace-specific.

2. **Replicate the minimal-resource ablation on all three methods**, reporting a data-scaling curve to establish whether the "$1.60 attack" claim generalizes.

3. **Report open-set linkage performance with scaled distractors** (e.g., gallery sizes of 10k, 100k by inserting random face images) to establish the practical threat under realistic deployment conditions.

4. **Strengthen Section 9.** Add a direct baseline (predict soft biometrics from the raw protected template without distillation), run multiple seeds, and avoid overstating the MinusFace result.

5. **Provide one paragraph analyzing the Amazon API vs. Face++ discrepancy in Table 7** (~44–57% Amazon vs. 94–96% Face++). If this reflects AI-image sensitivity, it is an important boundary condition on the regeneration attack.

## Evaluation

**Novelty:** Moderate. The distillation + conditional generation pipeline is entirely standard. The contribution is in the *application* and *framing*: using simplicity as an argument about structural vulnerability and proposing identity-centric evaluation. This is meaningful for the PPFR community but not technically novel at the method level.

**Technical soundness:** Good for the main results on three systems. Weakened by the ArcFace-circularity in regeneration evaluation and the absence of validation for alternative embedding spaces.

**Empirical support:** Strong for the core claims on the three evaluated PPFR systems. Weak for generalization claims beyond the frequency-domain family and for soft-biometric claims.

**Significance:** High within the PPFR subfield — directly challenges evaluation practices used in recent top-venue publications (ICCV, CVPR, NeurIPS). More modest from a broader ML security perspective.

**Clarity:** Generally clear and well-structured. Section 6 and Section 9 are undercooked relative to the main results.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a real and substantive empirical point that matters for the PPFR research community. The core attack results are convincing and the minimal-resource/constrained-attacker variants add genuine value. However, by ICLR standards: technical novelty is modest, the most important structural generalizations are asserted rather than established, and the circularity in the ArcFace-centric evaluation pipeline is not empirically resolved. The paper sits comfortably at an ACM Multimedia level but falls short of the ICLR bar on novelty, theoretical depth, and empirical completeness of its broader claims.
