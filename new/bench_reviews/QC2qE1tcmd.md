## Summary
This paper proposes a clean abstraction of simplicial message passing as relational message passing, then uses that abstraction to import graph-style oversquashing analyses into higher-order settings via aggregated influence matrices and influence graphs. It also proposes a simple rewiring heuristic for relational structures and evaluates it on graph/simplicial models over TUDatasets plus the synthetic RINGTRANSFER benchmark.

Overall, the paper has a real and interesting conceptual contribution: the Section 2 relational formalization is useful and likely to be reusable. However, several of the stronger theoretical and empirical claims are overstated relative to what is actually established, and one named theoretical contribution—about hidden dimension mitigating oversquashing—is not adequately supported by the derivation presented.

## Strengths
- **The axiomatic reduction from simplicial message passing to relational message passing is the paper’s clearest and strongest contribution.** Section 2 does not merely gesture at a connection: it explicitly defines relational message passing (Definition 2.5) and then constructs the simplicial instance through \(R_1,\dots,R_5\), matching boundary/co-boundary/lower/upper adjacencies and the corresponding message-passing scheme. This is a substantive unification rather than a superficial reframing.
- **The influence-graph construction is a useful bridge that makes existing oversquashing tools portable to higher-order settings.** Defining \(\tilde A\), \(B\), and the influence graph gives a concrete mechanism to analyze relational/topological message passing with graph-theoretic machinery. Even where the resulting theory is mostly reduction-based, this bridge is still valuable because prior graph analyses do not directly apply to simplicial/cellular message-passing schemes.
- **Lemma 3.2 and Theorem 3.5 provide meaningful conditional extensions of graph sensitivity/depth analyses to the relational setting.** The Jacobian upper bound in Lemma 3.2 and the depth-dependent bound in Theorem 3.5 are plausible, technically relevant, and appropriately framed as analogues of prior graph results under bounded-Jacobian and normalization assumptions.
- **The paper is commendably honest in parts of the discussion about limitations of direct graph-vs-complex comparison.** Section 6 explicitly notes that comparing graphs and clique complexes directly is difficult because of major size/structure differences, and it acknowledges that the tested rewiring algorithms were not designed for weighted directed influence graphs. That self-awareness strengthens confidence in the parts of the paper that are claimed more modestly.
- **The rewiring heuristic is simple, reusable, and easy for others to build on.** Algorithm 1 is straightforward: collapse the relational structure to a graph, run a graph rewiring method, then add the resulting edges as a new relation. Even if its empirical benefits are mixed, it is a reasonable baseline adaptation that future work can refine.

## Weaknesses

###: Fatal
- **Section 3.4 does not substantiate its mechanistic claim that increasing hidden dimension mitigates oversquashing.** The argument given is that if \(\beta_i^{(\ell)} = O(p_{i,\ell})\) and \(\alpha^{(\ell)} = O(p_{\ell+1})\), then the *upper bound* in Lemma 3.2 scales with width (Eq. 13), after which the paper states: “This implies that low hidden dimensions... can exacerbate the oversquashing problem. Increasing the hidden dimensions will raise the upper bound, which can help improve the model's ability to propagate information effectively.” That inference does not follow from the math presented. A looser upper bound on sensitivity is not evidence of larger actual sensitivity, nor of better information propagation. Since hidden dimensions are listed as one of the paper’s core theoretical contributions, this is a substantive flaw in the paper’s theoretical interpretation.

### Major:
- **Several theoretical claims are stronger than what the actual results show: the paper mostly provides graph-style upper-bound analyses on induced influence graphs, not a distinctly higher-order theory of oversquashing.** This matters because the framing repeatedly suggests that the paper analyzes oversquashing *in topological message passing* in a deeper structural sense. In practice, much of Section 3 works by collapsing relational structure into \(\tilde A\)/\(B\) and reusing graph machinery there. That is still useful, but it is narrower than claims like “extend key graph-theoretic results to higher-order structures” may lead readers to expect. The stronger version would require results showing how higher-order/topological structure affects oversquashing in ways not already captured by the induced graph reduction.
- **The empirical evidence for the practical rewiring claims is mixed and does not support broad statements that rewiring generally mitigates oversquashing or that topological models “respond similarly” to graph models.** Table 1 is visibly inconsistent: rewiring helps in many cases, hurts in others, and sometimes changes little. The paper’s own text says the impact “varies across datasets,” which is fair, but the abstract/conclusion language is more positive than the evidence warrants. Likewise, “respond similarly” is not operationalized or quantified. There is no aggregate analysis of effect sizes, win rates, or interaction by model family to support that comparative claim.
- **The real-world experimental protocol is exploratory rather than conclusive because the paper deliberately uses fixed, dataset- and model-agnostic hyperparameters for rewiring.** The paper explicitly states: “We use fixed, dataset- and model-agnostic hyperparameters, diverging from prior work where hyperparameter sweeps are carried out.” That is acceptable for a stress test of a heuristic, but it weakens any stronger comparative conclusion about rewiring effectiveness across model families and liftings, because different models are unlikely to respond uniformly to the same rewiring hyperparameters. The paper itself notes that tuning can significantly impact performance; that caveat substantially limits what should be concluded from Table 1.
- **The curvature/local-geometry story is under-justified as geometry of the original relational object rather than of the collapsed influence graph.** Definition 3.3 introduces an “extended Forman curvature” on weighted directed influence graphs, and Proposition 3.4 then derives a bound in terms of that quantity. But the paper does not convincingly establish that this curvature captures meaningful local geometry of the original simplicial/relational structure rather than just motif statistics of the induced graph. Without either stronger theoretical justification or direct empirical evidence that this curvature predicts oversquashing-relevant behavior better than simpler graph statistics, the geometric interpretation remains somewhat instrumental.

### Minor
- **Theorem 3.5 is meaningful but its interpretation depends heavily on assumptions that the paper calls “non-restrictive” too casually.** In particular, row-normalization directly shapes \(M\) and therefore the decay regime in Eq. 12. The theorem is best read as a conditional result under a common but materially consequential normalization choice, not as a broadly assumption-light explanation of depth-induced oversquashing.
- **The influence-graph/collapsed-graph reductions may discard higher-order distinctions, and the paper does not analyze what is lost.** This matters because the paper’s central conceptual move is to study higher-order message passing through graph proxies. A short discussion or ablation on alternative collapses would have helped assess whether important structure is being erased by construction.
- **The synthetic experiments are only directionally consistent with the theory; they do not directly validate the quantities appearing in the theorems.** Section 5.2 varies width, ring size, and rewiring iterations on RINGTRANSFER, which is sensible. But these experiments measure downstream accuracy, not the Jacobian sensitivities, path counts, \(M\), or curvature quantities used in the theory. So “consistent with” is fair, whereas “confirm” is too strong.
- **The evaluation of the rewiring heuristic is incomplete across all dataset/lifting/rewiring combinations due to computational constraints.** This is not fatal, but it leaves the practical story somewhat patchy.

### Trivial
- **Some terminology occasionally slides from “small sensitivity upper bound” to “oversquashing explanation” too quickly.** The distinction is important: the lemmas give sufficient conditions for weak long-range influence in a normed sensitivity bound, not a full characterization of oversquashing.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Add a direct empirical probe of the theoretical quantities in Section 3: Jacobian sensitivity, path counts in the influence graph, or before/after rewiring changes in distances/connectivity.
- Provide an aggregate analysis of rewiring effects by model family and lifting, instead of relying on case-by-case reading of Table 1.
- Include a small ablation on the collapsed adjacency construction in Algorithm 1 to test whether different projections of the relational structure materially change rewiring outcomes.
- Quantify the computational overhead of lifting plus rewiring, especially since adding relations/edges can increase message-passing cost.
- If kept in the paper, reposition Section 3.4 as heuristic discussion unless a stronger width-specific theory is added.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.

- **“Weak baselines / missing state-of-the-art comparisons / compare to more rewiring methods / more model families.”** Removed as a core weakness because this paper already evaluates a fairly broad set of model types for its stated goal: graph (SGC/GCN/GIN), relational graph (RGCN/RGIN), and topological (SIN/CIN/CIN++) across multiple lifts and datasets. Asking for still more baselines is largely a generic escalation request rather than a paper-specific flaw. It remains a nice-to-have, not a central acceptance issue.
- **“No comparison against direct topological oversquashing baselines like skip connections or increased hidden dimensions.”** Removed as a major criticism because this extends beyond the paper’s stated scope, which is primarily a unifying framework plus a simple transfer of rewiring heuristics, not a comprehensive benchmark of all mitigation strategies.
- **“No cellular/CW experiments despite broader claims.”** Weakened/removed as a main weakness. The paper does say the framework extends to cellular complexes, but the title and experiments are explicitly framed as a *case study on simplicial message passing*. It is reasonable not to empirically cover every higher-order domain in a first paper.
- **“TUDatasets are too small / should use larger long-range benchmarks.”** Weakened and moved out of the core weaknesses. A request for LRGB-style benchmarks would strengthen the paper, but the current evaluation already includes a synthetic long-range benchmark designed for oversquashing and multiple real-world datasets. This is not a decisive flaw.

## Novel Insights
The most important synthesis across the reviews is that the paper’s actual contribution is strongest when read as a **transport framework** rather than as a full new theory of topological oversquashing. Section 2 plus the influence-graph construction successfully create a route by which graph oversquashing analyses can be ported to simplicial/relational settings. But once viewed this way, the paper should be judged less on whether each derived bound is “deeply topological” and more on whether the transport preserves meaningful higher-order structure. That reframing clarifies both the paper’s value and its current limitation: it is a solid abstraction layer, but not yet a definitive higher-order oversquashing theory. The paper would be stronger if it embraced this identity more explicitly instead of sometimes overselling the resulting analyses as intrinsically topological.

## Suggestions
- **Fix Section 3.4 decisively.** Either remove the claim that width theoretically mitigates oversquashing, or replace it with a clearly labeled heuristic/speculative discussion plus controlled experiments that directly measure long-range signal transmission.
- **Tone down the strongest empirical claims.** Replace “confirm” with “are consistent with,” and avoid implying that rewiring broadly helps or that topological models respond similarly to graph models unless you provide aggregate comparative analysis.
- **Clarify the scope of the theory.** Reframe the contribution as a reduction that enables graph-style sensitivity and geometry analyses on induced influence graphs for relational message passing.
- **Strengthen the rewiring section with direct oversquashing metrics.** Show before/after rewiring changes in path length, influence connectivity, or Jacobian sensitivity on synthetic structures.
- **Discuss information loss in the reductions.** A short analysis of what the influence graph and collapsed adjacency omit would make the framework more convincing.
- **Be more precise about assumptions.** In particular, row-normalization should not be called “non-restrictive” without qualification.

## Score and Decision
**Novelty:** good. The relational reframing is genuinely useful and not just cosmetic.  
**Technical soundness:** mixed. Section 2 and parts of Section 3 are sound/plausible, but Section 3.4 overinterprets a bound in a way that is not technically justified.  
**Empirical support:** moderate-to-weak for the strongest practical claims. The experiments are broad enough to be informative, but the evidence is mixed and the conclusions should be more cautious.  
**Significance:** moderate. The framework could be impactful for future TDL analysis, but the paper does not yet deliver a fully convincing practical or mechanistic story about oversquashing mitigation.  
**Clarity:** generally decent, with some overclaiming and a few places where interpretation outruns theorem content.

**Calibration against human-reviewed papers:**
- Compared to **Topological Blindspots** (`EzjsoomYEb.md`, accepted oral, 8/8/8): this submission is clearly less complete and less decisive. That paper paired a strong conceptual/theoretical contribution with clearer, more targeted empirical support and a sharper answer to its central question.
- Compared to **SaNN** (`eUgS9Ig8JG.md`, accepted spotlight, 6/6/8/8): this paper is somewhat similar in being a meaningful simplicial/TDL contribution with real technical content, but here the empirical/practical story is weaker and one theoretical section is materially unsound in interpretation. So I place it below SaNN.
- Compared to **Simplicial Representation Learning with Neural k-Forms** (`Djw0XhjHZb.md`, accepted poster, 8/6/5/6/5): this paper has a stronger unifying abstraction than that work, but a weaker end-to-end validation story and less clean alignment between theory and claims. Roughly similar overall quality, perhaps slightly below.
- Compared to **The Effectiveness of Curvature-Based Rewiring Revisited** (`EcrdmRT99M.md`, accepted poster, 6/5/6/6): this submission is conceptually stronger and more ambitious, but its practical rewiring evidence is likewise nuanced rather than decisive. The best comparison is that this paper is somewhat above that one in novelty, but dragged down by the Section 3.4 issue.
- Compared to **Locality-Aware Graph Rewiring** (`4Ua4hKiAJX.md`, accepted poster, 8/5/3/6): that paper offered a stronger practical method claim; this one offers a stronger abstraction/unification claim. Because this submission’s practical story is weaker and one theoretical claim is unsoundly interpreted, I would not score it above that acceptance band.

**Overall judgment:** borderline but leaning reject. There is a real paper here, and a potentially important framework, but ICLR standards are high, and the current version overclaims both theoretically and empirically. The hidden-dimension section in particular needs correction, and the practical conclusions need to be narrowed to match the evidence.
