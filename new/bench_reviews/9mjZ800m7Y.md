## Summary

MODNAS proposes a multi-objective differentiable NAS algorithm that trains a single MetaHypernetwork conditioned on preference vectors and hardware device embeddings to generate Pareto-optimal architectures across multiple devices without repeated search. The core idea is to amortize the cost of profiling the Pareto front—normally requiring O(C×T) separate search runs across constraints C and devices T—into one conditional training run, enabling zero-shot transfer to new hardware targets. The method combines a bank of hypernetworks (MetaHypernetwork), a shared supernet for accuracy estimation, pretrained hardware-metric predictors (MetaPredictor), and Multiple Gradient Descent (MGD) across devices. Experiments span four search spaces (NAS-Bench-201, OFA/MobileNetV3, HAT, HW-GPT-Bench), up to 19 devices, and up to 3 objectives.

---

## Claims and Support

**Claim 1: MODNAS profiles the Pareto front across multiple devices in a single search run.**
*Partially supported.* The method trains once and generates 24 architectures per device via sampled preference vectors. That the trained hypernetwork can generate diverse candidates quickly is well-supported. That the resulting 24-point set constitutes a strong Pareto front approximation is supported by HV results across four benchmarks. However, HV alone—without diversity/spread metrics or comparison against a reference front in coverage—cannot fully establish "representative and diverse" profiling as claimed.

**Claim 2: Zero-shot transfer to new devices.**
*Partially supported.* Held-out device results in all four settings (6 test devices in NAS-Bench-201, 1 in HAT, 1 in OFA, 8 GPU devices in HW-GPT-Bench) show competitive performance. The paper references Figure 25 (appendix) showing generalization with only 2 search devices. However, no ablation removes device conditioning or shuffles device embeddings, so whether the device-conditioned mechanism specifically enables transfer (vs. general architecture search quality) is not mechanistically verified. The t-SNE clustering used as supporting evidence is descriptive, not predictive.

**Claim 3: MODNAS is the first gradient-based method to provide a "global view of the Pareto solutions" in a single run.**
*Partially supported for the amortized single-run aspect; overstated for "global view."* The practical contribution—one conditional training run covers all device/constraint combinations—is genuine and meaningful. But "global view of the Pareto solutions" overstates what 24 sampled points from linear scalarizations demonstrate.

**Claim 4: MODNAS outperforms existing MOO NAS methods across all evaluated settings.**
*Well supported empirically.* Results in Figures 3, 5, 9, 10, and 11 consistently show MODNAS achieving higher HV than all baselines. The NAS-Bench-201 results include means of 3 runs. The baseline set is somewhat dominated by black-box MOO methods, and the paper gives baselines 2.5× more runtime budget on HAT, which is a fair or intentionally favorable comparison rather than a limitation.

**Claim 5: O(1) search cost reduction.**
*Misleading as stated.* Algorithm 1 explicitly iterates over all T devices in lines 2 and 10 per training step; training cost scales with T. The paper does acknowledge this in Section 4.4: *"Although MODNAS iterates over T devices to compute g_n\* and g_m\*, Figure 25 in Appendix H.1 shows that MODNAS generalizes well on 17 test devices with only 2 search devices."* The paper's O(1) claim in Table 1 is best understood as "one single training run regardless of number of device/constraint combinations at deployment time," not an asymptotic per-iteration claim. This distinction is important but never explicitly drawn in the main text, creating a misleading impression.

**Claim 6: MGD is an effective update scheme for the MetaHypernetwork.**
*Supported for NAS-Bench-201.* Figure 6 compares MGD against mean, sequential, and MC-sampled gradients, showing better HV and faster convergence. Ablation is limited to this one setting.

**Claim 7: MODNAS demonstrates effectiveness "beyond convex Pareto fronts."**
*Unsupported as stated.* The paper uses linear scalarization throughout (Eq. 5). Linear scalarization provably cannot recover non-convex Pareto regions. Section 4.3 states: *"which features a non-convex Pareto front between perplexity and hardware metric objectives... demonstrating its effectiveness in optimizing beyond convex Pareto fronts."* Doing well on a benchmark with a non-convex front is not evidence of recovering the non-convex portions; MODNAS could simply be recovering good points on the convex hull. No analysis identifies which returned points are in non-convex regions.

## Strengths

- **Practical amortization is a genuine contribution.** The key insight—train one conditional model, then query it for any device/preference at near-zero marginal cost—addresses a real and expensive pain point in hardware-aware NAS. The reduction from O(CT) repeated full searches to one training run is clearly practically valuable.

- **Comprehensive empirical scope.** Four qualitatively different search spaces (cell-based CNN, MobileNet, encoder-decoder transformer, decoder-only GPT), three tasks, up to 19 devices, and 3 objectives is among the most thorough multi-device NAS evaluations in recent literature.

- **MODNAS consistently outperforms all baselines.** Results across Figures 3, 5, 9, 10, and 11 show robust improvements in HV across devices, baselines, and search spaces. Notably, baselines are given equal or greater compute budgets (2.5× in HAT), making the comparisons favorable to baselines and the wins more credible.

- **Well-designed ablations.** The MGD vs. mean/sequential/MC-sampling comparison (Figure 6) clearly validates the gradient aggregation design. The RHPN baseline isolates the contribution of learned architecture generation. The constrained variant study (Figure 7) adds practical flexibility.

- **Reproducibility.** Code is publicly available, and implementation details are described in appendices. The NAS-Bench-201 section uses a tabular benchmark, making results especially reliable.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None identified. The core contribution—amortized single-run Pareto front approximation—is real and empirically supported. The weaknesses below are overstated claims or missing validation, not fundamental methodological failures.*

### Major

- **The non-convex Pareto front claim is methodologically unsupported.** The paper explicitly states the GPT space "features a non-convex Pareto front" and concludes MODNAS demonstrates "effectiveness in optimizing beyond convex Pareto fronts" (Sec. 4.3). However, the method uses linear scalarization (Eq. 5) throughout—an approach that provably cannot recover solutions in non-convex (non-supported) regions of the Pareto front. Strong performance on HW-GPT-Bench confirms MODNAS finds good points, but does not establish recovery of non-convex regions. The paper does not analyze which returned points are in non-convex regions, nor does it acknowledge the theoretical tension between its scalarization approach and this claim. This claim should be narrowed or removed.

- **The O(1) complexity framing is misleading.** Table 1 labels MODNAS search cost as O(1), but Algorithm 1 loops over all T devices per training step (lines 2 and 10). The paper acknowledges this in Section 4.4 and mitigates it via meta-learning generalization from 2 search devices to 17 test devices (Figure 25). However, this important nuance is in the appendix, and the main-text framing presents O(1) without qualification, conflating "number of full search runs needed at deployment" with computational complexity. This is not fatal—the paper's practical amortization claim stands—but the framing should be corrected to avoid misleading readers about actual training cost.

- **Zero-shot transfer mechanism is asserted but not validated.** The paper claims device conditioning enables zero-shot transfer to new hardware and states that "the MetaHypernetwork learns to map the new test device to the most similar device" (Sec. 3.2). This is plausible but mechanistically unverified: no ablation removes device conditioning, shuffles device embeddings, or compares against a device-agnostic baseline on held-out devices. The t-SNE clustering of device embeddings (Figure 12, appendix) is descriptive, not causal evidence. Given that zero-shot hardware transfer is one of the paper's explicitly stated contributions, this is a significant evidential gap.

### Minor

- **Evaluation relies exclusively on hypervolume.** HV measures dominated volume but does not capture Pareto front coverage, spread, or uniformity. A method achieving high HV from clustered solutions near the extremes would appear competitive despite poor coverage of the front. Diversity metrics (e.g., spacing, inverted generational distance) would directly validate the stated goal of "representative and diverse" architectures, which HV alone cannot.

- **MetaPredictor error propagation is unanalyzed.** The entire hardware-objective optimization relies on pretrained predictors. The paper notes in limitations that "the method relies on differentiable proxies for objectives, which may not always align with ground truth values," but provides no quantitative analysis of predictor accuracy (R², MAE) or sensitivity of the returned Pareto set to predictor errors. This is especially relevant for the OFA and HAT experiments where final architectures are not retrained from scratch.

- **MGD ablation is limited to NAS-Bench-201.** The update scheme comparison (Figure 6) is shown only in the cell-based CNN setting. Whether MGD's advantages persist on the transformer spaces is not verified.

### Trivial

- Reference point ρ for HV computation is not explicitly stated in all experiments; this should be reported for complete reproducibility.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Pareto front scatter plots showing spread of the 24 sampled architectures.** Current front plots (e.g., Fig. 9) show MODNAS vs. baselines but do not clearly show whether MODNAS's own 24 points are uniformly spread or clustered.

- **Architecture visualization as preference varies.** Showing how discovered architectures change structurally (depth, width, kernel sizes) as the preference vector shifts from accuracy-heavy to latency-heavy would make the method's behavior more interpretable.

- **Sensitivity to Dirichlet concentration parameter β.** The uniform Dirichlet (β=1) is used throughout without analysis of whether this is optimal for non-convex front coverage.

## Novel Insights

The reviewers collectively highlight one genuinely underappreciated tension: the paper uses linear scalarization as its multi-objective mechanism, which is provably restricted to recovering the convex hull of the Pareto front, yet claims effectiveness on non-convex fronts. While this criticism applies equally to many hypernetwork-based MOO papers, its appearance here is particularly visible because the paper explicitly advertises HW-GPT-Bench's non-convex front as a distinct contribution. Resolving this would either require replacing linear scalarization with a Chebyshev or hypervolume-based scalarization, or explicitly demonstrating via front geometry analysis that the conditional hypernetwork produces points outside the convex hull—which is theoretically conceivable (the learned distribution may produce such architectures) but unverified. This tension is the paper's most interesting unresolved methodological question.

---

## Suggestions

1. **Remove or carefully caveat the non-convex front claim.** Either analyze which returned architectures from HW-GPT-Bench lie in non-convex regions of the true front, or replace "beyond convex Pareto fronts" with "settings where the true front is non-convex," which makes a weaker and accurate claim.

2. **Reframe the O(1) complexity claim in Table 1.** Add a footnote or sentence in Section 4.4 clarifying that O(1) refers to "number of full search runs required at deployment time" and that training cost itself scales with the number of search devices (though meta-learning enables amortization with as few as 2 devices in practice).

3. **Add a device-conditioning ablation.** Even a single experiment comparing MODNAS with and without device features on the NAS-Bench-201 held-out devices would strongly validate the hardware transfer mechanism.

4. **Report one diversity/spread metric alongside HV.** Reporting spacing or IGD for NAS-Bench-201 (where ground truth is available) would directly validate the "representative and diverse" claim.

5. **Clarify MGD's role precisely.** The paper sometimes implies MGD optimizes all objectives jointly, but as implemented, MGD resolves gradient conflict across *devices* while within-device objective trade-offs are handled by linear scalarization. Stating this distinction clearly would prevent overreading of the theoretical language in Section 3.3.
