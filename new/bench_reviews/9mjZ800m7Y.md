## Summary

MODNAS proposes a multi-objective differentiable NAS method that uses a MetaHypernetwork conditioned on preference vectors and device embeddings, combined with multiple gradient descent (MGD), to profile the Pareto front across multiple hardware devices and objectives in a single search run. The method reduces the cost of constructing Pareto fronts from O(CT) independent searches to a single training procedure, and supports zero-shot transfer to unseen devices via learned device embeddings.

## Strengths

- **Principled and novel integration for multi-device multi-objective NAS.** Combining preference-conditioned hypernetworks with device embeddings and MGD for simultaneous optimization across devices is a clean and well-motivated design. The MetaHypernetwork architecture (bank of K sub-networks with attention-based device routing) is reasonable, and the ablation in Figure 6 validates that MGD outperforms simpler gradient aggregation schemes.

- **Impressive experimental breadth.** The paper evaluates on 4 search spaces (NAS-Bench-201, MobileNetV3/OFA, HAT transformer, HW-GPT-Bench), 3 tasks (classification, MT, LM), up to 19 hardware devices, and up to 3 objectives. This scope is uncommon in NAS papers and demonstrates genuine generalizability of the proposed approach. On NAS-Bench-201 (where ground truth is available), MODNAS consistently achieves higher hypervolume than baselines across all 19 devices (Figure 3).

- **Practical efficiency gains.** The core practical contribution — producing a full Pareto front for multiple devices from a single search run rather than repeating search per device/constraint — is genuinely useful and clearly demonstrated. Table 1 correctly shows the reduction from O(CT) to O(1) Pareto set build cost, and the NB201 experiments show MODNAS matching or exceeding baselines that receive equal or greater compute budgets (Figure 5).

- **Zero-shot device transfer with meaningful evidence.** The learned device embeddings produce sensible clustering (Figure 12 in appendix), and the method achieves strong HV on held-out devices. The ablation in Figure 25 (appendix) showing good generalization with only 2 search devices is a notable practical result.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **Linear scalarization cannot guarantee coverage of non-convex Pareto fronts, but the paper claims effectiveness on non-convex settings without addressing this limitation.** Linear scalarization (weighted sums) is well-known to fail for non-convex regions of the Pareto front. The paper explicitly claims that HW-GPT-Bench has a "non-convex Pareto front" and demonstrates "effectiveness in optimizing beyond convex Pareto fronts" (Section 4.3), yet no methodological modification is made beyond standard linear scalarization, and no theoretical argument is provided for why the hypernetwork mapping would circumvent this limitation. While it is *possible* that the learned mapping compensates partially, this is not analyzed — there is no comparison against methods that can provably cover non-convex fronts (ε-constraint approaches, Chebyshev scalarizations), nor any analysis of which Pareto front regions are recovered vs. missed. This directly undermines the central claim of profiling the *full* Pareto front.

- **MetaPredictor quality is central to the method's empirical claims but is not validated.** All hardware objectives (latency, energy) during search are replaced by predictor outputs, and the predictors are pre-trained and frozen. However, the paper provides no quantitative error metrics (R², RMSE, calibration) for any predictor, no analysis of systematic biases in particular architecture/device regions, and no sensitivity analysis to prediction noise. Since the optimization landscape MODNAS navigates is the predictor's approximation of the true landscape, the quality of the reported Pareto fronts depends directly on predictor fidelity. On NAS-Bench-201 (tabular), final architectures can be evaluated against ground truth, which partially mitigates this — but for HAT, OFA, and HW-GPT-Bench, no such validation exists. The limitation section mentions proxy misalignment in one sentence but treats it as a caveat rather than a central threat to empirical validity.

- **The O(1) complexity claim is misleading.** The abstract, introduction (Contribution 2), Section 3.1, and Table 1 all describe MODNAS's cost as "O(1)" in the number of devices and constraints. However, Algorithm 1 explicitly loops over all T devices in both the architecture update (lines 2–8) and weight update (lines 10–15), making per-iteration cost O(T). The MGD Frank-Wolfe solver also scales at least linearly with T. The correct claim is that MODNAS requires *one supernet training* rather than O(CT) independent searches, with per-step cost linear in T. As written, "O(1)" suggests essentially constant cost regardless of the number of devices, which the algorithm does not support.

- **Evaluation for large-scale experiments relies on supernetwork accuracy proxies without from-scratch validation.** In the OFA and HW-GPT-Bench experiments, supernetwork weights are frozen and the search only updates the MetaHypernetwork. The reported hypervolumes are computed using supernetwork accuracy, not actual independently trained architectures. This is standard NAS practice but well-known to have ranking quality issues, especially for Transformer spaces. For NAS-Bench-201 (tabular), ground truth is available and the claims are well-supported; for the other three spaces, the reported advantages over baselines may conflate supernet proxy quality with genuine multi-objective optimization improvements, particularly since baselines use different (often cheaper) accuracy surrogates.

### Minor:

- **The number of preference vectors (24) used for Pareto front profiling is fixed without analysis.** No sensitivity analysis or theoretical discussion is provided on how the number of sampled preference vectors affects Pareto front coverage, especially for M=3 objectives where the simplex is 2-dimensional and uniform Dirichlet(1,1,1) sampling may concentrate points away from boundaries. This is relevant because HV is sensitive to front coverage density.

- **No ablation on the number of sub-hypernetworks K or comparison to simpler MetaHypernetwork designs** (e.g., a single hypernetwork with concatenated inputs). The bank-of-K design is presented without justifying why this capacity is needed or how performance scales with K.

- **The "comparison to constrained single-objective optimization" (Section 4.1, Figure 7) uses MetaD2A+HELP requiring 24 separate search runs, which is a strong baseline but was given an asymmetric advantage.** This is a valid and correctly designed comparison (giving the baseline more resources strengthens the paper's claim), but the text could be clearer about this asymmetry.

### Trivial:

- Statistical significance: only 3 independent runs are reported with no standard deviations or confidence intervals for most experiments, though this is common in the NAS community.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Comparison against preference-conditioned hypernetwork methods (e.g., PHN/Navon et al. 2021, COSMOS) adapted for NAS would isolate the contribution of MGD + device conditioning from the hypernetwork paradigm itself.

- Analysis of how HV degrades as the number of training devices decreases further (beyond the 2-device result mentioned in the appendix), or as test devices become more distant from training devices, would strengthen the zero-shot transfer claims.

- From-scratch retraining of the top architectures from at least one large-scale Pareto front (e.g., OFA on ImageNet) would confirm that supernetwork-based advantages translate to real performance gains.

- Providing predictor error metrics (R², RMSE on held-out architectures) for each device and objective would allow readers to assess the reliability of the optimization landscape.

## Removed Points

- **"Missing comparison to preference-conditioned hypernetwork MOO methods (PHN, COSMOS)"** — The human finder flags this, but these methods are from different communities (general MOO/Pareto front learning, not NAS). The paper's baselines are appropriate for the NAS context where the comparison set is black-box MOO methods and existing hardware-aware NAS approaches. A discussion of connection to these methods would be informative but not a required comparison. **Kept as a nice-to-have rather than a weakness.**

- **"Incremental novelty — just combining existing components"** — This is a fair observation but overstates the case. While the components (hypernetworks, MGD, device embeddings from HELP) exist individually, their specific integration for multi-device multi-objective differentiable NAS — with the MetaHypernetwork bank+attention design and the bi-level optimization with MGD over both preference and device dimensions — is a genuine contribution. Papers like ParetoFlow (accepted with similar novelty patterns) and POMONAG (rejected for more fundamental issues) suggest the novelty bar is met here.

- **"Frozen supernetwork reduces method to post-hoc architecture selection"** — This characterizes the OFA/GPT experiments but not NB201 (where full joint training happens) or HAT (where search is more extensive). The frozen-supernet setting is a practical choice for large-scale experiments that is standard in the NAS literature (OFA itself does this), and the paper is transparent about it.

- **"No accounting of predictor pretraining cost in complexity analysis"** — The predictor pretraining costs (3-6 GPU hours) are negligible compared to the search costs (22 GPU hours for NB201, 6 days on 8 GPUs for HAT), and the paper does mention these costs in the experiment sections. This is a minor presentation issue, not a fundamental flaw.

- **"Demanding standalone training of architectures on ImageNet/WMT/OpenWebText"** — This is impractical for a NAS paper evaluating 24 architectures per device per method × multiple methods × multiple devices. The NB201 tabular benchmark provides exact ground truth, and the supernet proxy is the standard evaluation protocol in the field for larger spaces.

## Novel Insights

The observation that combining MGD across device gradients with preference-conditioned hypernetworks naturally produces a unified gradient direction that respects multi-device multi-objective trade-offs — rather than relying on separate per-device optimizations — is genuinely novel for NAS. The empirical finding that only 2 search devices are sufficient for good generalization (Figure 25) is practically important and non-obvious: it suggests the MetaHypernetwork learns generalizable device embeddings rather than simply memorizing training devices. However, the paper misses the opportunity to analyze *why* this works — whether the embedding space is smooth enough to interpolate, or whether the attention mechanism over the bank of K hypernetworks plays a critical role in this transfer.

## Suggestions

- Replace "O(1)" complexity claims with the more precise statement: one supernet training with per-iteration cost O(T), and O(1) Pareto set construction cost (a single forward pass per preference vector and device).
- Provide quantitative predictor error metrics (R², RMSE by device and by objective) for at least the NB201 and HAT settings, and a brief sensitivity analysis showing that moderate predictor noise does not substantially change HV rankings.
- Discuss the linear scalarization limitation explicitly: either analyze what fraction of the true Pareto front is recovered on the HW-GPT-Bench non-convex front, or replace/augment with Chebyshev scalarization and compare.
- For at least one experiment beyond NB201 (e.g., OFA on ImageNet), fine-tune and evaluate the top few Pareto architectures from scratch to confirm that supernetwork rankings hold.
