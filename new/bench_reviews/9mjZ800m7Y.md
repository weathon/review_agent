## Summary
MODNAS proposes a hardware-aware multi-objective differentiable NAS algorithm that approximates the full Pareto front across multiple devices and objectives in a single search run. The core idea is a MetaHypernetwork conditioned on both preference vectors (encoding accuracy/hardware trade-offs) and device embeddings, updated via Multiple Gradient Descent (MGD) over all device tasks simultaneously. Experiments span four search spaces (NAS-Bench-201, MobileNetV3/OFA, HAT Transformer, HW-GPT-Bench), three tasks (image classification, machine translation, language modeling), up to 19 hardware devices, and up to 3 objectives, demonstrating the method's scalability and superiority over both black-box and constrained single-objective NAS baselines.

---

## Strengths

- **O(1) Pareto set build cost (Table 1, Section 4.4):** MODNAS is the only method that achieves O(1) in both search cost and Pareto set build cost. Competing methods like MetaD2A+HELP require O(CT), ProxylessNAS O(CT), and OFA+HELP O(CT) to build the Pareto set. This is the central efficiency claim and is backed concretely by the cost table.

- **MetaHypernetwork design enabling zero-shot transfer (Section 3.2, Figure 12):** The bank-of-hypernetworks + linear attention over device feature vectors is a parameter-efficient, modular design. The initialization to a uniform architectural distribution is a principled inductive bias. The learned device embeddings cluster similar hardware correctly (Figure 12), providing interpretable evidence of effective meta-learning.

- **MGD ablation (Figure 6):** The ablation cleanly isolates the value of MGD over mean gradient, sequential updates, and MC sampling. MGD achieves higher final HV, better anytime performance, and faster convergence. This is a well-designed experiment that directly supports the key algorithmic design choice.

- **Comparison to MetaD2A+HELP (Figure 7):** The most direct and honest test of the paper's claims — running MetaD2A+HELP 24 times with different constraints vs. a single MODNAS run — shows MODNAS produces more diverse Pareto sets with higher hypervolume, including on test devices.

- **Breadth of evaluation (Sections 4.1–4.3):** Four qualitatively different search spaces, three tasks, 19 devices, and 3 objectives in a single paper is genuinely impressive scope. The RS+MODNAS control, which decouples architecture quality from search efficiency, is a thoughtful addition that strengthens the empirical analysis.

- **Scalability to 3 objectives (Figure 8):** MODNAS scales to M=3 (accuracy + latency + energy) without additional hyperparameter tuning, achieving HV close to the global optimum on FPGA and Eyeriss.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **MetaPredictor accuracy is never characterized, yet it is the backbone of all hardware-objective evaluation.** The MetaPredictor (a frozen GCN for NAS-Bench-201, MLP for OFA) is used directly as L_t^m in the upper-level optimization (Equation 5), and the paper explicitly relies on it for zero-shot device transfer. No MSE, Spearman rank correlation, or worst-case error on held-out architectures is reported anywhere for either train or test devices. The paper acknowledges in the Limitations section that "the method relies on differentiable proxies for objectives, which may not always align with ground truth values," but provides no quantification. Without predictor accuracy metrics — especially on the 6 test devices in NAS-Bench-201 that were not used during predictor training — the claim that generated architectures are truly Pareto-optimal in hardware latency rests on unvalidated proxies. This is the most significant empirical gap in the paper.

### Minor

- **MGD convergence framing overstates the formal guarantee.** Section 3.3 invokes MGD's property of monotonically decreasing all objectives and converging to a Pareto stationary point. However, in Algorithm 1 (lines 2–6), a different scalarization **r** is independently sampled for each device at each step. This means each per-device gradient g_Φ^t is estimated under a different r, making the formal convergence of MGD (which requires fixed objective gradients) inapplicable. The algorithm is better framed as a stochastic approximation where MGD serves as a heuristic gradient regularizer across devices, supported empirically by Figure 6. The distinction is real but not fatal — the algorithm works well empirically — however the theoretical framing should be corrected for precision.

- **Zero-shot transfer is asserted but not quantified.** The paper correctly identifies 13 training devices and 6 test devices on NAS-Bench-201, and the radar plot (Figure 3) marks test devices in red. However, no table or analysis compares HV on training vs. test devices quantitatively. The claim in Contribution 2 ("zero-shot transferability to new hardware devices") is supported only qualitatively by Figure 3 and the t-SNE clustering. A simple table reporting average HV on train vs. test device subsets for MODNAS vs. a device-specific baseline would directly validate or constrain the claim.

- **Budget comparison opacity in the NAS-Bench-201 experiment.** Section 4.1 states baselines are allocated "the same search time budget across all devices equivalent to the MODNAS search + evaluation (22 GPU hours)." On a tabular benchmark like NAS-Bench-201, where accuracy is a lookup and latency uses a HELP predictor, 22 GPU hours could support millions of architecture evaluations. The paper separately shows Figure 5 (honest comparison: 24 MODNAS evaluations vs. 50 for baselines) but the radar plot in Figure 3 uses the wall-clock budget without explaining what uses those 22 GPU hours for black-box methods. The methodology should be clarified.

### Trivial

- Table 1 (computational complexity) omits the MetaPredictor pretraining cost (3–6 GPU hours on architecture-latency pairs), which is a required prerequisite. While small, it should appear for completeness.

- Similarly, the amortized cost of pretraining the OFA and HW-GPT-Bench supernetworks (thousands of GPU-hours) is silently embedded in the O(1) search cost claim for those experiments. A brief note acknowledging this would improve transparency.

## Novel Insights

The most underappreciated observation in the reviews is that MODNAS achieves competitive performance on non-convex Pareto fronts (HW-GPT-Bench, Figure 11) despite using linear scalarization — a combination that is theoretically expected to fail for non-convex regions. This likely emerges from the MetaHypernetwork's ability to interpolate across the preference simplex using discrete architecture sampling (which can escape local linear minima), not from any property of linear scalarization per se. This interaction between hypernetwork-based amortized optimization and discrete architecture spaces may explain why the method generalizes better than purely continuous MOO approaches.
