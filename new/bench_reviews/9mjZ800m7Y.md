Based on my thorough reading of the paper and cross-checking against the reviewer claims, here is my consolidated review.

## Summary

This paper proposes MODNAS, a differentiable neural architecture search (NAS) method that uses a device-conditioned MetaHypernetwork together with Multiple Gradient Descent (MGD) to profile Pareto-optimal architectures across multiple hardware devices and conflicting objectives in a single search run. The method is evaluated on four distinct search spaces (NAS-Bench-201, MobileNetV3/OFA, Hardware-Aware Transformer, and GPT-2), across up to 19 devices and three objectives. The core idea—conditioning a hypernetwork on both preference vectors and hardware embeddings to amortize search cost across devices—is novel and well-motivated.

## Strengths

- **Novel algorithmic synthesis.** The combination of a MetaHypernetwork with a bank of $K$ hypernetworks modulated by learned device attention, optimized via MGD for multi-task, multi-objective NAS, is creative and distinguishes the method from prior scalarization approaches (Section 3.2, Figure 2, Algorithm 1). The Frank-Wolfe solver for combining device-specific gradients is a non-trivial extension of MGD to the supernet setting.
- **Exceptional breadth of evaluation.** The paper tackles four qualitatively different search spaces (CNN cell, MobileNetV3, encoder-decoder Transformer, decoder-only GPT-2) across image classification, machine translation, and language modeling, with up to 19 hardware devices and 3 objectives (Sections 4.1–4.3). This breadth is unusual in the NAS literature and provides strong evidence for generality when the comparisons are fair.
- **Compelling tabular-benchmark efficiency.** On NAS-Bench-201, MODNAS achieves higher hypervolume than independent per-device baseline searches (Figure 3) and reaches high hypervolume with far fewer architecture evaluations than black-box methods (Figure 5). The ablation showing MGD outperforms naïve gradient aggregation schemes (Figure 6) further validates the design choice.
- **Zero-shot device generalization.** The learned device embeddings enable transfer to held-out test devices without retraining the hypernetwork (Section 3.2, Figure 3), and the t-SNE clustering analysis (Appendix Figure 12) provides evidence that the embedding space captures hardware similarity.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Ambiguous and potentially unfair baseline comparison on NAS-Bench-201.** Section 4.1 states that baselines are allocated “the same search time budget across all devices equivalent to the MODNAS search + evaluation,” while also noting that “we conduct 19 separate search runs (one for each device).” This phrasing is ambiguous: if the total compute budget for all 19 independent baseline searches equals the budget for a single MODNAS run, each baseline receives roughly $1/19$ of the resources, which would structurally disadvantage them. If each baseline instead receives the full MODNAS budget, the total baseline compute is $19\times$ higher and the efficiency claim is misrepresented. The paper does not clarify which interpretation holds, making it impossible to assess whether the NAS-Bench-201 superiority stems from better optimization or from an unmatched protocol.
- **Overclaiming Pareto front coverage given linear scalarization.** The paper repeatedly claims to return “the full Pareto front” (Abstract, Section 1, Section 3.1) and states that MODNAS is effective at “optimizing beyond convex Pareto fronts” (Section 4.3). However, the method exclusively uses linear scalarization $r^\top L$ (Eq. 5), which in standard multi-objective optimization theory can only guarantee recovery of points on the convex hull of the Pareto front. While the hypernetwork and sampling introduce practical approximations, the paper does not provide theoretical or empirical evidence that non-convex regions are actually covered, nor does it acknowledge this well-known limitation in the main text. The claims should be tempered to “approximating the Pareto front” or the method extended to non-linear scalarizations.

### Minor

- **Large-scale empirical claims rely on unvalidated proxies.** For ImageNet-1k, GPT-2, and HAT, the reported Pareto fronts and hypervolumes are computed using supernetwork validation accuracy/loss and MetaPredictor estimates of hardware metrics (Sections 4.2–4.3). There is no standalone retraining of sampled architectures from scratch to verify that proxy-based rankings correlate with true standalone performance. While the paper acknowledges in the Limitations section that “the method relies on differentiable proxies for objectives, which may not always align with ground truth values,” the headline claims that MODNAS “outperforms existing MOO NAS methods … on ImageNet-1k” are therefore claims about supernet profiling quality rather than about finding better actual networks. At minimum, a correlation study or a small-scale standalone validation would strengthen these claims considerably.
- **Conflicting evaluation descriptions in Section 4.3.** The text first states that both MODNAS and baselines are evaluated “using the OFA pretrained supernetwork,” but immediately adds that baselines are “run for 192 hours using the OFA pretrained accuracy predictor.” It is unclear whether the baselines are evaluated with the predictor or the supernetwork, and whether the 192 hours refers to per-baseline or total time. This undermines reproducibility for the strongest empirical claims.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation comparing linear scalarization against non-linear variants (e.g., Tchebycheff or augmented scalarization) on the explicitly non-convex HW-GPT-Bench would clarify whether non-convex regions are missed and would directly justify the design choice.
- A visualization showing the convex hull of the true front versus the solutions found by MODNAS on HW-GPT-Bench would expose any non-convex coverage gaps.
- Quantification of how many reference architectures are needed for zero-shot device transfer and how transfer error degrades for out-of-distribution hardware.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.

- **Criticism of “zero-shot” transfer requiring reference architecture evaluations.** The paper explicitly states that device embeddings $d_t$ are “obtained by evaluating a fixed set of reference architectures on device $t$” (Section 3.2). In the hardware-aware NAS literature, “zero-shot” standardly means no retraining or fine-tuning of the search method is required for new devices; device profiling is expected and standard (e.g., Lee et al., 2021b). This criticism reflects a terminological disagreement, not an author error.
- **O(1) complexity “misrepresentation.”** The critic argues that because Algorithm 1 iterates over $T$ devices, the $\mathcal{O}(1)$ claim in Table 1 is false. However, Section 4.4 explicitly acknowledges that MODNAS “iterates over $T$ devices to compute $g_\Phi^*$ and $g_w^*$.” The $\mathcal{O}(1)$ notation is used to indicate that the method trains a *single* supernetwork/hypernetwork and avoids $T$ independent searches, not that per-epoch FLOPs are constant in $T$. This is a reasonable shorthand in the NAS efficiency literature, though the table could be more explicit.
- **Lower-level gradient averaging inconsistency.** The critic questions why the lower-level supernetwork update uses mean gradients while the upper-level uses MGD. This is justified: the supernetwork weights are shared across all devices and preference vectors, so averaging is natural, whereas the hypernetwork generates different architectures per device/objective, requiring Pareto-aware MGD. This is not a true inconsistency.
- **Missing appendix proofs and references.** Per instructions, these are parser artifacts; the original submission contains appendices.
- **Typos, formatting, and grammar issues.** These are parser artifacts, not author errors.

## Novel Insights

The paper makes a genuinely novel algorithmic contribution by framing multi-objective, multi-device NAS as a multi-task hypernetwork learning problem solved with MGD. The device-attention mechanism over a bank of hypernetworks is elegant, and the empirical breadth is commendable. However, the gap between the strong theoretical claims (“full Pareto front,” “beyond convex Pareto fronts”) and the structural limitation of linear scalarization is a missed opportunity for intellectual honesty. If the authors explicitly scoped their contribution as “efficient approximation of the Pareto front via amortized linear scalarization” rather than “full profiling,” the theoretical framing would align cleanly with the strong empirical results. Similarly, clarifying the baseline protocol on NAS-Bench-201 would resolve the most pressing methodological concern.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the NAS-Bench-201 baseline protocol.** Explicitly state whether the total baseline budget equals one MODNAS run (fair total compute) or whether each baseline receives the full MODNAS budget (fair per-device compute). If the former, rerun baselines with a matched per-device budget; if the latter, revise the efficiency claims accordingly.
2. **Temper Pareto front claims or extend the method.** Either revise “full Pareto front” to “Pareto front approximation” throughout, or incorporate a non-linear scalarization (e.g., Tchebycheff or augmented) into the hypernetwork framework to theoretically and empirically justify non-convex coverage.
3. **Add standalone validation for large-scale tasks.** Retrain even 3–5 architectures per front from scratch on ImageNet-1k or GPT-2 to validate proxy rankings, or cite existing correlation studies for the specific supernetworks used.
4. **Clarify Section 4.3 baseline evaluation.** Specify whether baselines use the OFA supernetwork or the accuracy predictor for evaluation, and clarify the 192-hour budget.
