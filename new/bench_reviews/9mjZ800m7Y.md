## Summary
This paper proposes MODNAS, a differentiable multi-objective NAS method that amortizes Pareto-front profiling across both user preferences and hardware devices through a preference- and device-conditioned MetaHypernetwork. The method combines scalarization over objectives with MGD across devices, and is evaluated across four search spaces and multiple tasks, with broad empirical evidence that it can generate strong trade-off architectures efficiently from a single trained system.

## Strengths
- **Addresses an important and practically meaningful problem.** The paper targets a real limitation of hardware-aware NAS: repeated search for each device/constraint pair. The proposed formulation directly aims to amortize this cost across devices and preference vectors, which is a meaningful systems contribution for deployment-oriented NAS.
- **The method is technically coherent and reasonably well motivated.** The core design—MetaHypernetwork conditioned on preference vector and device embedding, differentiable architecture sampling, supernet for expensive objectives, MetaPredictor for hardware metrics, and MGD across devices—fits together cleanly and is grounded in existing MOO/NAS methodology rather than being ad hoc.
- **Experimental scope is unusually broad for this area.** The paper evaluates on NAS-Bench-201, OFA/MobileNetV3, HAT, and HW-GPT-Bench; across image classification, machine translation, and language modeling; with up to 19 devices and up to 3 objectives. That breadth materially strengthens the empirical case that the approach is broadly useful rather than overfit to one benchmark.
- **Results appear strong and consistent across settings.** In the presented figures, MODNAS generally achieves better HV than a range of black-box baselines and constrained-search alternatives, often with many fewer final architecture evaluations. The NB201 study across 19 devices is especially compelling.
- **There are meaningful ablations, not just headline comparisons.** The MGD-vs-mean/sequential/MC comparison in Figure 6 is useful and directly supports a nontrivial algorithmic design choice. The comparison against a randomly initialized hypernetwork (RHPN) also helps establish that the learned conditional model matters.
- **The paper shows some evidence of scalability beyond 2 objectives.** The 3-objective NB201 experiment is a useful stress test and supports the claim that the framework extends naturally beyond simple latency/accuracy trade-offs.
- **Clarity is generally solid.** The paper is well organized, the problem setup is understandable, and the method is described in a way that makes the core contribution legible.

## Weaknesses

###: Fatal

None.

### Major:
- **The central comparative claim is somewhat overstated because the evaluation mainly demonstrates superiority in an amortized multi-device/multi-preference setting, not necessarily like-for-like superiority as an optimizer under matched assumptions.**  
  The paper repeatedly claims “single search run,” “without any additional costs,” and broad outperformance over prior MOO NAS methods. But in Section 4.1 it explicitly states: “For the baselines, we conduct 19 separate search runs (one for each device), whereas MODNAS leverages meta-learning … in a single search run.” This is a real advantage of the proposed formulation, but it also means the experiments conflate two things: (i) better amortization/meta-learning across devices and preferences, and (ii) better per-instance optimization. The paper convincingly shows the former; it is less definitive on the latter. This does not invalidate the paper, but it does mean the claims should be framed more carefully as amortized superiority rather than universal optimizer superiority.
- **The evidence for “Pareto front profiling” is weaker than the strongest wording suggests.**  
  At test time, the paper samples **24 equidistant preference vectors** and evaluates the resulting architectures. High HV over those points is good evidence that the method returns useful trade-off solutions, but it is not the same as showing close approximation to the full Pareto front or demonstrating strong front coverage. This matters especially because the method is built on **linear scalarization**, which the paper introduces in Section 2 without discussing its limitations for unsupported/concave regions of the front. The issue becomes more salient in Section 4.3, where the paper states that HW-GPT-Bench has a **“non-convex Pareto front”** but does not analyze why the scalarization-based generator still covers it adequately or what may be missed. The empirical results are promising, but the front-profiling claim is stronger than what is directly validated.
- **The zero-shot transfer claim to unseen devices is only partially validated in the main paper.**  
  The paper clearly claims “zero-shot transferability to new devices” and conditions the MetaHypernetwork on device embeddings to enable this. However, the main-paper evidence is limited mostly to held-out-device HV and qualitative embedding discussion; the stronger claim in Section 4.4 (“generalizes well on 17 test devices with only 2 search devices”) is deferred to the appendix. There is little analysis of transfer difficulty as device shift increases, nor comparison to simpler device-conditioning/interpolation alternatives. Since unseen-device transfer is a key selling point, the main paper should validate it more directly.

### Minor
- **Proxy quality is not analyzed enough given how central it is to the method.**  
  The search depends on pretrained, frozen MetaPredictors for hardware objectives and on the supernet for performance-related objectives. The paper acknowledges in the limitations that “the method relies on differentiable proxies for objectives, which may not always align with ground truth values,” but the main text gives little quantitative analysis of predictor error, unseen-device predictor quality, or how proxy inaccuracies affect final HV/front quality.
- **The evaluation does not cleanly separate search-time proxy quality from final architecture quality in all settings.**  
  This is most noticeable in the larger-space experiments. For OFA/ImageNet, the paper says it starts from the pretrained OFA supernetwork, **keeps the supernetwork frozen**, and evaluates generated architectures using the OFA pretrained supernetwork. That makes this closer to preference/device-conditioned decoding over an existing supernet than a full end-to-end NAS procedure, and the paper should be more explicit about that distinction. More generally, without from-scratch validation of discovered models in these large spaces, it is harder to know how much of the gain survives beyond the search proxies.
- **The asymmetry between using MGD for hypernetwork parameters and simple averaging for supernet weights is underexplained.**  
  Algorithm 1 uses MGD to aggregate device-specific gradients for the MetaHypernetwork, but uses a plain mean gradient for the shared supernet weights. This may be a sensible pragmatic choice, but the paper does not really justify why device conflict is important enough to require MGD for \(\Phi\) but not for \(w\).
- **The complexity discussion in Section 4.4 is directionally useful but rhetorically too coarse.**  
  Declaring MODNAS cost as \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) for search and Pareto-set build abstracts away dependence on the number of devices iterated over during training, MetaPredictor pretraining, device feature construction, and inherited supernet costs in some experiments. The asymptotic message is understandable, but the presentation risks overstating the practical computational story.
- **Some key design choices are not deeply ablated.**  
  The paper ablates gradient aggregation but not important aspects of the MetaHypernetwork such as bank size \(K\), embedding dimension, or alternative conditioning schemes. This does not break the paper, but it leaves uncertainty about how sensitive the method is to these architectural choices.

### Trivial
- **The paper could report uncertainty more consistently across all experiments.**  
  Figure 3 explicitly reports means over 3 runs, but similar clarity is not emphasized throughout all settings. More systematic variance reporting would strengthen confidence in the cross-benchmark gains.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Add direct NB201 analyses against the true Pareto front, e.g., dominated fraction, distance to true Pareto set/front, or coverage/recall metrics.
- Report predictor quality metrics (e.g., MAE/R²) and analyze how MetaPredictor error correlates with downstream front quality.
- Add a more explicit study of how preference vectors map into objective-space trade-offs, to verify that the learned conditional generator does not collapse or overpopulate only certain front regions.
- Provide a clearer total wall-clock/memory accounting that includes preparatory costs such as predictor pretraining and any inherited supernet assumptions.
- Expand the zero-shot transfer analysis to more heterogeneous device shifts in the main paper.
- Discuss alternatives to linear scalarization for better unsupported-front coverage.

## Novel Insights
The paper’s strongest contribution is best understood not as a new multi-objective optimizer in isolation, but as an **amortized conditional NAS system**: it trades stronger up-front structure (device embeddings, hypernetwork conditioning, scalarized training) for cheaper downstream Pareto set generation across many user/device contexts. That framing helps reconcile both the strengths and the limitations. It explains why the method wins so convincingly in evaluation-efficiency terms, but also why the most important open questions concern conditional coverage, proxy fidelity, and out-of-distribution hardware transfer rather than raw optimization mechanics alone.

## Suggestions
- **Reframe the headline claims more precisely.** Emphasize that MODNAS is strongest as an amortized multi-device, multi-preference NAS method, and avoid wording that implies unconditional superiority over all prior MOO NAS methods on a matched footing.
- **Strengthen front-quality validation on NB201.** Since true benchmark structure is available there, directly measure closeness/coverage relative to the true Pareto front rather than relying mainly on HV of 24 queried points.
- **Add quantitative analysis of MetaPredictor and proxy quality.** Report predictor accuracy and, if possible, show how proxy error affects discovered fronts.
- **Clarify evaluation semantics in OFA/HAT/GPT experiments.** Be explicit about when the method is performing full search versus conditional decoding/selection on top of pretrained/frozen supernets and predictors.
- **Expand the zero-shot transfer evidence in the main paper.** Include analyses across more heterogeneous device shifts and compare against simpler device-conditioning baselines.
- **Discuss scalarization limits explicitly.** Since the method relies on linear scalarization, the paper should explain what front regions may be missed and why the empirical results remain strong despite that limitation.
- **Provide fuller practical cost accounting.** A table with wall-clock, memory, and preparatory costs would make the efficiency claims more credible to practitioners.
