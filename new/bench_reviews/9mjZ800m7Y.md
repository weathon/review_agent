## Summary

MODNAS proposes a multi-objective differentiable NAS algorithm that trains a single MetaHypernetwork conditioned on user preference vectors and hardware device embeddings, enabling Pareto-front profiling across multiple devices in a single search run. The method combines linear scalarization with Multiple Gradient Descent (MGD) over devices and is evaluated on four search spaces (NAS-Bench-201, MobileNetV3/OFA, HAT, HW-GPT-Bench) spanning image classification, machine translation, and language modeling with up to 19 devices and 3 objectives.

---

## Strengths

- **Genuine and timely problem formulation.** Amortized Pareto-front generation across multiple devices and user preferences is a real bottleneck in hardware-aware NAS. The MetaHypernetwork conditioning on both preference vectors and device embeddings is a clean, natural solution.
- **Principled optimization design.** Using MGD across devices while scalarizing objectives within-device is coherent and better motivated than ad hoc multi-objective NAS heuristics. The ablation in Figure 6 directly validates the choice of MGD over mean/sequential gradient updates, showing clear empirical benefit.
- **Breadth of evaluation.** Four search spaces across CNN and Transformer architectures, three tasks, up to 19 heterogeneous devices, and extension to 3 objectives is a genuinely broad experimental story. Few hardware-aware NAS papers evaluate at this scale.
- **Positive zero-shot transfer results.** The MODNAS MetaHypernetwork consistently outperforms RHPN (random hypernetwork baseline) on NAS-Bench-201, confirming the device embedding and training contribute meaningfully. The t-SNE cluster analysis (Figure 12) provides qualitative support that device similarities are learned.
- **Practical efficiency.** The paper demonstrates that MODNAS with only 2 training devices generalizes to 17 test devices (Figure 25), supporting the meta-learning claim.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None.*

### Major

- **"Full Pareto front" / "global view" language is theoretically unjustified.** The optimization backbone is linear scalarization (Eq. 5), which provably can only recover *supported* (convex-hull) Pareto points; non-supported points on non-convex fronts are inaccessible by construction. Yet Section 1 claims this is "the first to provide a global view of the Pareto solutions," and the abstract speaks of "profiling the Pareto front." In Section 4.3, the paper explicitly notes the GPT-2 space has a "non-convex Pareto front between perplexity and hardware metric objectives" and presents MODNAS results there—but never reconciles the theoretical limitation with this empirical claim. The paper should either (a) reframe the contribution as approximating a subset of the Pareto front (which may still be practically useful), or (b) explain concretely why the hypernetwork parameterization partially circumvents the non-convexity issue empirically.

- **No differentiable multi-objective NAS baseline.** All baselines are black-box (RS, NSGA-II, CMA-ES, BO variants) plus the constrained single-objective method MetaD2A+HELP, and the ablation RHPN. There is no comparison against a differentiable MOO method adapted to NAS (e.g., PHN-style hypernetwork conditioned on preferences without device meta-learning). This means it is impossible to attribute the observed gains to (a) the MOO hypernetwork formulation, (b) differentiability/supernet efficiency, or (c) device meta-learning. The RHPN ablation only partially addresses this, as RHPN also lacks a trained search procedure.

- **RHPN ablation absent for OFA and GPT-2 experiments.** In the NAS-Bench-201 experiment the RHPN baseline shows a large gap vs. MODNAS, validating the hypernetwork learning. But for OFA (Sec. 4.3) and HW-GPT-Bench (Sec. 4.3), the supernetwork is frozen and only the MetaHypernetwork is trained—arguably the setting where the RHPN ablation matters most. Without it, we cannot distinguish the quality of the pretrained supernetwork from the contribution of the learned hypernetwork in these larger-scale experiments.

### Minor

- **O(1) complexity framing overstates the runtime benefit.** Table 1 lists MODNAS as O(1) for Search Cost, but Algorithm 1 explicitly iterates over all T devices per training step (lines 2–16). The paper itself acknowledges in Section 4.4: "Although MODNAS iterates over T devices to compute g_n* and g_m*..." The claim is defensible in the asymptotic sense that one training run covers all devices, but Table 1's presentation hides the O(T) per-iteration dependence. This should be clarified to avoid misrepresenting wall-clock costs vs. conceptual amortization.

- **Zero-shot transfer tested on same-distribution devices.** The NAS-Bench-201 test devices are 6 Samsung phones (same family as the 13 training phones). While Figure 4 shows an FPGA as a "test device," the bulk of validation is on similar-family hardware. A stronger demonstration of zero-shot generalization would include devices from genuinely different hardware categories (e.g., FPGA/ASIC/TPU when trained only on mobile CPUs), especially given that "zero-shot transferability to new devices" is listed as a headline contribution.

- **Hypervolume is the sole evaluation metric; diversity not separately measured.** The paper claims to yield "representative and diverse architectures" (Abstract), but only reports HV. HV is sensitive to the reference point choice and does not separately measure Pareto set spread or uniformity. A clustered Pareto set can achieve high HV. The reference point normalization details are relegated to appendices.

- **MetaPredictor error analysis absent from main text.** The predictor is frozen during search, so all objective values for hardware metrics are proxy estimates. No analysis of predictor accuracy or error propagation to Pareto dominance is provided in the main text; only a general caveat appears in Limitations. This is especially relevant for the OFA and HAT experiments where evaluation is entirely supernet/predictor-based.

### Trivial

- The parallelizability of the MetaD2A+HELP baseline (which runs 24 separate searches) is not acknowledged. Wall-clock comparisons would benefit from noting that baseline runs can be trivially parallelized, narrowing the practical efficiency gap.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation on the number of training devices (e.g., 2, 4, 8, 13 for NAS-Bench-201) would clarify how much data is needed for device generalization to work.
- Switching from linear to Chebyshev or augmented Chebyshev scalarization would allow recovery of non-convex Pareto points and would directly address the theoretical gap while being straightforward to implement in the existing framework.
- Reporting actual Pareto front plots (not just HV bars) for OFA and GPT-2 across methods would allow readers to assess whether MODNAS dominates the full front or only in selected regions.

## Removed Points

> *These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.*

**Harsh Critic – "Comparison conflates several advantages; no apples-to-apples per-device test":** The paper is transparent that MODNAS amortizes across devices while baselines run separate per-device searches. This is the intended design paradigm, not an unfair comparison—it is *precisely* the use-case being claimed. The RHPN and gradient-scheme ablations do isolate sub-components. Partially removed as overstated; the missing differentiable baseline concern is kept as a separate Major weakness.

**Harsh Critic – "Results are proxy-based and cannot establish true superiority":** Supernet + predictor evaluation is the community norm in one-shot NAS; requiring from-scratch training of all found architectures would be out of scope. The paper does evaluate BLEU for HAT (pretrained supernet is used, but BLEU scores reflect real translation quality). Removed as a scope-creep demand; the predictor error concern is kept as a Minor weakness.

**Harsh Critic – "Why average gradients for supernet weights w but use MGD for Φ?":** The asymmetry is explicitly noted (Algorithm 1, line 15 vs line 9). The paper's reasoning is that the hypernetwork Φ directly encodes cross-device conflicts while the supernet w provides a shared feature extractor—this design choice is defensible and standard in meta-learning. The harsh critic notes it "deserves justification," which is fair, but not a fatal concern. Removed as a minor stylistic comment rather than a substantive weakness.

**Neutral Reviewer – "Sensitivity to preference sampling / Dirichlet parameters":** The paper sets β₁=...=βₘ=1 for uniform sampling and notes users can change this. Sensitivity to this hyperparameter is a standard caveat in scalarization-based MOO and not specific to this work. Moved to Nice-to-Have.

**Human Finder – "Limited theoretical analysis of MGD convergence":** MGD's theoretical convergence properties are well established in the cited prior work (Désidéri 2012, Sener & Koltun 2018). Demanding re-derivation for the NAS setting is not standard in empirical NAS papers. Removed.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation is the architectural choice to apply MGD *across devices* (treating each device as a task) while applying linear scalarization *within device* across objectives—these are two distinct levels of the multi-task hierarchy, and their combination is non-trivial. The empirical finding that generalization to 17 test devices is achievable with only 2 training devices (Figure 25) is particularly striking and suggests strong inductive bias in the device embedding structure. The counterintuitive success on the "non-convex" GPT-2 Pareto front with a linear scalarization method raises an open question about whether the search landscape under one-shot weight sharing is empirically more convex than the true architecture-quality landscape, which would deserve investigation.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe "full Pareto front" language** throughout the abstract and introduction to "Pareto front approximation" or "profiling supported Pareto solutions"; optionally, add Chebyshev scalarization as an alternative that covers non-convex regions.
2. **Add RHPN baseline for OFA and GPT-2** (frozen-supernet settings) to isolate the learned hypernetwork contribution in those experiments.
3. **Add one differentiable MOO baseline** (e.g., PHN conditioned on preferences, without device conditioning) to confirm that device meta-learning, not just differentiability, drives the observed gains.
4. **Clarify O(1) in Table 1** by noting this is amortized (single training run) and does not eliminate per-iteration O(T) cost; add wall-clock vs. device-count plots for transparency.
5. **Evaluate zero-shot transfer on an out-of-distribution device category** (e.g., FPGA or ASIC if trained on mobile GPUs) to substantiate the zero-shot claim beyond same-family Samsung phones.
