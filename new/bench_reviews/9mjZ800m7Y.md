## Summary
The paper introduces MODNAS, a hardware-aware differentiable neural architecture search algorithm that profiles multi-objective Pareto fronts across multiple devices in a single search run. By conditioning a hypernetwork on device embeddings and preference vectors and optimizing it via Multiple Gradient Descent (MGD), MODNAS achieves zero-shot transfer to unseen devices. Extensive experiments across four search spaces (NAS-Bench-201, OFA, HAT, HW-GPT-Bench) and up to 19 devices demonstrate that the method outperforms black-box MOO baselines while maintaining significantly lower search costs.

## Strengths
- **Ambitious and thorough experimental validation**: The paper evaluates on four distinctly different search spaces (CNN cell, macro CNN, encoder-decoder Transformer, and decoder-only GPT) across three tasks and up to 19 hardware devices, demonstrating the method's broad applicability (Sections 4.1–4.3). This scope is substantially wider than is typical for NAS papers.
- **Efficiency gains from single-run Pareto profiling**: MODNAS reduces complexity from $\mathcal{O}(CT)$ (repeated searches for each device/constraint) to $\mathcal{O}(1)$ by jointly training a single conditioned hypernetwork. The anytime-efficiency plots (Figure 5) show that only 24 evaluations are needed to match or exceed baselines that require substantially more compute.
- **Effective use of MGD for multi-device gradient aggregation**: The ablation in Figure 6 empirically validates that MGD converges faster and achieves higher final hypervolume than mean, sequential, or MC-sampling baselines, supporting the design choice in Algorithm 1 (lines 7–9).
- **Clean architectural formulation**: The attention-weighted bank of sub-hypernetworks conditioned on both device embeddings and scalarization vectors (Figure 2) provides a structured and interpretable way to generalize across diverse hardware. The learned embeddings cluster semantically similar devices well (Appendix Figure 12).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None

### Major
- **Freezing the supernet in OFA and HW-GPT experiments undermines the "Differentiable NAS" positioning**: The paper claims to perform *differentiable NAS* across these spaces, but Section 4.3 explicitly states *"During the search, we only update the MetaHypernetwork weights and keep the pretrained supernetwork weights frozen."* For OFA and HW-GPT-Bench, the method is therefore not searching architecture weights from scratch but rather tuning a hypernetwork to interpolate within a pre-explored weight-sharing supernet. Baselines like NSGA-II, RS-BO, and qEHVI search the architecture space from scratch (relying on predictors or actual evaluations), so comparing a frozen-supernet hypernetwork tuner against full-space black-box optimizers conflates supernet quality with search quality. This limits the strength of the differentiable-NAS claim for half of the experimental matrix.
- **Applying MGD to device gradients computed with independently sampled preference vectors deviates from its theoretical basis**: MGD (Désidéri, 2012; Sener & Koltun, 2018) guarantees simultaneous Pareto improvement across *fixed* objectives. In Algorithm 1, line 3 samples a fresh preference vector $\mathbf{r} \sim \text{Dir}(\beta)$ per device per iteration, meaning the effective "objective" for each device rotates stochastically. While the empirical results in Figure 6 show MGD outperforms other aggregation schemes, the paper does not derive why or whether MGD's descent guarantee holds under dynamically rotating scalarizations, making the theoretical framing of MGD in Section 3.3 partially disconnected from the actual algorithm.

### Minor
- **Zero-shot device transfer assumes test devices lie within the convex hull of training device features**: The paper claims zero-shot transferability to unseen hardware, but this depends entirely on MetaHypernetwork interpolation between learned training-device embeddings. No experiment probes out-of-distribution device profiles (e.g., dramatically different architectures or extreme latency/energy characteristics), leaving open whether the zero-shot claim holds for truly novel hardware regimes.
- **Constraint-handling is a pragmatic gradient masking rule rather than a formally integrated optimization step**: Section 4.1 describes zeroing gradients for objectives that exceed a user-specified constraint (e.g., $p_{\theta}^m(\alpha_{\phi}, d_{\phi}^m) \leq c^m$). This is applied externally in lines 6 and 14 of Algorithm 1 rather than embedded in the Frank-Wolfe/Lagrangian formulation (Section 3.3, Eq. 3). While practically useful, it breaks the mathematical coherence of the MGD update and its convergence guarantees.
- **Reference point $\rho$ for hypervolume computation is not explicitly defined in the main text**: Hypervolume is sensitive to the choice of $\rho$. While the metric definition references $\rho$ in Section 4 and a normalization note appears in the Figure 8 caption ("$(1, 1, 1)$ reference point"), the specific $\rho$ values used for the 2-objective experiments across all 19 devices are not reported, which complicates cross-paper comparability of HV magnitudes.
- **Cost accounting does not fully separate meta-training time from zero-shot retrieval time**: Table 1 reports $\mathcal{O}(1)$ search and Pareto set build cost for MODNAS, but this abstracts away the non-trivial cost of pretraining the MetaPredictors (e.g., 3 GPU-hours for NB201, 6 days on 8 GPUs for HAT, etc.) and the MetaHypernetwork itself. While the paper matches search-time budgets with baselines, an end-to-end wall-clock comparison that accounts for the one-time meta-training investment would provide a more complete efficiency picture.

### Trivial
- **Figure 5 mixes zero-shot retrieval for MODNAS with iterative evaluation counts for baselines**: While the figure effectively communicates that few evaluations suffice, plotting a flat MODNAS line at "24 evaluations" alongside gradually climbing iterative baselines is visually slightly misleading about the nature of the cost trade-off; adding a secondary panel or annotation noting that the 24 evaluations are *post-hoc retrieval* rather than online search iterations would clarify the comparison.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Gradient cosine-similarity analysis across devices**: Reporting inter-device gradient cosine similarity during meta-training would reveal whether MGD's advantage over mean aggregation stems from true conflicts or complementary subgradients. The ablation in Figure 6 answers this empirically, but a gradient-conflict diagnostic would deepen the understanding.
- **MetaPredictor error propagation sensitivity analysis**: Since the hardware objectives rely on frozen predictors, a brief perturbation analysis showing how prediction errors in latency/energy affect the final architecture distribution and HV would strengthen confidence in the proxy-based gradients.
- **Extreme-end Pareto front coverage visualization**: With uniform Dirichlet sampling of preference vectors, the extremes of the simplex (very high accuracy / very low latency and vice versa) can be under-sampled. Plotting front coverage near these boundaries (e.g., for 24 points) would confirm the method profiles the full Pareto spectrum rather than just the middle.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.

1. **[Harsh Critic] "Anytime efficiency evaluation protocol is a category error" (Figure 5)** — *Removed*. The figure is explicitly captioned "For MODNAS we only do 24 full evaluations." The x-axis counts architecture evaluations performed to *construct* the Pareto front. MODNAS evaluates exactly 24 architectures from the hypernetwork, while baselines evaluate many more to converge. This is not a fabrication; it is a legitimate comparison of evaluation counts required to obtain a Pareto set of the same size/purpose. The harsh critic misreads the figure's framing — the 24 points are not zero-cost forward passes; they are 24 *evaluated* architectures. The concern about conflating meta-training cost vs. search cost is valid but is properly captured in the Minor points above, not as a "category error."

2. **[Harsh Critic] "Misapplication of MGD invalidates the optimization guarantee"** — *Weakened to Major*. The harsh critic frames this as a fatal flaw ("fractures the theoretical foundation"). However, MGD is used here as a practical gradient aggregation scheme, not as a rigorous theoretical proof. The empirical ablation (Figure 6) directly shows it works better than alternatives. The theoretical mismatch is worth flagging as a major concern about the over-interpretation of MGD, but it does not invalidate the results or core claims.

3. **[Harsh Critic] "Claim 'without any additional costs' is misleading"** — *Removed*. The abstract's phrase "without any additional costs" refers to generating architectures for *new preferences on already-encountered devices* or *new devices* — i.e., no *additional search* beyond the one meta-training run. This is standard terminology in zero-shot / meta-NAS literature and is not deceptive.

4. **[Harsh Critic] "Missing experiments to decouple meta-training cost"** — *Moved to Minor / Nice-to-Have*. The suggestion for total wall-clock comparison is reasonable but goes beyond the paper's stated scope (search-time comparison, not end-to-end lifetime cost). It is addressed as a nice-to-have rather than a structural flaw.

5. **[Harsh Critic] "Gradient conflict vs. alignment analysis needed"** — *Moved to Nice-to-Have*. The gradient conflict analysis is interesting but not required to validate the paper's core results. Figure 6 already provides strong empirical evidence that MGD is effective.

## Novel Insights
The paper's most distinctive contribution lies in reframing cross-device Pareto front profiling as a *single-hypernetwork meta-learning* problem rather than repeated single-device searches. The conditioning of the hypernetwork over both scalarization preferences and learned device embeddings allows the method to interpolate architectural distributions across a heterogeneous device fleet in one training run. While the MGD component is borrowed from existing MOO literature, its application as a per-device gradient aggregator within a hypernetwork architecture search loop is novel, and the empirical finding that it outperforms simpler aggregation schemes (mean, sequential, MC) across both CNN and Transformer NAS benchmarks provides concrete evidence that cross-device gradient conflicts are a practical concern the community should address.

## Suggestions
- **Clarify the frozen-supernet scope in Section 4.3**: Explicitly state in the heading or opening paragraph of Section 4.3 that the OFA and HW-GPT experiments use frozen supernetwork weights and position them as "efficient post-supernet exploration" rather than full differentiable NAS. This will prevent reviewers from conflating the results with from-scratch search.
- **Add a brief discussion on MGD with dynamic scalarizations in Section 3.3**: A short paragraph acknowledging that the independently sampled preference vectors mean MGD is applied to rotating effective objectives, with a note that convergence is not formally guaranteed but is empirically validated (cite Figure 6), would reconcile the theory-practice gap.
- **Report $\rho$ values for HV computation**: Add a table or appendix entry listing the reference point $\rho$ for each multi-objective experiment to enable reproducible cross-paper HV comparisons.
- **Include an end-to-end wall-clock efficiency analysis**: In the appendix, report total training hours (predictor pretraining + meta-training + 24 evaluations) and compare to baselines that received the same *per-device* search time. This would preempt scope-creep criticisms regarding total compute accounting.

## Score and Decision
I calibrated this paper against several anchors from the ICLR 2025 human-review corpus:

- **High anchors (avg score ≥ 6)**: *MosT* (Neb17mimVH, 6.17) is a comparable multi-objective optimization paper that uses MGDA + optimal transport for many-objective settings. It was accepted for having solid theoretical guarantees and broad empirical validation across federated learning, MTL, and LLM prompting. *Tchebycheff set scalarization* (O4N9kWwV6R, 7.00) was praised for comprehensive experiments with many objectives and theoretical guarantees. MODNAS has broader NAS-specific empirical coverage (4 search spaces, 19 devices) than MosT, but its theoretical contribution is weaker (MGD is adapted rather than developed, and the dynamic-preference mismatch noted above means convergence guarantees do not directly apply).
- **Medium anchors (avg score ~4–6)**: *DiffusionNAG* (dyG2oLJYyX, 5.75) and *LayerNAS* (2DldCIjAdX, 5.75) were borderline accept/reject papers with solid empirical results but incomplete ablations or limited theoretical framing. MODNAS clearly surpasses these in experimental rigor and evaluation breadth.
- **Low anchors (avg score ≤ 4)**: *u6Y0GdTEYp* (2.50) was rejected for misleading framing, unfair baselines (gradient vs. population methods without comparable compute), and negligible insights. MODNAS is substantially stronger — its baselines receive matched or greater compute budgets, and the experimental scope is well beyond the low anchors.

MODNAS sits between the medium and high anchors. It has exceptionally strong empirical evidence (four diverse search spaces, 19 devices, thorough ablations of gradient aggregation) comparable to or exceeding the high anchors, but the theoretical foundation for MGD is weaker than methods like MosT, and the frozen-supernet limitation for OFA/HW-GPT slightly undercuts the "differentiable NAS" framing. The concerns are substantive but not fatal — the empirical results robustly validate the method's utility even if the theoretical framing could be tighter. Compared to MosT (6.17), MODNAS trades slight theoretical weakness for much stronger empirical novelty in the NAS domain. This places it comfortably above medium and in the strong-accept range, but not at the very top due to the theoretical gaps.

I rate it at **7.0**, a strong accept, anchored above the medium-borderline papers (5–5.75) and slightly above MosT (6.17) in empirical strength, but below highly theoretical accept papers (7.0+) due to the MGD framing gap and frozen-supernet limitation.
