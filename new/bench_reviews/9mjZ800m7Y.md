## Summary

MODNAS proposes a multi-objective differentiable NAS method that profiles the entire Pareto front across multiple hardware devices in a single search run. It uses a MetaHypernetwork conditioned on both preference (scalarization) vectors and device embeddings, optimized via Multiple Gradient Descent (MGD), to generate Pareto-optimal architectures for arbitrary preference-device combinations. Experiments across 4 search spaces (NAS-Bench-201, MobileNetV3/OFA, HAT Transformer, HW-GPT-Bench), 3 tasks, up to 19 devices, and up to 3 objectives demonstrate strong hypervolume performance versus baselines.

## Strengths

- **Novel and coherent integration of ideas.** The combination of a MetaHypernetwork conditioned on both preference vectors and device embeddings with MGD optimization is a principled and non-trivial design. It addresses a real practical inefficiency: existing hardware-aware NAS methods require separate searches per device and per constraint level. The method cleanly enables zero-shot transfer to new devices via learned embeddings (Figure 12 confirms meaningful device similarity structure).

- **Comprehensive experimental evaluation.** The paper evaluates across 4 qualitatively different search spaces (cell-based CNNs, MobileNetV3, encoder-decoder Transformers, GPT-style decoders), 3 tasks (classification, translation, language modeling), up to 19 devices, and 3 objectives. This breadth is a substantial strength and goes well beyond typical NAS papers.

- **Strong results on tabular benchmark with known optimum.** On NAS-Bench-201 (where the global Pareto front is available), MODNAS consistently achieves near-optimal hypervolume across 19 devices (Figure 3), providing a concrete upper bound on quality.

- **Useful ablations.** The comparison of MGD vs. mean/sequential/MC gradient schemes (Figure 6) directly validates the MGD design choice. The extension to constraints (Figure 7) and 3 objectives (Figure 8) demonstrates flexibility.

- **Practical efficiency advantage.** The core amortization idea—training one MetaHypernetwork once and querying it for any device/preference—is genuinely useful. The reduction from multiple per-device searches to a single run, even with some setup costs, represents a real practical improvement.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **MetaPredictor accuracy and its impact on Pareto front quality is unanalyzed.** All hardware objectives (latency, energy) are optimized through frozen pretrained predictors, not ground truth. The paper provides no error metrics (RMSE, R², rank correlation) for these predictors—neither on training devices nor, more critically, on test devices where generalization matters. Since the optimization signal flows through these predictors, errors could distort the true Pareto front (e.g., finding architectures that look good to the predictor but are actually dominated). On NAS-Bench-201, where ground-truth latencies are available, the paper has an excellent opportunity to compare Pareto fronts computed with predicted vs. ground-truth objectives—this is not done. This gap undermines confidence that the reported hypervolumes reflect real multi-objective quality.

- **Evaluation protocol conflates amortization with algorithmic novelty.** MODNAS's key advantage is meta-learning: training one supernet+MetaHypernetwork across devices and reusing it. The baselines (NSGA-II, CMA-ES, qEHVI, RS, etc.) are per-device black-box methods that cannot amortize across devices. This means observed HV gains may largely reflect amortization (which any weight-sharing NAS approach would provide) rather than the specific MGD + scalarization-aware hypernetwork design. The most direct comparison—MetaD2A+HELP—is only on NB201, and even there, MODNAS requires a pre-trained supernet and predictor while baselines get per-device computation budgets. The paper would be substantially stronger with: (1) a like-for-like comparison against a strong amortized baseline (e.g., OFA+predictor run per preference without MetaHypernetwork), and (2) per-iteration or wall-clock HV curves for the larger search spaces to assess efficiency fairly.

- **The "O(1) search cost" claim is overclaimed in two respects.** First, the O(1) claim in Table 1 does not include the cost of training MetaPredictors (3 GPU hours for GCN latency predictor, 5 GPU hours for HAT predictors) or evaluating reference architectures on each new device to compute embeddings. Second, Algorithm 1 iterates over T devices per update step (lines 2 and 10), so per-iteration cost is O(T). The O(1) refers only to the number of search runs (one), which is useful but the framing is misleading—the amortized advantage is real, but it is not cost-free for new devices or O(1) in computational cost per iteration.

### Minor

- **Objectives may be highly correlated, reducing the meaningfulness of multi-objective optimization.** For the 3-objective experiments (accuracy, latency, energy), latency and energy consumption are typically strongly correlated across architectures (both driven by model complexity). The paper does not analyze objective correlations or demonstrate that the Pareto front is genuinely multi-dimensional rather than an effective accuracy-vs-complexity tradeoff. This does not invalidate the method, but it does affect the perceived contribution of the multi-objective formulation.

- **No retraining from scratch for larger benchmarks.** On OFA and HW-GPT-Bench, Pareto front quality is evaluated using supernet proxy accuracy (not from-scratch retraining). While standard practice in one-shot NAS, the supernet-to-reality gap is known to be significant and can distort Pareto front shapes. The HAT experiments do use fully evaluated architectures, but the larger-scale evaluations do not.

- **24 preference vectors may be sparse for M≥3 objectives.** With 3 objectives, 24 points on the 2-simplex provide very coarse Pareto front coverage. The paper does not study sensitivity to this parameter or visualize how well 24 points cover the 3D front.

- **The scalarization-based approach may miss concave regions of the Pareto front.** Linear scalarization is known to be unable to find solutions on concave portions of the Pareto front. The paper acknowledges (Section 2) that scalarization produces convex combinations but does not discuss whether this limitation affects the reported results, particularly on HW-GPT-Bench where the Pareto front is described as "non-convex" (Section 4.3).

### Trivial
- The notation is occasionally dense (e.g., the simultaneous use of L for loss and Λ for the Architect), though generally manageable.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Ablation on the number of training devices needed for zero-shot transfer (Figure 25 in the appendix shows results for 2 search devices on 17 test devices, but this is not discussed in the main text).
- Comparison against preference-conditioned hypernetwork methods (PHN, PMO) augmented with device conditioning, to isolate the contribution of the MGD + MetaHypernetwork design from amortization alone.
- HV vs. wall-clock-time curves for HAT, OFA, and HW-GPT-Bench experiments (not just vs. number of architecture evaluations as in Figure 5 for NB201).
- Statistical significance indicators (variance across the 3 independent runs mentioned for NB201) on other benchmarks.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:
- **"Not yet released / cannot be independently verified" concerns about HW-GPT-Bench or other cited tools** — The paper cites these as existing resources; per review rules, we treat them as real.
- **"Missing related work" claims (e.g., PSL, other Pareto set learning methods)** — Per rules, we do not flag missing related works without confirming their existence.
- **Pure formatting/style nitpicks** — Removed as trivial.
- **"MGD theoretical guarantees don't hold"** — The harsh critic's detailed structural critique (Weakness 4) overstates this: the paper does not formally claim convergence guarantees in the approximate NAS setting, and the MGD ablation (Figure 6) empirically validates the design. The concern about theoretical gaps is noted in Minor (scalarization missing concave regions), but is not a major methodological flaw.
- **"Baselines don't have supernets and this is unfair"** — This is partially true, but it is also partially addressed by the RHPN baseline and MetaD2A+HELP comparison. Moreover, some unfairness to baselines actually strengthens the paper's case since it shows MODNAS outperforms even with per-device computation advantages for baselines.

## Novel Insights

The most insightful observation across the reviews is that MODNAS's core contribution is best understood as a **meta-learning** contribution (learning a mapping from device embeddings + preferences to architectures) rather than a pure MOO algorithmic contribution. The MGD component provides a principled gradient aggregation scheme, but the practical gains flow primarily from amortizing search across devices and preferences via the conditional MetaHypernetwork. The paper would be stronger if it framed the contribution this way rather than claiming O(1) algorithmic superiority against per-device black-box methods.

## Suggestions

- Report MetaPredictor accuracy metrics (e.g., Kendall τ, MAE, R²) for both training and test devices, and on NB201, compare HV computed with predicted vs. ground-truth objectives.
- Add a like-for-like amortized baseline (e.g., OFA with a simple preference-conditioned architecture sampler without the MetaHypernetwork) to isolate the contribution of the MGD + device-conditioning design.
- Clarify the O(1) claim: reframe it as "one search run amortized across devices" and explicitly list the setup costs (predictor training, reference architecture evaluation) in a table.
- Analyze objective correlations (especially latency vs. energy) and discuss whether the multi-objective problem degenerates for the datasets used.
- Include HV vs. wall-clock curves for at least the HAT and OFA experiments.

## Score and Decision

Calibration against retrieved papers:
- **POMONAG** (Reject, scores 6/5/5/3/3): Similar multi-objective NAS topic, similar concerns about correlated objectives and predictor dependency. POMONAG had weaker novelty (extension of DiffusionNAG) and narrower evaluation. MODNAS is significantly stronger on both counts.
- **ParetoFlow** (Accept Poster, scores 6/6/6/6): A general MOO method with comparable concerns about predictor dependency. MODNAS has broader experiments but also has the evaluation fairness concern that ParetoFlow does not.
- **LayerNAS** (Reject, scores 5/5/8/5): NAS paper with good results but strong methodological assumptions and some comparison fairness issues. MODNAS is more novel but also has fairness concerns in evaluation.
- **SWAP-NAS** (Accept Spotlight, scores 8/8/8/6): Exceptionally strong NAS paper with clear, reproducible results across many benchmarks and minimal weaknesses.

MODNAS is clearly more substantial than POMONAG and LayerNAS (which were rejected) but has legitimate concerns about evaluation fairness and predictor validation that prevent it from reaching the level of ParetoFlow or SWAP-NAS. The unanalyzed MetaPredictor accuracy is a notable gap, and the amortization-vs-algorithmic-novelty conflation weakens the strength of the empirical claims. However, the method itself is novel, well-motivated, and extensively evaluated, and the practical efficiency contribution is real even if somewhat overclaimed.
