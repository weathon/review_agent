## Summary
The paper introduces Chain-of-Jailbreak (CoJ), an attack that decomposes malicious image generation queries into sequences of benign-looking sub-queries for iterative image editing, thereby bypassing safety filters. The authors construct CoJ-Bench (150 seed queries across 9 safety scenarios, expanded to 776 test sequences via 3 edit operations × 3 edit elements), demonstrate high jailbreak success rates (up to 62%) on GPT-4V, GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5, and Gemini 1.5 Pro, and propose a "Think-Twice Prompting" defense achieving 95%+ defense success.

## Strengths
- **Identifies a real and practically important vulnerability.** Multi-turn image editing is a widely deployed feature in commercial systems (GPT-4, Gemini), and the paper correctly identifies that current safefuards are poorly equipped to track cumulative harm across editing steps. The attack success rates (up to 62%) on production systems are concerning and relevant to practitioners.
- **Systematic benchmark design.** CoJ-Bench spans 9 safety categories with 3 edit operations × 3 edit elements, enabling fine-grained analysis (e.g., insert-then-delete outperforms other edit operations; word-level edits outperform char- and image-level). The analysis of increasing edit steps (Figure 5) showing monotonically increasing success rates is a useful empirical finding.
- **Comprehensive evaluation with both human and automatic annotation.** The paper uses 3 human annotators with majority voting alongside GPT-4-based automatic evaluation. Both evaluation methods show consistent trends (Table 3), lending credibility to the overall results.
- **Clear and well-structured presentation.** The threat model, edit operations, and evaluation protocol are described clearly. Examples (Figure 1, Figure 3) effectively illustrate the attack concept.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **The core attack idea has limited novelty relative to existing multi-turn decomposition attacks.** The paper frames CoJ as a "novel" method, but decomposing a harmful query into a sequence of benign sub-queries across multiple turns has been explored extensively in the LLM jailbreak literature (Crescendo, Chain of Attack, ActorAttack, MRCJ, etc.). The paper acknowledges Deng & Chen (2023) which already does decomposition of unethical intents into benign descriptions of individual elements. The Levenshtein-inspired framing (insert/delete/change operations) provides a structured taxonomy but does not constitute a new attack algorithm — the actual decompositions are generated by Mistral using 5 hand-crafted demonstrations (§3.2). The adaptation to image *editing* workflows is a reasonable domain contribution, but the framing as "novel" overstates the conceptual contribution.

- **Unfair comparison with single-turn-only baselines.** Table 4 compares CoJ (an inherently multi-turn attack) exclusively against single-turn prompt-based jailbreak methods (instruction ignore, refusal suppression, role play, etc.), reporting CoJ at 82–86% vs. baselines at 0–14%. No multi-turn jailbreak baseline is included, even though the paper cites related multi-turn methods (Yang et al., 2024; Deng & Chen, 2023). Without comparing to established multi-turn attacks adapted to image generation, the empirical superiority claim is tautological: multi-step attacks outperform single-step ones on systems designed to resist single queries. This is expected, not surprising.

- **Defense evaluation is inadequate for the stated claims.** Three interconnected issues undermine the "95%+ defense" claim: (1) The defense is evaluated on only **40 test cases** sampled from the 776-case benchmark — a 5% subset that is explicitly conditioned on being successful jailbreaks for all models, not representative of the full attack distribution. (2) The "Think-Twice Prompting" is appended as a *user prompt suffix*, but under the paper's own threat model (§2.1), the attacker controls all prompts. An adversary can simply omit or negate this instruction. The authors acknowledge they "do not have the access to GPT-4 and Gemini system prompts," but this means the defense evaluation does not match the threat model. (3) No analysis of over-refusal (false positive rate on benign queries) or adaptive adversarial evaluation is provided. Without these, the 97% defense success rate (Table 7) is not actionable.

- **Automatic evaluation has an entanglement problem.** GPT-4V serves as both a target model and the automatic toxicity evaluator for all models. OpenAI's safety policy defines what counts as "unsafe," which may differ from Google's policy, making cross-model JSR comparisons unreliable. For instance, a 62% JSR on GPT-4o vs. 28% on Gemini 1.5 Pro could reflect policy differences rather than robustness differences. While human evaluation partially mitigates this, the paper does not report per-instance agreement (κ, confusion matrix) between human and automatic evaluation, so it is unclear whether the "similar trend" claim holds at the instance level or only in aggregate.

### Minor:

- **CoJ-Bench is tightly coupled to current proprietary model policies.** Seeds are filtered to retain only queries refused by *all four models*, and decompositions are generated by Mistral and manually checked without reporting inter-annotator agreement or decomposition quality metrics. Changes to any model's safety policy would change what counts as a "hard" seed, limiting the benchmark's stability and generalizability.

- **The multi-step experiment (Figure 5) is small-scale and manually constructed.** Only 50 previously-failed cases are hand-extended to 3–5 steps. This provides suggestive but not conclusive evidence that attack success increases monotonically with edit steps. A systematic, automated method for generating longer chains would strengthen this claim.

- **The paper excludes Stable Diffusion and Midjourney** (footnote 4) claiming their safeguards are "too weak"; this limits the scope of conclusions to models with relatively strong safety alignment, and the reader cannot verify this claim.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Compare CoJ against adapted multi-turn baselines (e.g., Crescendo, Chain of Attack) in the image generation setting to establish whether the advantage comes from decomposition generally or from the specific editing-based approach.
- Evaluate the defense (Think-Twice Prompting) as a system prompt modification on an open model (e.g., Stable Diffusion with a safety filter) and test against an adaptive attacker who knows the defense.
- Report per-instance agreement between human and automatic evaluation (Cohen's κ or similar).
- Analyze over-refusal rates: what fraction of benign image generation requests are blocked by Think-Twice Prompting?

## Removed Points

While the core idea (decompose into steps) is intuitive, the systematic framework (edit operations × edit elements), the empirical characterization on production systems, and the domain-specific adaptation to multi-turn image editing constitute a legitimate security evaluation contribution, even if the novelty is incremental. - **"Over-reliance on text-in-image attacks":** The neutral reviewer noted that word/character-level edits primarily target text slogans. However, the paper does include image-level edits (19.3% of prompts) and reports results for all three elements in Table 6. The criticism that image-level editing is "less explored" is valid but mild — it is addressed, just not in depth.

## Novel Insights
The most interesting empirical finding is that insert-then-delete operations (adding and removing "not" or similar benign modifiers) consistently outperform delete-then-insert and change-then-change operations, especially on Gemini models. This suggests that safety filters may be more sensitive to the presence of explicitly harmful tokens in prompts than to the absense of negation words — a concrete vulnerability pattern. Also notable: Gemini models are substantially more robust (28–33% JSR) than GPT models (55–62%), but the paper does not analyze *why*; understanding this gap (architectural differences, safety training, context window handling) would significantly advance the field's understanding of multi-turn safety.

## Suggestions
- **Compare against at least one multi-turn baseline adapted to the image generation setting** (e.g., apply Crescendo or PAIR-style prompting in a multi-turn image editing context). This is the single most important change to strengthen the empirical contribution.
- **Evaluate Think-Twice Prompting on the full benchmark (all 776 cases)** and against adaptive attackers who can insert counter-prompts. Test on an open model where system prompts can be modified.
- **Report inter-annotator agreement** for human evaluation and agreement between human and automatic evaluation at the instance level.
- **Add analysis of why Gemini is more robust** (even speculative discussion of possible factors).
- **Report over-refusal/false positive rates** for the defense.
