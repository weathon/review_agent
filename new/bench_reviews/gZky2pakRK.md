## Summary

HAICOSYSTEM introduces a framework for holistically evaluating AI agent safety across the full ecosystem of human↔AI↔environment interactions. It simulates multi-turn interactions between LLM-driven human users (both benign and malicious) and AI agents equipped with domain-specific tools, using LLM-emulated environments. The paper proposes HAICOSYSTEM-EVAL, a seven-dimensional evaluation framework covering targeted, operational, content, societal, and legal risks alongside efficiency and goal completion. Across 132 scenarios spanning 7 domains and 12 models (~8K episodes), the paper reports that SOTA LLMs exhibit safety risks in 62% of cases, with risks compounding when tool use and malicious users coincide.

## Strengths

- **Holistic integration filling a clear gap**: Table 1 provides direct evidence that HAICOSYSTEM is the only framework simultaneously supporting multi-turn human→AI interaction, multi-turn AI→environment interaction, and both benign and malicious user intents. Prior frameworks each miss at least one dimension (ToolEmu lacks malicious users; Cresc lacks environment interaction; WildTeaming is single-turn with no environment).

- **Tool use + malicious users compound risks**: Figure 5 demonstrates this concretely—GPT-3.5-turbo risk ratio jumps from 0.52 (malicious, no tools) to 0.76 (malicious, with tools), and GPT-4-turbo from 0.42 to 0.54. This validates the paper's central claim that safety must be evaluated holistically.

- **Multi-dimensional evaluation taxonomy**: The seven dimensions (TARG, SYST, CONT, SOC, LEGAL, EFFICIENCY, GOAL) with scenario-specific checklists (§3, component 3) capture meaningfully different failure modes beyond binary safe/unsafe judgments. TARG achieving the highest risk ratios (0.46–0.66 in Figure 3) shows the checklists are surfacing scenario-relevant risks.

- **Cross-model safety profiling reveals model-specific vulnerabilities**: The finding that Llama3.1-405B outperforms smaller models with benign users but is worse in malicious no-tool scenarios (Figure 5: 0.63 risk ratio vs. 0.59 for Llama3.1-70B in malicious w/ tools) is novel and practically important for deployment decisions.

- **Modular, extensible platform**: The paper releases a code platform for creating custom scenarios, simulating interactions, and evaluating agents, lowering the barrier for future safety research.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The single-turn vs. multi-turn comparison (Figure 6, §5.3) does not cleanly isolate the effect of turn structure.** The paper claims the comparison "solely focuses on the influence of multi-turn interactions on the safety risks of AI agents" (§5.3, line 269). However, the multi-turn condition uses a GPT-4o simulated user that strategically builds up to harmful requests across turns, while the single-turn condition presents the original static jailbreak prompts. These differ not only in turn count but also in the nature and strategy of the attacking user. The higher risk in multi-turn could reflect that GPT-4o crafts more persuasive attacks than the static DAN/PAP prompts, not the multi-turn structure itself. While the broader point—that static benchmarks underestimate risks compared to dynamic interactions—is valid, the specific causal claim about multi-turn structure is confounded. A cleaner design would use the same simulated user in both conditions (truncated at turn 1 vs. allowed to continue).

- **The LLM-emulated tool execution means safety outcomes are partially determined by the emulator rather than real system behavior, and this is insufficiently validated.** When an AI agent calls `EpicFHIRGetPatientDetails`, the response is generated by GPT-4o, not an actual API (§2, §3). If the emulator declines to simulate returning sensitive data, a real safety risk is hidden; if it hallucinates returning data that a real system would refuse, a spurious risk is flagged. The paper cites Ruan et al. (2024) for the claim that "risks identified in emulated environments are also likely to manifest in the real world" (§2), but provides no direct validation of this in its own setup. While this follows the ToolEmu methodology, ToolEmu validated that 68.8% of identified failures would be valid real-world failures—HAICOSYSTEM's validation (100 episodes, 90% accuracy, 0.8 Pearson correlation) measures agreement between LLM and human evaluators on trajectories co-generated by GPT-4o, not whether the emulated environment faithfully represents real system behavior.

### Minor

- **The "62% safety risk" headline is not fully interpretable without severity distributions.** An episode is classified as risky if *any* of the six risk dimensions receives a negative score on a [-10, 0] scale (§4, line 112). With six dimensions, even a score of -1 on a single dimension counts as "risky." The paper reports per-dimension risk ratios (Figure 3) but does not report what fraction of "risky" episodes involve severe scores (e.g., ≤ -5) versus marginal ones (-1). This makes the headline figure difficult to interpret in terms of actual risk severity.

- **The claim of "grounded environments" in the introduction is overclaimed.** The introduction states HAICOSYSTEM is "the first framework to study AI safety issues through simulating interactions between agents and humans in grounded environments" (§1, line 62). The environments are LLM-emulated, not grounded in the sense of real API execution. While the paper acknowledges the emulation approach (§2-3), the introduction's use of "grounded" overstates the contribution relative to what the methodology delivers.

- **The Llama3.1-405B anomaly is under-analyzed.** The finding that Llama3.1-405B shows *higher* risk than smaller models in malicious no-tool scenarios (0.63 risk ratio, Figure 5) is potentially the most interesting empirical result—it directly challenges the safety-scaling narrative. The paper attributes it to "Llama3.1-405B's strong ability to use tools" (§5.3, line 261) in one sentence, but this explanation doesn't apply to the no-tool condition. This deserves deeper analysis.

- **The 100-episode validation of automated evaluation is limited.** Section 5.1 reports 90% accuracy and 0.8 Pearson correlation on 100 randomly sampled episodes, but does not report inter-annotator agreement among human evaluators or Cohen's kappa for the binary risky/safe classification. For a framework claiming to produce reliable safety measurements, this validation is thin.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Validate LLM-emulated environments against real tool execution for even a small subset of scenarios (e.g., 10–20 scenarios with actual API calls or rule-based implementations) to ground the emulation fidelity claim.
- A controlled single-turn vs. multi-turn comparison where the same simulated user runs both conditions, truncated after turn 1 vs. allowed to continue, to isolate the effect of turn structure from strategic user behavior.
- Report severity distributions of risk scores (e.g., fraction of episodes with scores ≤ -5 vs. -1) to make the 62% headline interpretable.
- Sensitivity analysis of the evaluation to scale choice, LLM evaluator choice, and risk threshold.

## Novel Insights

The paper reveals that model safety profiles are not monolithic but stage-specific: Llama3.1-405B's superior tool-use capability paradoxically makes it *less* safe than smaller models in certain malicious-user scenarios without tools, suggesting that capability improvements can create asymmetric vulnerability profiles. This challenges the common assumption that stronger models are uniformly safer and points to the need for deployment-specific safety profiling rather than one-size-fits-all safety ratings.

## Suggestions

- Run a controlled ablation where the same GPT-4o simulated user interacts with the agent, but the interaction is truncated at turn 1 in one condition and allowed to run full multi-turn in another. This would cleanly isolate the effect of turn structure and significantly strengthen the §5.3 finding.
- Report the severity distribution of risk scores (histogram or fraction of episodes at each score level) so readers can assess whether the 62% headline reflects severe safety failures or marginal threshold crossings.
