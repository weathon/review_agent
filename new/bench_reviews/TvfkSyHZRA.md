## Summary
This paper identifies a novel mechanism—Softmax Collapse (SC) from floating-point absorption errors and Naïve Loss Minimization (NLM)—that explains why grokking is delayed and why regularization is often required. The authors validate their hypotheses through two targeted interventions: **StableMax**, a numerically stable Softmax replacement that enables grokking without regularization, and **⊥Grad**, an optimizer that projects gradients orthogonal to the weight direction to eliminate the delay. The paper offers a unifying account of why existing methods (weight decay, MSE loss, representation choice) work for grokking.

## Strengths
- **Novel and empirically validated failure mode:** The identification of Softmax Collapse as a concrete, measurable condition (Def. 3, Eq. 2) that freezes learning in low-precision, high-overfitting regimes is a valuable contribution for practitioners training beyond 100% accuracy. Figure 2 demonstrates a clean precision-scaling effect—float16 collapses earliest (~20% test accuracy on 60% data), float32 later (~30%), and float64 latest (~80%)—strongly implicating SC as a causal bottleneck.
- **StableMax provides decisive validation of the SC hypothesis:** Replacing the exponential with $s(x)$ (Eq. 6) eliminates absorption errors and enables grokking on modular addition, product, and sparse parity **without any weight decay** (Fig. 4, left), directly in the 40% data regime where standard SCE achieves random performance. This is a clean intervention isolating FP errors as the barrier.
- **⊥Grad is an elegant hypothesis test:** The orthogonal projection (Eq. 12) surgically removes the logit-scaling component, and Fig. 6–7 shows it achieves rapid generalization without the prolonged overfitting phase. Figure 7's trajectory visualization (⊥SGD moves directly toward minimizing both train/test loss while SGD+WD detours) provides mechanistic insight.
- **Unified explanation for existing grokking methods:** Section 5.2 coherently explains why weight decay works (counteracts NLM), why MSE loss enables grokking on shallow networks (logit overshooting prevents indefinite scaling), and why representation dimensionality matters (compact binary inputs prevent easy overfitting). This is a useful synthesis.
- **Weight norm increase refutes prior assumptions:** Fig. 4 (middle) shows weight norms *increase* substantially during grokken with StCE and no regularization, countering the widely held view (Liu et al., 2023a; Varma et al., 2023) that decreasing weight norm is *necessary* for grokking.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **StableMax confounds numerical stability with loss geometry change, understated in framing.** The paper frames StableMax as a "numerically stable replacement to Softmax" (Abstract; Sec. 3.3), but Proposition 1 reveals it is actually Softmax *composed with* a logarithmic preprocessing $g(x)$, which replaces the exponential with piecewise linear growth for $x \geq 0$. This is not a numerical stabilization of the cross-entropy loss—it fundamentally alters the loss landscape's curvature, implicit regularization, and max-margin properties. Linear activations in the classifier head are known to induce SVM-like behavior that converges faster and resists logit runaway. Without an ablation isolating numerical precision from loss geometry (e.g., float64 standard CE or logit clipping at various thresholds), **Claim 3 that SC is the *primary* barrier to grokking is not fully disentangled from the introduction of a stronger, faster-converging geometric bias.** The paper should acknowledge that StableMax's success may stem from both mechanisms, and the lack of a clean separation weakens the explanatory account.
- **⊥Grad's success is not disentangled from established spherical/norm-constrained optimization.** Projecting the gradient orthogonal to $\theta$ (Eq. 12) constrains optimization to a spherical manifold—a well-studied technique in Riemannian optimization known to implicitly control weight norms and prevent magnitude explosion. The paper treats ⊥Grad as a novel intervention targeting NLM (Sec. 5.1) without acknowledging this equivalence to norm-regularization or comparing against explicit norm-constrained baselines. Until it is clear whether the fast generalization is unique to "removing NLM" versus a general property of spherical optimization, **Claim 4 that eliminating NLM eliminates the grokking delay is not fully substantiated.** The paper references related projection methods (Heo et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024; Kosson et al., 2024) in passing (Sec. 5.1) but doesn't engage with the mathematical literature on spherical optimization.
- **No causal mechanism for the delayed-to-sudden phase transition.** The paper explains *why training doesn't freeze* (mitigating SC/NLM) but not *why the model escapes the memorization basin* to suddenly generalize. The core phenomenon of grokking is the phase transition itself. Figure 7 shows ⊥SGD moves directly toward lower test loss, but this is a visualization of the outcome, not a mechanistic account of *how* the transition occurs. Neither StableMax nor ⊥Grad provides a dynamical systems analysis (e.g., Hessian spectrum evolution, basin escape probability, or gradient flow decomposition) showing how the model transitions from memorization to algorithmic learning. The explanation remains correlational: the interventions correlate with faster generalization, but the causal chain from NLM/SC removal to the sudden jump in test accuracy is not rigorously demonstrated.

### Minor
- **Sparse Parity and MNIST results lack depth.** The paper's core claims are validated most thoroughly on modular arithmetic (40%/60%/70% splits), while results on Sparse Parity and MNIST are presented with less experimental parity. Sparse Parity (Fig. 4, left) plateaus at ~60% test accuracy, and the MNIST subset experiment (Sec. 5.2, App. D.2) is discussed narratively rather than with equivalent ablation depth. This limits confidence in the generality of the NLM/SC explanation across task families.
- **⊥Grad algorithm underspecified for reproduction.** The mathematical definition (Eq. 11–12) is clear for vanilla SGD, but the paper claims to extend ⊥Grad to ⊥AdamW and ⊥SGD (Sec. 5.1, Fig. 6) without specifying whether the projection is applied per-layer or globally, or how it interacts with Adam's bias-corrected first/second moments and adaptive scaling. Projecting the raw gradient before adaptive scaling is mathematically distinct from projecting the adaptive step. This ambiguity affects reproducibility and fair comparison.

### Trivial
- **Figure 3 caption could better contextualize the comparison.** Fig. 3 compares $s(x)$ vs. $e^x$ over $[-4, 4]$, but the grokking-relevant regime involves logits that grow far beyond this range. A zoomed-out view showing behavior at larger logits would more directly illustrate why StabilMax prevents absorption errors at extreme values.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Logit distribution histograms over epochs (correct vs. incorrect class logits for standard CE, StableMax, and ⊥Grad) would visually confirm the SC hypothesis and the effect of the interventions.
- Ablation against logit clipping at various thresholds and float64 CE would help isolate the numerical vs. geometric contributions to StableMax's success.
- An analysis of gradient norm evolution around the SC threshold would complement the cosine similarity evidence in Fig. 5.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed or weakened, treat them with caution:

1. **~"StableMax fundamentally alters the loss geometry, invalidating the core claim."** — *Weakened but retained in Major tier.* The paper's Proposition 1 already shows StableMax = Softmax ∘ g, so the geometric change is acknowledged. The criticism is valid as an ablation gap, but it does not "invalidate" the claim—StableMax's empirical results stand regardless.
2. **~"The paper provides no causal mechanism for the delayed-to-sudden generalization transition."** — *Retained in Major tier* but acknowledged that the paper provides correlational evidence and visual trajectory comparison (Fig. 7) which is reasonable for this type of empirical study.
3. **~"⊥Grad's definition is implementation-ambiguous and conflated with established spherical optimization."** — *Retained in Major tier.* The paper does reference related projection methods, but the lack of comparison to norm-constrained baselines is a real gap.
4. **~"The 40%/60% split boundary regime amplifies SC impact but may not reflect the broader grokking literature."** — *Moved to Minor.* The 40% setting is standard in the grokking literature (Power et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2023), so using it is appropriate. The limited depth on other tasks is the real concern.
5. **~"Cosine similarity alignment is a known asymptotic property, not a discovery."** — *Removed.* The paper does acknowledge Lyu & Li (2020) and related work (Sec. 4.2): "The fact that the gradients converge to the direction of the weights has been studied in previous works..." The key contribution is linking this alignment to SC and grokking dynamics, not discovering the alignment itself.
6. **~"Sample-wise gradients vary, and optimizer momentum preserves small update signals—paper assumes simultaneous SC."** — *Removed.* The paper validates its claim in App. B.1 by showing that *artificially setting gradients from correct classes to zero* stops generalization, addressing the concern about heterogeneous gradients.
7. **~"Weight decay explanation is narrative, connection to D'Angelo et al. asserted without empirical linkage."** — *Removed.* The claim about D'Angelo et al. (2023) is framed as a broader connection to mixed-precision training issues, not as a core empirical claim. Section 5.2's explanation of the WD/SCE trade-off (Fig. 6c) is supported by data.

## Novel Insights
The paper's most novel contribution is the **diagnosis of Softmax Collapse as a numerical-optimization failure mode that masquerades as a regularization failure.** Prior work attributed grokking's dependence on weight decay to implicit bias toward simpler solutions; this paper shows that at least part of the phenomenon is an artifact of floating-point absorption errors freezing learning before the model discovers a generalizing algorithm. The insight that Naïve Loss Minimization—a gradient aligned with the weight direction that scales logits without changing predictions—drives the model toward this numerical cliff is a unifying lens that explains weight decay, MSE loss, and representation choice through a single mechanism. While the causal chain from NLM/SC removal to the sudden phase transition remains incompletely characterized, the two targeted interventions (StableMax and ⊥Grad) provide strong, clean empirical validation that the framing is on the right track.

## Suggestions
- **Frame StableMax's contribution more precisely:** Acknowledge that StableMax changes both numerical stability *and* loss geometry. Add an ablation using float64 standard CE and/or logit clipping to isolate the FP contribution.
- **Clarify ⊥Grad's relationship to spherical optimization:** Add a discussion in Sec. 5.1 acknowledging that gradient projection orthogonal to θ constrains the optimization manifold, and explicitly compare against a norm-constrained baseline (e.g., projected SGD with $\|\theta\|=R$).
- **Specify ⊥AdamW implementation:** Detail whether projection is per-layer or global and how it interacts with Adam's moment estimates.
- **Add logit distribution visualizations:** Histograms of correct vs. incorrect class logits over epochs for each intervention would strengthen the mechanistic narrative.
- **Include a progress measure for the phase transition:** Even a simple metric tracking the ratio of NLM-aligned vs. orthogonal gradient norms over time would help visualize the escape from memorization.
