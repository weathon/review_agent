## Summary

The paper introduces a formal-grammar-based experimental framework to disentangle "learning by rote" (memorization) from "learning with understanding" (generalization) in LLMs. By fine-tuning pretrained models (Pythia-1B, Llama3-8B, Mistral-NeMo-12B) on small datasets of strings generated by random and hierarchical grammars, the authors characterize overlapping memorization and generalization phases, show that the two modes are adversarial (memorizing new data erases prior rote memorization and vice versa), and demonstrate that dataset entropy inversely governs the ease of generalization versus rote memorization.

---

## Strengths

- **Clean experimental framework (Section 2):** Using probabilistic formal grammars provides three key properties that natural language lacks: guaranteed no pretraining contamination, exact control over entropy, and the ability to sample matched test sets from the same ground-truth distribution. This is the paper's most concrete methodological contribution, enabling a genuinely controlled laboratory for studying memorization dynamics.

- **Adversarial memorization/generalization finding (Figure 4):** The sequential memorization experiment is the paper's most striking result. Training a model on D_train,2 causes its loss on the previously memorized D_train,1 to rise from near zero back to the test loss level — with a brief re-generalization dip before the forgetting — shown consistently across all three model families and both grammar types.

- **Entropy experiments with two independent manipulation strategies (Figure 5):** Section 5 varies entropy via (i) alphabet size, (ii) oversampling a single token to match entropy levels, and (iii) skewing production rule probabilities in the hierarchical grammar. The consistent finding — lower entropy → better generalization, higher entropy → faster memorization — is replicated across nine subplot conditions, and the dual manipulation strategy partially controls for confounds.

- **Replication across models spanning >10× parameter count (Figures 2–5):** Every main finding holds for Pythia-1B, Llama3-8B, and Mistral-NeMo-12B with five random seeds each, giving the within-framework results reasonable robustness.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Missing connection to the grokking / double-descent literature:** The paper's core empirical structure — an early generalization phase followed by a diverging memorization phase, with overlapping transition — is the grokking phenomenon (Power et al., 2022) and is related to double-descent (Nakkiran et al., 2019). The paper presents the two-phase structure as a novel observation without engaging with this substantial and directly relevant body of work. Without placing the findings in the context of grokking, it is impossible to assess what is new: Are the dynamics the authors observe merely a re-instantiation of known grokking in a new LLM fine-tuning regime? Or do the findings go beyond what grokking predicts? The absence of this discussion is a significant gap that undermines the paper's originality claims.

- **The forgetting result (Section 4) is re-described without engaging with the catastrophic forgetting / continual learning literature:** The key finding in Section 4 — that training on D_train,2 causes the model to forget D_train,1 — is the phenomenon of catastrophic forgetting, documented since McCloskey & Cohen (1989) and extensively studied in the context of neural networks and, more recently, LLMs. The paper presents this as an "intriguing" and "surprising" result without citing or distinguishing itself from this body of work. The "re-generalization dip" before forgetting may be a novel observation, but without comparison to known continual learning dynamics it is not clear what is new versus expected. This gap also weakens the practical implication ("erasing cryptographic keys") since well-studied forgetting mitigation methods may already address or complicate this.

- **Critique of Carlini et al. and Tirumala et al. is a category mismatch:** Section 3.2 argues that Carlini et al.'s 50-token criterion "underestimates" memorization and Tirumala et al.'s single-token criterion "overestimates" it. However, those measures were calibrated for large-scale pretraining memorization (hundreds of billions of tokens), not for fine-tuning on n=8–64 synthetic out-of-distribution strings. The critique is not a logical refutation of their measures in their intended regime; it is a demonstration that the measures behave differently in a structurally different setting. The framing that the paper corrects "errors" in prior work is misleading. A more honest framing would be: "Prior measures were designed for a different setting; our framework reveals their limitations in a controlled fine-tuning context."

### Minor

- **No experiments on natural language:** The abstract and conclusion assert that the findings "apply to natural language data" and have "significant downstream implications." The limitation section acknowledges that synthetic data was used, but the stronger positive claim in the abstract is unsupported. At minimum, a single pilot experiment on natural language corpora (e.g., fine-tuning on a small set of domain-specific documents and checking whether train/test loss dynamics follow the predicted pattern) would test this claim. Without it, the paper is empirically scoped to synthetic data only.

- **The proposed memorization measure is not novel in concept:** The formula `memorization(M, D_train, D_test) = 1 - Loss(M, D_train)/Loss(M, D_test)` is, up to normalization, the training-test generalization gap — a foundational quantity in statistical learning theory. The conceptual insight (that you need a test set from the same distribution to estimate memorization) is valid and useful, but is presented with more fanfare than warranted. The paper does acknowledge the measure's practical shortcomings in Section 3.2, which is good, but the presentation around the "impossibility" claim overstates novelty.

- **The 5% divergence threshold (Section 3.1) is arbitrary and unvalidated:** All epoch-level claims about when memorization "starts" depend on `Loss(test)/Loss(train) > 1.05`. This threshold is unexplained and no sensitivity analysis is provided. Given that the paper's main claims about the relative timing of phases (overlap, sequence, etc.) depend on this criterion, checking whether conclusions are robust to reasonable variations (2%, 10%, 20%) would strengthen confidence in the phase characterization.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation is the sequential memorization experiment (Section 4): when a model transitions from memorizing D_train,1 to memorizing D_train,2, it passes through a brief re-generalization phase — test loss temporarily drops back to its minimum level before rising again as the new dataset is memorized. This trajectory suggests that the model's "understanding" of the distribution (its ability to generalize) is re-activated by the distribution shift before the new rote memorization overwrites it. This is not a standard prediction of catastrophic forgetting models, which typically expect monotonic forgetting rather than a temporary recovery. This observation, if confirmed in broader settings, would be worth dedicated follow-up.

## Suggestions

1. Engage seriously with the grokking literature (Power et al., 2022) and double-descent literature (Nakkiran et al., 2019) — position the paper's findings relative to what grokking theory predicts and identify whether there is a discrepancy.
2. Add a section discussing the catastrophic forgetting literature and explain why the re-generalization dip (Figure 4) is not predicted by standard forgetting dynamics.
3. Soften the abstract and introduction claims about natural language implications to reflect what the experiments actually demonstrate — controlled synthetic findings.
4. Include a sensitivity analysis over the 5% threshold in an appendix.
5. Reframe the Carlini/Tirumala critique as "these measures behave differently in a fine-tuning regime" rather than claiming prior work "erred."
