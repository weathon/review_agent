## Summary

The paper introduces Ambig-SWE, an underspecified variant of SWE-Bench Verified designed to evaluate how LLM agents handle missing information in software engineering tasks through interactive clarification. The authors decompose the problem into three research questions—detecting underspecificity, asking targeted questions, and leveraging interaction—and evaluate six models across these dimensions. Key findings include that interaction can recover up to 74% of performance lost to underspecification, models default to non-interactive behavior without prompting, and only Claude Sonnet 4 achieves notable detection accuracy (89%).

## Strengths

- **Well-structured problem decomposition.** Breaking evaluation into detection, questioning, and information leveraging (RQ1–RQ3) provides diagnostic granularity hidden by aggregate metrics. For example, the finding that Qwen 3 Coder achieves high information extraction (0.179 cosine distance) but fails to translate it into proportional performance gains due to rigid protocol-following is a concrete, actionable insight that would be invisible in a single-metric evaluation.

- **Realistic and challenging task environment.** Building on SWE-Bench Verified and integrating with OpenHands (a tool-equipped agentic framework) makes the evaluation setting substantially more realistic than toy code-generation benchmarks. The 500-instance evaluation with both proprietary and open-weight models (including controlled comparisons like Sonnet 3.5 vs. 4 for capability scaling, Sonnet 3.5 vs. Haiku 3.5 for parameter scaling) is thorough.

- **Insightful model-specific qualitative analysis.** The paper identifies distinct question-asking strategies (Claude's exploration-first approach vs. Qwen's ask-immediately approach vs. Deepseek's over-specific questions) with evidence from trajectories and quantitative metrics. The finding that Claude Sonnet achieves comparable information gain with 50% fewer questions than Qwen (§5.3) is valuable for agent designers.

- **Important practical finding about non-interactivity defaults.** The result that models almost never initiate clarification without explicit prompting (§4), and that prompt engineering alone cannot reliably induce detection across models (e.g., Qwen 3 Coder shows 100% FNR across all prompt conditions), has direct implications for deploying agents in underspecified real-world settings.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **Synthetic underspecification may not faithfully represent real-world underspecification patterns.** The underspecified issues are generated by GPT-4o summarizing fully specified SWE-Bench Verified issues. The authors' own distributional analysis (§2.1) shows that natural underspecified issues contain concrete technical details (code snippets, error messages, file/line references), reproducibility information, and conversational fragments—traits absent from their synthetic versions. The authors explain this omission by noting that naturally underspecified SWE-Bench issues "lack the paired ground truth necessary for causal measurement," which is a valid methodological concern for *causal* attribution but does not justify the complete absence of any evaluation on naturally underspecified issues. Even non-causal evaluation (measuring resolution rates and interaction patterns on natural issues) would provide crucial evidence about whether the observed model behaviors generalize beyond the specific distribution of GPT-4o-generated summaries. Without this, the central empirical claims about "how current state-of-the-art models handle missing information in complex software engineering tasks" are established only on an artificial distribution of aggressively stripped issues.

- **Detection evaluation (RQ2) conflates the decision to interact with the ability to detect missing information.** The experiment measures whether models choose to ask questions within the first three turns, treating this binary signal as evidence of underspecificity detection. However, "choosing to interact" reflects both a model's assessment that information is missing AND its instruction-following tendency to use the interaction channel. The paper itself shows that models like Llama 3.1 interact indiscriminately (46% FPR under Neutral prompting) while Qwen 3 never interacts (100% FNR)—these patterns reflect interaction policies and instruction-following capabilities rather than detection ability. The reported accuracy/FPR/FNR figures (e.g., "Claude Sonnet 4 achieves 89% accuracy") should be interpreted as measuring *interaction behavior under varying prompts*, not as clean measurements of detection capability. The paper partially acknowledges this (noting models are "sensitive to prompt framing") but the headline claims in §4 and the abstract overstate what the experiment actually measures.

- **Unequal turn budgets confound model comparisons.** Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder receive up to 100 interaction turns while all other models receive 30 (§3.1). This means some attributed "better interaction leveraging" may partially reflect more opportunities to brute-force solutions or ask more questions. The paper notes this design choice but does not provide an ablation at equal turn budgets, making it difficult to cleanly attribute performance differences to interaction capability versus computational budget.

### Minor:

- **GPT-4o serves as data generator, user proxy, and LLM-as-judge without cross-validation.** The underspecified issues, user responses, and question quality ratings (§5.1) all depend on GPT-4o. While cosine distance provides an alternative information gain metric, it uses OpenAI embeddings. No human validation of GPT-4o's judgments or user proxy adherence is reported. This is a standard concern for LLM-as-judge setups, and the paper's qualitative trajectory analysis partially mitigates it, but systematic validation (even on a small subset) would significantly strengthen the empirical contribution.

- **User proxy is more cooperative than realistic users.** GPT-4o responds with accurate information from the full specification and says "I don't have that information" only when details are genuinely absent. Real users may give partial, vague, or incorrect responses. The paper acknowledges this in limitations (§7) but does not bound how much performance degrades with noisier user behavior. This means the reported recovery rates (up to 74% improvement) should be treated as upper bounds under idealized interaction, not as estimates of real-world gains.

- **Claims about safety and resource wastage are not empirically substantiated.** The introduction and abstract frame the work as addressing "safety risks due to tool misuse" and "resource wastage" (Figure 1 caption), but no metrics or case studies specifically target safety failures or resource efficiency. The only efficiency metric—average action steps—is mentioned in passing (§3.2) and the paper itself concludes that "interaction yields no efficiency gains." The framing would benefit from alignment between what is claimed and what is measured.

### Trivial:

- The "multiple, interdependent gaps" framing in the introduction is not experimentally substantiated—no analysis quantifies the number or interdependence of missing pieces per instance.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation at matched turn budgets (30 turns for Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder) to isolate interaction capability from computational budget effects.
- A small human validation study of the synthetic underspecification (e.g., annotators rating whether removed information is critical) and of GPT-4o's question quality ratings.
- Evaluation on a subset of naturally underspecified SWE-Bench issues (even without paired ground truth) to assess generalization.
- A condition with a less cooperative user proxy (partial answers, occasional misdirections) to bound real-world interaction gains.
- Analysis of why interaction fails to close the gap to the Full setting—categorizing failure modes in the Interaction condition would strengthen actionable conclusions.

## Novel Insights

The decomposition of interactive underspecified task resolution into detection, questioning, and leveraging reveals a striking asymmetry: while current models can *extract* information moderately well when forced to ask questions (RQ3 scores around 4/5 from LLM-as-judge), they almost never *choose* to do so without explicit prompting (RQ2). This suggests that the bottleneck for deploying interactive agents is not question-asking quality per se, but rather metacognitive awareness of when information is missing—a capacity that prompt engineering alone cannot reliably induce, as evidenced by Qwen 3 Coder's complete non-responsiveness to interaction prompts. Additionally, the paper documents that improved coding ability does not straightforwardly translate to better interaction leverage: Claude Haiku 3.5 recovers a similar *relative* fraction of Full performance as Sonnet 3.5 despite much lower coding ability, suggesting that interaction integration may be a separable skill from base task competence.

## Suggestions

- Add a 30-turn ablation for Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder to enable fair comparison; even a subset of 50–100 instances would suffice.
- On a sample of 50 instances, have human annotators validate that the GPT-4o-generated underspecified versions actually require clarification (i.e., that an expert would struggle to solve the task from the summary alone) and that the user proxy responses are faithful to what a real issue reporter would know.
- Curtail claims about "safety risks" and "resource wastage" in the abstract and introduction to match what is actually measured (correctness of task resolution under interaction).
