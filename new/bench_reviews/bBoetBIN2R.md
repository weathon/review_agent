## Summary

NarrativeBridge proposes (1) a Causal-Temporal Narrative (CTN) benchmark dataset—generated by prompting an LLM to rewrite existing flat video captions as cause/effect pairs and filtering them via EMScore—and (2) a Cause-Effect Network (CEN) that uses separate CLIP-ViT encoders contrastively trained on the cause and effect halves of CTN captions, followed by a Transformer decoder for generation. The paper demonstrates large empirical gains (17.88/17.44 CIDEr over the closest baseline on MSVD-CTN/MSR-VTT-CTN) and cross-dataset generalization over standard video captioning baselines and fine-tuned VLMs.

---

## Strengths

- **Novel task and benchmark direction**: The observation that MSR-VTT/MSVD captions are flat, event-isolated descriptions lacking cross-event structure is accurate and well-motivated. Creating a benchmark that explicitly encodes narrative structure addresses a real gap.
- **Scalable pipeline with validation**: The LLM-based CTN generation pipeline with EMScore filtering and human evaluation (ICC = 0.87, 93% of captions rated ≥ 4/5) demonstrates the authors did not release raw LLM outputs unchecked—both automatic and human validation are included.
- **Substantial and consistent empirical gains**: CEN outperforms GIT (strongest traditional baseline) by +17.88/+17.44 CIDEr across both datasets, and significantly outperforms fine-tuned VideoLLaVA and ShareGPT4Video. The pattern is consistent across all three metrics.
- **Informative ablations**: Table 2 covers E_combined, w/o FT variants, Only E_cause/effect, and cross-dataset settings, making it possible to assess each design choice independently.
- **Qualitative results with failure modes visible**: Figure 6 shows CEN-generated captions alongside baselines, providing honest comparison.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None that fully invalidates the paper in isolation, but the two Major issues below jointly hollow out the central "causal understanding" claim.*

---

### Major

1. **Evaluation circularity undermines the core "causal understanding" claim** — The CTN captions are produced by an LLM that *never sees the video* (Prompt 1 explicitly says: *"you cannot directly access the video itself"*). Model performance is then measured with CIDEr, ROUGE-L, and SPICE against those same LLM-generated references. These metrics quantify n-gram and scene-graph overlap with the synthetic target, not causal correctness. A model that best learns to replicate LLM-style causal language patterns would top the leaderboard regardless of whether it genuinely understands causal video structure. EMScore only filters for video–caption relevance; it cannot verify whether the *asserted* cause→effect direction is grounded in the video. The paper's central conclusion—that CEN "understands and generates nuanced text descriptions with intricate causal-temporal narrative structures *present in videos*"—is therefore significantly stronger than the evidence supports.

2. **Benchmark causality is questionable and validated at insufficient scale** — The LLM prompt *requires* exactly one cause/effect pair from any video, regardless of whether genuine causal structure exists. Many videos in MSVD/MSR-VTT depict co-occurring or temporally sequential events without real causal linkage. The paper's own Figure 1 illustrates this risk: the CTN caption asserts "a group of guys started playing beer pong" as an *effect* of the car crash. While several original captions do mention beer pong co-occurring in the same clip (e.g., "a tumbler crashes on a dirt road and then a group of guys play beer pong"), this is sequential co-occurrence, not necessarily causation. The human study addresses this concern partially but evaluates only 100 out of 11,970 videos (margin of error 8.2% at 90% confidence), and rates caption quality (accuracy, temporal coherence, relevance)—not whether the *causal link itself* is actually observable in the video. At the scale of the full benchmark, unsupported or hallucinated causality could be systematically present.

---

### Minor

3. **Weak architectural justification for cause/effect separation given Figure 4(b)** — Both $E_{cause}$ and $E_{effect}$ receive the *entire* video without temporal segmentation. The paper's own Figure 4(b) shows near-complete overlap between cause and effect feature representations, which the authors interpret positively as "strong correlation aligning with the inherent structure of causal-temporal narratives." However, if the two encoders produce nearly identical representations, the claimed benefit of separate encoders capturing *distinct* causal and effect dynamics is not well-supported. Table 2 does show a real gain of ~8 CIDEr for the full CEN over $E_{combined}$, and ~6-7 CIDEr over single-branch variants—so the dual encoder provides empirical benefit—but the paper does not adequately explain *why* nearly-overlapping representations still benefit from dual encoders, nor does it rule out the simpler hypothesis that the gain comes from having twice the encoder capacity.

4. **Total loss equation (Eq. 9) inconsistently describes two-stage training** — Section 3.2.1 states Stage 1 is trained first and then *frozen* for Stage 2. Yet Eq. 9 writes $\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{cause} + \mathcal{L}_{effect} + \mathcal{L}_{caption}$ as if jointly optimized. As written, this is misleading. The training protocol should be stated unambiguously and the "total loss" framing dropped if the stages are sequential.

5. **Standard captioning metrics do not assess causal directionality** — CIDEr, ROUGE-L, and SPICE measure n-gram and scene-graph overlap with reference text. They do not penalize swapped cause/effect (e.g., "the beer pong game caused the car to flip"). Claiming these metrics measure "capturing causal relationships" (Section 4.1) overreads their capability. This is a persistent presentation problem throughout the paper.

### Trivial

- The Figure 1 discussion conflates sequential co-occurrence ("boys cheer on a friend" → same clip) with causal consequence, which weakens the motivating example; a clearer example of genuine video causality would strengthen the introduction.
- The limitations section (Section 5) lists "complex causal relationships" as a challenge but misses the more fundamental issue: that benchmark causality is inferred from text by an LLM, not grounded from video.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Human evaluation of causal accuracy by watching video**: Evaluators should watch the video and judge whether the CTN caption's asserted cause-effect relationship is actually observable—not just whether the caption reads well. This would transform the human study from a caption-quality check into a genuine benchmark-validity study.
- **VLM zero-shot prompting baseline for CTN style**: A VLM prompted with "describe the cause and effect in this video" at inference time (without fine-tuning on CTN) would clarify how much gain comes from the LLM-induced label distribution vs. the CEN architecture.
- **Quantitative analysis of cause/effect representation separation** (e.g., CKA or mutual information) to either support or challenge the dual-encoder claim more rigorously than visual UMAP inspection.
- **Evaluation on an externally validated causal benchmark** (e.g., NExT-QA) to test whether CTN training yields transferable causal reasoning beyond pattern matching on synthetic references.
- **Failure case analysis**: The qualitative section only shows successes; showing cases where CEN hallucinates causal connections would honestly bound its limitations.

## Novel Insights

The most important observation surfacing from the combined reviews is a structural tension inherent in the paper's paradigm: because the benchmark is generated by an LLM reasoning over text (not video), and the model is evaluated by matching that LLM's output style, the evaluation loop is partially closed. A model could score perfectly by learning to reproduce the LLM's causal language without ever internalizing video-grounded causality. This distinguishes the NarrativeBridge task from genuinely video-grounded causal benchmarks and suggests the paper's real contribution is closer to "narrative-style video captioning" than "causal video understanding." The nearly identical cause/effect feature representations (Fig 4(b)) independently reinforce this interpretation: the video itself may not contain the distinct visual signal needed to separate cause from effect at the representation level, which is why both encoders converge to the same point—and why the supervision comes entirely from the LLM-rewritten text partition, not from video-grounded event segmentation.

---

## Suggestions

1. **Re-scope the claims**: Replace "causal understanding" language with "narrative-structured captioning." The paper can still make strong claims—the benchmark and model are real contributions—but framing them as narrative style learning would be accurate and not require the circular causal validation loop.
2. **Extend human evaluation to causal validity from video**: Have annotators watch the actual video and rate whether the CTN caption's cause→effect direction is justifiable from visual evidence alone, separate from whether the caption is fluent.
3. **Address the Eq. 9 inconsistency**: Either describe two separate loss functions for the two stages, or clarify that Eq. 9 is a notational convenience and describe the actual optimization.
4. **Explain the architectural benefit under feature overlap**: Either provide a mechanistic explanation for why nearly-identical representations still benefit from separate encoders (e.g., independent gradient pathways enforce different text-alignment objectives), or revise the architectural narrative accordingly.
