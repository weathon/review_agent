## Summary
The paper proposes NarrativeBridge, a framework for video captioning centered on “causal-temporal narrative.” It introduces (i) CTN, a new set of captions for MSVD and MSR‑VTT generated by an LLM from existing captions and filtered with EMScore, and (ii) CEN, a two‑stage architecture with separate “cause” and “effect” visual encoders whose features are fused to generate a single narrative caption per video. Experiments show CEN substantially outperforms several prior captioning models and fine‑tuned VLMs on these CTN labels under standard captioning metrics.

## Strengths
- **Well-motivated problem formulation.** The paper clearly articulates that standard MSVD/MSR‑VTT captions describe isolated events rather than coherent narratives with explicit cause–effect and temporal linking (Fig. 1 and Sec. 1), and argues convincingly that this limits narrative-style video captioning.
- **Concrete, scalable CTN generation pipeline.** The CTN captions are produced via a carefully designed few-shot prompt (Prompt 1) to an LLM (Mixtral) that outputs short, structured “Cause”/“Effect” statements, followed by EMScore‑based video–caption relevance filtering and iterative regeneration (Fig. 2, Sec. 3.1). Human evaluation (Sec. 3.1, 4.3.1) with 5 experts, high mean scores (4.8/5) and ICC=0.87 suggests that, as text, CTN captions are generally coherent, relevant and temporally consistent.
- **Clear, reproducible modeling description.** The CEN architecture is carefully specified: Stage 1 uses two CLIP‑ViT encoders and CLIP text encoders with contrastive losses for cause/effect captions; Stage 2 uses Uni‑VL encoders/decoder with frozen Stage 1 encoders to generate combined CTN captions (Sec. 3.2, Fig. 3). Training details and loss formulations are explicit.
- **Strong empirical performance on CTN labels.** On both MSVD‑CTN and MSRVTT‑CTN, CEN substantially outperforms SEM‑POS, AKGNN, GIT and fine‑tuned VideoLLaVA and ShareGPT4Video under ROUGE‑L, CIDEr and SPICE (Table 1), with large margins (e.g., +17.88 and +17.44 CIDEr vs GIT). Ablations (Table 2) show that naive variants (single encoder, no fine‑tuning, or only cause/effect encoder) are systematically worse, indicating thoughtful engineering rather than a trivial tweak.
- **Reasonable scope and limitations.** The paper explicitly acknowledges remaining challenges with “complex convoluted causal relationships” and the lack of explicit spatial reasoning or multi‑agent modeling (Sec. 5), which is aligned with what is actually implemented.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None rise to the level of “this is not a paper” or obvious scientific invalidity. However, there are structural issues that, in my judgment, make the core causal‑narrative claims insufficiently supported for acceptance.

### Major

- **1. Evaluation does not directly measure causal‑temporal understanding.**  
  All main quantitative results (Table 1, Table 2) use standard captioning metrics (CIDEr, ROUGE‑L, SPICE; Sec. 4.1) against the CTN captions. These metrics measure lexical/semantic overlap with references; they are not designed to test whether:
  - a model’s text differentiates cause vs effect,
  - the asserted causal link is logically valid, or
  - temporal orderings of events are correct.  
  The paper repeatedly interprets higher CIDEr/ROUGE/SPICE as evidence that “CEN’s effectiveness in capturing causal narratives and temporal dynamics” (Sec. 4.3.1) and “setting a new SOTA in causal‑temporal narrative learning” (Sec. 1). What is actually shown is that CEN matches its LLM‑generated CTN captions better than baselines under generic similarity metrics. There is no:
  - metric that separately evaluates the “Cause” and “Effect” portions,
  - test of event ordering, or
  - human study comparing different models’ causal correctness on the same videos.  
  Given that “causal‑temporal narrative” is the core claim, this mismatch between the stated goal and the evaluation protocol is a structural weakness; substantial new evaluation work would be needed to truly support the headline claims.

- **2. CTN labels reflect LLM‑inferred narrative, not directly video‑grounded causality.**  
  CTN is created by feeding *only* the original human captions (no video) into Mixtral, which is instructed to produce a single Cause/Effect pair (Prompt 1). The LLM:
  - never sees the video,
  - infers plausible causal relations from textual descriptions and world knowledge,
  - is constrained not to add new *events*, but can still impose a causal link between events originally just co‑mentioned or sequential.  
  EMScore then filters for caption–video relevance, but EMScore is a *semantic alignment* metric; it checks whether the described events appear in the video, not whether the causal link asserted by the LLM is valid in the visual evidence. The human evaluation (Sec. 3.1, 4.3.1) corroborates that CTN captions are accurate, temporally coherent, and relevant as text, but it does not distinguish “video‑grounded causality” from “plausible causal explanation imposed by the LLM.”  
  For example, the motivating case in Fig. 1 links reckless driving, a crash, and then a group playing beer pong as cause and effect; the video might simply show these in sequence without an actual causal connection from crash → beer pong. This illustrates that the dataset is best characterized as *LLM‑inferred narrative structure consistent with the video*, not as human‑validated causal annotation. Training CEN to reproduce those captions and evaluating via CIDEr/SPICE primarily measures how well CEN approximates the LLM’s narrative style, not whether it models real‑world causality.

- **3. “Cause” and “Effect” encoders are not temporally or structurally grounded.**  
  CEN claims to “capture cause and effect dynamics using dedicated encoders” (Sec. 1, 3.2), but by design:
  - Both \(E_{cause}\) and \(E_{effect}\) take “raw video frames from the entire video without requiring explicit segmentation of cause-effect regions” (Sec. 3.2).
  - In Stage 1, the *same video* is paired with cause text for \(E_{cause}\) and effect text for \(E_{effect}\), each trained contrastively. There is no temporal constraint encouraging \(E_{cause}\) to focus on earlier, initiating frames or \(E_{effect}\) on later, outcome frames.
  - The UMAP in Fig. 4(b) shows the cause and effect embeddings having “near‑complete overlap” in feature space, which is strong evidence that the two encoders learn very similar global representations of the same video.  
  Thus, the model does not actually separate video into cause and effect segments or explicitly encode temporal transitions; it learns two slightly different projections of the same global embedding, conditioned on different text distributions. The large performance gains in Tables 1 and 2 are therefore more parsimoniously explained by *task‑specific fine‑tuning and additional capacity* rather than true causal decomposition. This undermines the interpretive claim that CEN “explicitly models cause-effect relationships and temporal dynamics” (Conclusion).

- **4. Interpretation of baselines and gains is not fully convincing.**  
  While the set of baselines is reasonably broad, the fairness and adaptation are under‑specified:
  - It is stated that “for the SOTA models, we follow the hyperparameter settings specified in their respective methods for training on the MSR‑VTT and MSVD datasets” (Sec. 4.2), but *how* SEM‑POS, AKGNN, GIT are adapted to the single‑caption CTN setting, and to the specific cause+effect style, is not detailed.
  - For VLMs (VideoLLaVA, ShareGPT4Video), the fine‑tuning regimes are summarized only briefly (LoRA vs simple fine-tuning), with no ablation on prompt design, output length control, or decoding settings. Given VLM sensitivity to prompt/format, poor VLM scores could stem from mismatched training objectives and output form rather than inability to learn causal narrative.
  - No variance or significance tests are reported; given that only a single CTN caption per video is used, metric variance can be higher than in multi‑reference settings.  
  The empirical evidence does show that CEN is a strong model on this label space, but the attribution of the gains specifically to “better causal‑temporal narrative understanding” remains not fully justified.

- **5. Some internal evidence contradicts the claimed benefit of dual encoders.**  
  The ablation in Table 2 is informative but also raises questions:
  - “Only \(E_{cause}\)” and “Only \(E_{effect}\)” are very close to the full CEN (e.g., on MSVD‑CTN: 56.42 vs 63.51 CIDEr; on MSRVTT‑CTN: 47.10 vs 49.87), suggesting that combining both is beneficial but not essential.
  - \(E_{combined}\), a single encoder fine‑tuned on combined cause+effect captions, is not hugely behind CEN (e.g., 55.72 vs 63.51 CIDEr on MSVD‑CTN).  
  This pattern plus Fig. 4(b)’s overlapping embeddings reinforce that the main effect of Stage 1 is fine‑tuning CLIP to the CTN captions, rather than learning genuinely distinct cause/effect dynamics. The narrative in Sec. 4.3.2 somewhat overstates the causal interpretability of the architecture given these results.

### Minor

- **UMAP interpretation is misleading.**  
  Fig. 4(a) shows CTN vs original caption features forming different clusters; this mainly reflects that CLIP was fine‑tuned on different text distributions, not that CTN embeddings are “more causal.” Fig. 4(b) shows cause and effect features almost overlapping, yet the text claims this supports the architecture. Intuitively, if distinct encoders are meant to capture complementary roles, substantial overlap suggests they are not very distinct. This does not invalidate the model, but it weakens the causal‑interpretation story.
- **Scope of “narrative” is narrow.**  
  CTN captions are constrained to a single Cause and a single Effect with at most 15 words each. This is a reasonable design choice for a first step, but it should be more explicitly acknowledged that this only captures simple dyadic cause–effect pairs; richer multi‑step or multi‑agent narratives are out of scope.
- **Cross-dataset generalization is modest.**  
  Table 2’s Zero Shot X results show sizeable drops (e.g., CIDEr 63.51 → 39.65 on MSVD‑CTN), indicating that generalization across CTN variants is limited. The text states these are “better than or comparable with SOTA and VLMs” but does not emphasize that performance roughly halves in some cases.

### Trivial
- Minor typos (e.g., “onvolutod” in Sec. 5) and some duplicated figure captions are present due to extraction, but these are easily fixable and do not affect substance.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A small human-authored CTN subset (e.g., 200–500 videos) where experts write causal‑temporal narratives directly from video, used solely as an evaluation set, would significantly strengthen claims about real causal grounding.
- Additional analysis of how EMScore thresholding (θ=0.2 vs higher values) changes CTN quality and downstream model behavior.
- Visualizations of temporal or spatial attention from the cause vs effect pathways, to empirically check whether they attend to different parts of the video.

## Novel Insights
The most important insight from synthesizing the reviews and the text is that this paper occupies an intermediate space between “video captioning with richer stylistic labels” and “true causal‑temporal reasoning.” On the positive side, it offers a practical, scalable method to reshape caption datasets into short causal narratives and builds a tailored architecture that performs very well on these labels. On the negative side, because the causal structure is injected by a text‑only LLM and evaluation uses generic similarity metrics, the work primarily demonstrates strong modeling of an LLM‑defined narrative style rather than demonstrable visual causality. Recognizing this distinction clarifies both the value of the work—as a useful benchmark and model for narrative‑style captions—and its limitations regarding claims about causal reasoning.

## Suggestions

- **Clarify and soften causal claims.**  
  Throughout the paper, rephrase claims to better match the evidence: instead of “explicitly models cause-effect relationships and temporal dynamics” or “significantly outperforms SOTA in causal‑temporal narrative learning,” emphasize that CEN “better matches CTN narrative captions” and that CTN encodes *LLM‑inferred* causal‑temporal narratives consistent with video content.
- **Add at least one causal-focused evaluation.**  
  Even a modest addition could help: for a subset of videos, have humans (or a carefully prompted LLM‑judge) compare CEN vs a strong baseline on *causal correctness* given the video. This would move the work closer to substantiating its central claim.
- **Analyze CTN generation failure modes.**  
  Include examples where CTN captions introduce questionable or incorrect causal links, and quantify their frequency. This would give a more honest and informative picture of the benchmark’s strengths and limitations.
- **Probe cause vs effect encoders.**  
  Use attention maps or gradient‑based saliency to show whether \(E_{cause}\) and \(E_{effect}\) focus on different temporal regions or object states. If they do, highlight that; if they don’t, reconsider how you describe what the architecture learns.
- **Strengthen baseline discussion.**  
  Add more detail on how SEM‑POS, AKGNN, and GIT are adapted to CTN, and include at least one strong single‑encoder baseline with the same Uni‑VL decoder, so the added value of dual encoders is clearer.
- **Acknowledge the “LLM prior” nature of CTN more directly.**  
  Make explicit that CTN is an LLM‑derived narrative augmentation of existing captions, and that while human evaluation supports its quality, it is not a replacement for ground‑truth causal annotation.

On the requested axes:
- **Originality:** Moderate. The dataset framing and two‑branch architecture are interesting, though technically incremental.
- **Importance of question:** High. Moving from flat descriptions to narrative, causal video understanding is a valuable direction.
- **Support for claims:** Weak to moderate. Strong for “better on CTN metrics,” weak for “better causal‑temporal understanding.”
- **Soundness of experiments:** Sound within the chosen metrics, but incomplete with respect to the causal claims and somewhat under‑analyzed baselines.
- **Clarity of writing:** Good; method and experiments are clearly explained.
- **Value to community:** Mixed. As a dataset/model package for narrative‑style captions, it has potential; as a benchmark of causal reasoning, it is not yet convincing.
