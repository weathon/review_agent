## Summary

NarrativeBridge introduces a framework for causal-temporal narrative video captioning comprising two components: (1) a Causal-Temporal Narrative (CTN) captions benchmark dataset, generated by prompting Mixtral to rewrite existing video captions into cause-effect pairs and filtered via EMScore, and (2) a Cause-Effect Network (CEN) with separate CLIP-ViT encoders for cause and effect video representations, trained with contrastive alignment and a sequence-to-sequence caption generator. The authors report significant improvements over SOTA methods and VLMs (17.88 CIDEr over GIT on MSVD-CTN) and demonstrate cross-dataset generalization.

## Strengths

- **Identifies a real gap**: Existing video captioning benchmarks lack structured causal annotations, and the paper's motivation for adding cause-effect structure to captions is well-taken and addresses a meaningful research direction (Section 1).
- **Complete system with significant quantitative gains**: CEN achieves large improvements over baselines on both MSVD-CTN and MSRVTT-CTN across all three metrics (Table 1: +17.88 CIDEr over GIT on MSVD-CTN, +17.44 on MSR-VTT-CTN), and cross-dataset evaluation ("Zero Shot X" and "Fine-tune X" in Table 2) demonstrates generalization capability.
- **Human evaluation of dataset quality**: A study with 5 domain experts evaluating 100 videos on 3 criteria (causal accuracy, temporal coherence, relevance) yields a mean score of 4.8/5 with ICC=0.87, providing evidence that the generated captions are perceived as high-quality (Section 4.3.1).
- **Transparent prompt design**: The LLM prompt for generating CTN captions is provided in full (Prompt 1), enabling reproducibility of the data generation pipeline.
- **Ablation study provides some architectural insight**: The comparison between E_combined, w/o FT variants, and single-encoder baselines (Table 2) helps decompose the contribution of different components.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Circular evaluation pipeline undermines core claims**: CTN captions are LLM-generated rewrites of original captions, serving as both training targets and evaluation references. Models are evaluated by measuring n-gram overlap (CIDEr, ROUGE-L, SPICE) against these synthetic references. This does not establish that CEN understands causal-temporal narrative — it establishes that CEN is better at reproducing the specific text transformations Mixtral applied to the original captions. The generating LLM had no access to the video content (only original text captions), so there is no assurance the targets themselves are causally grounded in the video rather than in textual inference. The human evaluation (Section 4.3.1) validates *dataset quality*, not *model outputs*, leaving the central claim about CEN's capability unverified against external ground truth. This circularity is a structural methodological limitation that applies to all baselines equally but fundamentally undermines claims about causal-temporal understanding.

- **Evaluation metrics do not measure what is claimed**: The paper's core motivation is that existing benchmarks fail to capture causal-temporal narrative (Section 1), yet the proposed evaluation uses CIDEr, ROUGE-L, and SPICE — standard text similarity metrics. The paper claims SPICE "effectively assess[es] the ability to capture causal relationships and event sequences" (Section 4.1), but SPICE measures scene graph overlap, not causal correctness. A model could achieve high CIDEr/SPICE by producing fluent text that shares phrasing with the LLM reference without capturing genuine cause-effect dynamics. A causal-specific evaluation (e.g., probing whether cause and effect can be correctly attributed, or human evaluation of model outputs on causal accuracy) would be needed to support the paper's claims.

- **Dual-encoder design has questionable motivation given near-complete feature overlap**: Both E_cause and E_effect encode identical full video frames — differentiation comes solely from contrastive supervision against cause vs. effect text. Figure 4(b) shows near-complete overlap of cause and effect feature spaces, which the paper interprets as "align[ing] with the inherent structure of causal-temporal narratives." An equally plausible interpretation is that the encoders are not learning meaningfully different representations. The ablation supports this concern: "Only E_cause" achieves CIDEr 56.42 and "Only E_effect" 57.14 on MSVD-CTN, while the full dual-encoder CEN achieves 63.51 (Table 2). The ~7 CIDEr gap is meaningful but could be partially explained by doubled representation capacity. A "double capacity" single-encoder baseline (duplicating features before the decoder) would clarify whether gains come from architectural causality-awareness or capacity doubling, and its absence weakens the architectural claims.

- **Dataset conflation of temporal sequence with causation**: The paper's motivating example (Figure 1) states that a car crash *caused* a group of guys to play beer pong, but these events are merely temporally adjacent — this is clearly not a causal relationship. The dataset generation process labels temporal sequences with "Cause" and "Effect" labels via LLM prompting, without verification that genuine causation exists. This suggests the CTN annotations may systematically confound temporal ordering with causation, meaning CEN may be learning a much simpler task than claimed — essentially temporal ordering with forced causal labels.

### Minor

- **Low EMScore threshold provides limited quality filtering**: The threshold θ=0.2 is very low; 66.4% of unfiltered captions already exceed it (Figure 5). The paper states 52.1% exceed 0.27, meaning nearly half of all generated captions fall below a still-modest threshold. The iterative regeneration process for sub-threshold captions is not well characterized (how many iterations are typically needed, is there an upper limit?).

- **No human evaluation of model outputs**: While the dataset quality is validated, there is no human study rating CEN vs. baseline outputs on causal accuracy, temporal coherence, or factual correctness. This is a significant gap given that the central claim is about generating better causal-temporal narratives.

## Novel Insights

The paper highlights a genuine and underexplored problem — the lack of explicit causal-temporal structure in video captioning benchmarks. However, its proposed solution reveals an inherent tension: using LLMs to generate causal annotations from text alone (without video access) creates labels that may reflect textual inference patterns rather than visual causality, and evaluating against those same LLM-generated labels using n-gram metrics cannot verify genuine causal understanding. The dual-encoder architecture's near-indistinguishable feature spaces for cause and effect raise the broader question of whether video-level representations can meaningfully decompose into cause and effect without explicit temporal segmentation of the input.

## Suggestions

- **Add a human evaluation of model outputs** — not just dataset quality — rating CEN vs. baseline captions on causal accuracy and temporal coherence to directly test the central claim.
- **Include a "double capacity" baseline** — a single CLIP-ViT encoder with duplicated features fed to the decoder, trained on combined cause+effect text with the same total feature dimensionality as CEN, to isolate the contribution of dual contrastive alignment from pure capacity effects.
- **Quantify the causal validity of CTN annotations** — sample and annotate a subset for genuine causation vs. temporal sequence to characterize what the dataset actually measures.
- **Consider temporal segmentation** — experiment with feeding different temporal segments of video to cause vs. effect encoders to create structurally differentiated inputs aligned with the paper's causal-temporal framing.
