## Summary

The paper introduces Preference-based Planning (PbP), a simulated embodied environment built on OmniGibson/Omniverse for studying how AI agents learn human preferences from few demonstrations and use them for planning. PbP defines 290 hierarchical preferences (action, option, and sequence levels) across 50 scenes with 15,000 egocentric video instances. The paper benchmarks several contemporary models (ViViT, LLaVA-NeXT, EILEV, GPT-4V, Llama3-8B, GPT-4, DAG-Opt) under end-to-end and two-stage (preference-prediction-then-planning) settings, finding that explicitly predicted preference labels substantially improve planning, while models—especially vision-based ones—struggle to infer preferences from raw observations.

## Strengths

- **Important and well-motivated problem formulation.** The paper identifies personalization through preference-based planning as a genuine gap in embodied AI, with a clear motivating example (Figure 1) showing how implicit preferences (e.g., "wash fruit before eating") are essential beyond explicit instructions. The connection to psychological literature on preference understanding (Section 1) provides grounding.

- **Thoughtful hierarchical preference design.** The three-tier taxonomy (action, option, sequence levels) captures a meaningful spectrum of personalization—from fine-grained action parameters to sub-task ordering. This hierarchy is a useful conceptual contribution that could inform future work.

- **Substantial benchmark engineering.** PbP provides 290 preferences across 50 scenes with 15k egocentric videos, multiple input modalities (egocentric video, bird's-eye-view maps, text annotations), and systematic evaluation across seven models under two settings. This is a significant resource for the community.

- **Insightful decomposition analysis.** The comparison between end-to-end and two-stage settings (Table 1) clearly reveals that preference inference is the primary bottleneck: GPT-4V's option-level Levenshtein distance drops from 15.63 (end-to-end) to 1.26 (two-stage), demonstrating that planning succeeds when given correct preference labels. The ablation (Table 3) and generalization study (Table 4, Figure 6) further probe prior knowledge and scene transfer, yielding the valuable finding that vision-based models over-rely on contextual visual cues rather than truly abstracting preferences.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The central claim that "preference as a key intermediate representation significantly improves planning" is not cleanly isolated from the effect of additional supervision.** In the two-stage setting, models receive ground-truth preference labels—constituting substantial additional supervision—before planning. The improvement over end-to-end could stem from having more informative input (explicit labels) rather than from the representational choice itself. A fairer test would compare: (a) an end-to-end model trained with the same total supervision (actions + preference labels jointly, without a separate prediction stage); (b) planning conditioned on *predicted* (not ground-truth) preference labels in a single pass; and (c) the current two-stage pipeline. Without such controls, the conclusion that the intermediate representation itself is causally important—rather than just that "more information helps planning"—is not supported. This matters because it is the paper's primary claimed contribution (Abstract: "incorporating preference as a key intermediate representation in planning can significantly improve the personalization and adaptability").

- **"Personalized preference learning" is overclaimed given the benchmark's design.** The 290 preferences are designer-specified generic categories (e.g., "wash fruit before eating," "group by category"), not truly idiosyncratic user preferences. There is no notion of persistent user identity, no inter-individual variability, no inconsistency, and all demonstrations are generated by a deterministic rule-based planner. The paper's title, abstract, and introduction heavily emphasize "personalized" preferences and "human preferences," but what the benchmark actually models is *discrete task-attribute classification from clean demonstrations*. The psychological literature on preferences cited in Section 1 (implicit, unconscious, contradictory) is precisely what PbP does not capture. The paper should either substantially weaken these claims or acknowledge this fundamental scope limitation prominently (not just in a brief limitations paragraph about visual realism).

- **Action-level preferences (75 of 290) are omitted from two-stage evaluation.** The paper defines a three-level hierarchy but only reports option-level and sequence-level results in the two-stage setting (Tables 1, 2, 3). Action-level preferences constitute over 25% of the preference vocabulary; their absence leaves the evaluation of the hierarchy incomplete and leaves the reader unable to assess whether the approach scales to fine-grained preferences.

- **No variation in number of demonstration shots.** The entire benchmark uses exactly 3 demonstrations. A paper centered on *few-shot* learning should evaluate how performance scales with k=1,2,5,10 shots. Without this, we cannot determine whether models are actually performing preference induction or simply pattern-matching at a fixed context length, and the "few-shot" characterization remains unsubstantiated.

### Minor

- **Levenshtein distance as the sole planning metric does not capture semantic preference satisfaction.** Preferences typically induce equivalence classes of acceptable plans (e.g., "wash fruit before eating" is satisfied by many valid action sequences). Levenshtein distance penalizes valid alternative orderings equally with genuine preference violations, and rewards exact trajectory imitation rather than preference compliance. A complementary metric (e.g., checking whether induced ordering constraints hold, or partial credit for satisfying subsets of preference predicates) would strengthen the evaluation.

- **No comparison with prior personalized planning methods discussed in related work.** NeatNet and SAND are cited as the most relevant prior work on personalized embodied planning (Section 2.2), but are never evaluated on PbP. Including these baselines—even with adapted evaluation—would help position the benchmark relative to the methods it claims to advance beyond.

- **Symbol-based vs. video-based model comparison conflates perception difficulty with preference reasoning.** The conclusion that "symbol-based approaches show promise in effectiveness and scalability" largely reflects that symbol-based models receive already-abstracted, lossless action sequences, while video-based models must parse raw visual input. This is an important confound; the paper is transparent about it, but the conclusions in Section 6 should more explicitly acknowledge that the symbol-based results represent an upper bound assuming perfect perception.

- **Insufficient detail on preference vocabulary construction and data splits.** For a benchmark paper, the criteria for selecting the 290 preferences, how the hierarchy was determined, and train/test/validation splits (including whether preferences overlap across splits) are under-specified, limiting reproducibility and assessment of coverage.

## Trivial
- The description of DAG-Opt (Section 4.2) is quite terse; a few sentences clarifying how causal graphs are used at inference time would help readers.
- Figure 6's legend is described in text but difficult to parse; improved captioning would aid interpretation.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Evaluate with a semantic preference-satisfaction metric (e.g., check whether the predicted action sequence satisfies all preference constraints as logical predicates) alongside Levenshtein distance, to distinguish "matches exact trajectory" from "respects the preference."
- Add an experiment varying the number of shots (k=1,2,5,10) to properly evaluate the few-shot claim.
- Report two-stage planning results using *predicted* (not ground-truth) preference labels to quantify the real-world pipeline's performance end-to-end.
- Include action-level results in the two-stage setting to complete the evaluation of the proposed hierarchy.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:

- **The dataset does not instantiate personalization because preferences are generic categories, not user-specific traits** — This is partially retained above as a *major* weakness about overclaiming, but the harsh critic's version overstates the case as if the paper claims nothing valuable. The few-shot inference setup does capture *some* notion of personalization (inferring from observations), even if not the full richness of human preferences. The critique is valid against the *framing*, not against the benchmark's utility as a starting point.

- **Unfair comparison between symbol-based and video-based models is a "methodological gap"** — While the comparison is indeed confounded by input modality, the paper transparently presents these as different input types (Section 4.2 organizes models as "VIDEO-BASED INPUT" vs "SYMBOL-BASED INPUT"). This is a deliberate comparison, not an oversight. The concern is retained as a minor point about how conclusions are drawn, but removed as a major methodological flaw since the paper is not claiming these are controlled comparisons.

- **The two-stage setting "bypasses" few-shot preference learning by using supervised labels** — This is partially valid and retained, but the harsh critic's framing that the paper "doesn't test" few-shot learning at all is wrong. Table 2 *does* evaluate preference prediction from demonstrations, and the end-to-end setting *does* require inference from examples. The valid concern is about how strong the claims should be given that the best planning results use ground-truth labels.

- **Missing baselines like ProgPrompt, Code-as-Policies, SayCan, or more recent VLMs** — This is a nice-to-have rather than a major flaw. The paper already evaluates 7 diverse models and is primarily a benchmark contribution. Additional baselines would strengthen but don't invalidate the findings.

- **The paper should discuss sim-to-real transfer more** — This is scope creep. The paper explicitly acknowledges the synthetic nature of the dataset in limitations. Evaluating sim-to-real transfer is a substantial research direction beyond the scope of a benchmark paper.

- **Noisy/inconsistent preferences are absent** — Valid observation but inherent to the initial benchmark design. The paper's stated scope is learning from clean demonstrations; adding noise is a natural extension, not a core flaw.

- **The societal impact discussion is shallow / doesn't address privacy concerns** — The preferences studied are synthetic (not real user data). Privacy concerns about synthetic preferences are minimal. This is a minor presentation issue, not a substantive weakness.

- **Predefined discrete preferences oversimplify real human preference structure** — This is essentially the same as the personalization overclaim point and is partially retained. The full philosophical argument about continuous/categorical preferences goes beyond what's needed.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most novel finding is the decomposition of preference-based planning into two stages revealing that *preference inference* (not planning) is the bottleneck for current models. The generalization analysis (Table 4, Figure 6) showing that vision-based models rely heavily on scene-specific visual cues rather than abstracting transferable preference knowledge is a particularly insightful empirical observation—models may "get the right answer" for the wrong reasons (visual pattern matching rather than genuine preference understanding). This finding has implications beyond this benchmark for any system that needs to learn reusable abstractions from visual demonstrations.

## Suggestions

- Tone down claims about "personalized" and "human" preferences; reframe the contribution as a *hierarchical preference classification and planning benchmark* with synthetic but structured task attributes, which is still valuable.
- Add a proper ablation that isolates the benefit of the intermediate representation from the benefit of additional supervision (e.g., train an end-to-end model with access to preference labels as auxiliary input).
- Report results on predicted (not ground-truth) preference labels in the two-stage pipeline to show end-to-end performance of the full system, not just each stage in isolation.
