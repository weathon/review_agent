## Summary

This paper proposes EdgePrompt and EdgePrompt+, the first graph prompt tuning methods to operate at the edge level rather than the node level. EdgePrompt learns a shared prompt vector per GNN layer that is aggregated with neighbor representations during message passing; EdgePrompt+ extends this with attention-weighted combinations of shared anchor prompts to enable per-edge customization. The authors evaluate the methods on ten datasets across four pre-training strategies (contrastive and generative) for both node and graph classification, reporting strong empirical improvements over six baselines.

## Strengths

- **Novel and well-motivated perspective.** The paper correctly identifies that existing graph prompt tuning methods (GPF, All-in-one, GraphPrompt, etc.) inject prompts at the node feature or hidden representation level, ignoring structural dependencies. The edge-level formulation in Equation (2) and the conceptual comparison in Table 1 and Figure 1 are clear and technically grounded (Section 4.2).
- **Strong and consistent empirical results.** EdgePrompt+ achieves the best or runner-up accuracy in nearly every experimental setting (Tables 2 and 3), often by large margins (e.g., +15 points on 5-shot Cora under EP-GPPT pre-training, 66.52 vs. 62.84 on NCII09 under GraphCL). The evaluation spans ten diverse datasets and four distinct pre-training strategies, demonstrating broad compatibility.
- **Efficient parameterization for customization.** Rather than learning $O(|\mathcal{E}|)$ independent prompts, EdgePrompt+ represents each edge’s prompt as a weighted average of shared anchor prompts (Equation 4), with weights generated by an attention-based score function (Equations 5–6). This makes per-edge customization feasible in few-shot settings (Section 4.2).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Theorem 2 asserts an implausible universality for arbitrary topology transformations.** Theorem 2 (Section 4.4) claims that for *any* graph transformation $\mathcal{T}$ producing $\mathcal{G}'=(\mathbf{X}',\mathbf{A}')$, there exists a set of *shared additive edge prompts* such that a pre-trained GNN $f$ on the original graph yields exactly the same representations as $f$ on the transformed graph. Under the message-passing formulation in Equation (2), edge prompts are aggregated alongside neighbor representations. For standard architectures such as GCN or GIN, the neighbor set is fixed by the original adjacency $\mathbf{A}$; an additive prompt vector cannot simulate the removal of an existing edge (it cannot nullify a neighbor’s feature contribution) nor the addition of a new edge (it cannot inject a missing neighbor’s feature vector) in a feature-independent way. The theorem statement contains no restriction to feature-space transformations, which appears inconsistent with the mechanics of the very architectures the method targets. Because the paper uses this theorem to conclude that EdgePrompt has “comparable universal capability with GPF,” the theoretical grounding of the contribution is severely weakened.
- **Empirical gains of EdgePrompt+ are confounded by increased architectural capacity without controlled ablation.** EdgePrompt+ introduces an attention-based score function $\phi^{(l)}$, learnable anchor prompts $\mathcal{P}^{(l)}$, and weight matrices $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$—substantially increasing learnable capacity beyond all baselines. The paper does not report parameter counts, compute costs, or a capacity-matched node-prompt baseline (e.g., node-specific prompts generated by an equally expressive attention mechanism). Consequently, the experiments cannot fully disentangle whether the improvements stem from prompting *edges* per se or from adding more flexible learned modules on top of a frozen backbone. This gap weakens the evidence for the central claim that “the crucial role of edges” is the missing ingredient.
- **Theoretical results provide existence proofs without learning guarantees.** Theorem 1 proves that *some* edge prompts exist which improve linear separability on a homophilous contextual SBM, but offers no bound on what gradient-based optimization will find and does not discuss non-homophilous regimes. Theorem 2 is likewise an existence proof for a single fixed graph. Neither connects to the training objective in Equation (7) or the few-shot optimization landscape, making the theoretical analysis largely disconnected from the practical training procedure.

### Minor

- **The simple EdgePrompt variant is not consistently superior to node-prompt baselines.** In several settings, the shared-prompt EdgePrompt trails GPF (e.g., GraphCL on CiteSeer: 43.31 vs. 43.55; SimGRACE on CiteSeer: 43.94 vs. 44.87), suggesting that a global edge prompt alone does not always provide an advantage over node-level prompting. The headline claims should more explicitly acknowledge that the strong results are driven primarily by EdgePrompt+.
- **Confusing terminology in experimental setup.** The paper states that “The classifier adopted for downstream tasks is linear probes for all the methods” (Section 5.1). “Linear probe” typically implies training a linear classifier on *frozen* representations; here the representations are actively modified by learned prompts. The intended meaning is a linear classification head on top of prompted representations, and the phrasing should be clarified.
- **Limited scope of theoretical analysis.** The CSBM analysis in Theorem 1 assumes homophily ($p>q$). The paper does not discuss whether edge prompts could hurt performance on heterophilous graphs where inter-class edges carry useful signal, leaving open the question of whether the method is robust across structural regimes.

### Trivial

- **Equation (2) is slightly under-specified.** The paper states that $\text{AGG}$ aggregates both $\mathbf{h}_j^{(l-1)}$ and $\mathbf{e}_{ij}^{(l)}$ but does not explicitly specify the exact operation (e.g., addition, concatenation, or gating). Explicitly stating the aggregation rule (e.g., $\text{AGG}(\{\mathbf{h}_j^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{e}_{ij}^{(l)}\})$) would improve reproducibility.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Controlled capacity ablation.** Introduce a node-prompt baseline with the same attention-based gating mechanism and parameter budget as EdgePrompt+ to isolate the effect of edge-level versus node-level customization.
- **Parameter and compute accounting.** Report learnable parameter counts and training/inference costs for each method to verify that gains are not simply purchased with extra capacity.
- **Heterophilic evaluation.** Experiments on heterophilous datasets (e.g., Chameleon, Squirrel, Texas) would test whether edge prompting is beneficial or harmful when edges connect different classes.
- **Interpretability analysis.** Visualizing edge prompt weights or correlation with ground-truth edge properties (intra- vs. inter-class) would strengthen the claim that the model captures meaningful structural information.

## Suggestions

- Restrict Theorem 2 to a realistic class of transformations (e.g., feature perturbations or edge reweightings) that additive edge prompts can actually simulate under standard message passing, or provide a construction that explicitly handles topology changes.  
- Add a parameter-count-matched node-prompt baseline (e.g., attention-weighted anchor prompts indexed by node features rather than edge endpoints) to isolate the benefit of the edge perspective from added model capacity.  
- Clarify in Section 5.1 that a linear classification head is trained on top of prompted (not frozen) representations.
