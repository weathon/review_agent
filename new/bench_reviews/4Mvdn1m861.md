## Summary

TokenCount proposes a training-free object counting framework that leverages SAM's output tokens for verification (using TS-SS similarity) and a probabilistic iterative prompt generation strategy to discover objects without additional models or fine-tuning. The method achieves competitive MAE on FSC-147 (16.25, best among SAM-only training-free methods) and strong results on CARPK (4.68 MAE, outperforming all supervised methods), but exhibits high RMSE on FSC-147 and moderate improvement over simpler verification metrics.

## Strengths

- **Novel use of SAM's output tokens for verification**: The insight that SAM's decoder output tokens encode both semantic and positional information through bidirectional attention—and can thus serve as a richer verification signal than raw image embeddings—is genuinely interesting. The PCA visualization (Figure 5) effectively demonstrates that positive and negative instances separate more cleanly in output token space than in image embedding space, and Table 5 confirms the advantage (MAE 16.25 vs. 16.94).

- **Strong CARPK performance**: Achieving MAE 4.68 on CARPK, outperforming all training-based methods, is a notable result for a training-free approach. The consistency of viewpoint and object appearance in this domain plays to SAM's segmentation strengths, and the result demonstrates genuine practical utility.

- **Clean training-free, SAM-only paradigm**: Unlike TFCAC (which requires DINO alongside SAM), this method requires no additional models—a genuine deployment advantage in terms of simplicity and reduced computational prerequisites.

- **Principled prompt generation design**: The iterative probabilistic prompt generation with probability updates (zeroing positive regions, decreasing negative regions) is a well-motivated alternative to fixed grids or superpixels, and the ablation in Table 4 confirms that increasing prompts per iteration improves MAE from 21.74→16.25.

- **Comprehensive ablation studies**: Tables 3–5 systematically evaluate similarity metrics (TS-SS vs. cosine/L2/dot product), iteration/prompt configurations, and verification space (output tokens vs. image embeddings), providing useful evidence for the design choices.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **High RMSE on FSC-147 undermines reliability claims**: While the MAE of 16.25 is competitive among SAM-only training-free methods, the RMSE of 133.11 is dramatically worse than TFCAC (56.33) and LOCA (56.97). This large MAE–RMSE gap indicates significant outlier predictions on some images, particularly dense scenes with small objects—exactly the scenarios where robust counting matters most. The paper acknowledges this limitation in the Discussion ("performance degradation in scenarios with dense distributions of very small instances") but provides no quantitative breakdown of errors by object density or size category, making it impossible to assess the severity and scope of this failure. This is a meaningful concern that limits the practical utility of the method.

- **Marginal improvement from TS-SS does not support the core novelty claim**: The paper's central methodological contribution is the TS-SS metric on output tokens, argued to be necessary because standard metrics "have reached a performance plateau." However, the ablation (Table 3) shows only a 0.23 MAE improvement over cosine similarity (16.25 vs. 16.48) and 0.28 over L2 distance (16.25 vs. 16.53). These differences are within typical experimental variance for a single-run evaluation on a benchmark without confidence intervals. The narrative that TS-SS is a critical breakthrough is disproportionate to the evidence—the data suggest that almost any reasonable similarity metric over output tokens yields similar performance, and the real advantage comes from using output tokens rather than image embeddings (Table 5: 0.69 MAE gap). This weakens the paper's central claim that TS-SS is a key contribution.

- **Efficiency claims lack quantitative substantiation**: The paper repeatedly positions its method as more efficient than SAM+α methods (particularly TFCAC), claiming it is "more than twice as fast" on CARPK and features "superior computational efficiency." However, the only runtime reported is 1.69s/image for FSC-147 on a single A100, with no comparison to other methods' runtimes, no FLOPs analysis, and no prompt count comparison with baselines. The ablation (Table 4) reveals that the best configuration requires an average of 1438 prompts, which is substantial. Without controlled efficiency comparisons, the efficiency narrative is unsupported.

### Minor

- **Overclaims about "bypassing uncertainty of similarity"**: The paper frames its contribution as bypassing the uncertainty of image embedding similarity (Section 1: "bypass the uncertainty of similarity"). In reality, the probabilistic prompt generation (Eq. 1) still uses cosine similarity in image embedding space to create the initial probability distribution, and the verification stage uses TS-SS (a similarity metric). The method doesn't bypass similarity—it operates in a different representation space with a different metric. The improvement over image embedding cosine similarity is real but modest (Table 5: 0.69 MAE), and the framing overstates the conceptual departure from prior work.

- **Incomplete specification of algorithmic details**: The probability decrease factor for negative regions is described vaguely ("values decrease") without specifying the exact update rule (multiplicative? additive? what factor?). The early stopping mechanism (IoU > 0.95, patience = 5) is briefly mentioned in the ablation text rather than formally described in the method section. While these details don't invalidate the approach, they hinder reproducibility and make it difficult to assess the method's practical behavior.

- **One-shot results table (Table 2) is incomplete**: Several methods present in the main comparison (Table 1)—including SAFECount, PseCo, TFCounter, OmniCount—are missing from the one-shot comparison. The one-shot MAE of 18.57 is already higher than several training-based methods, and the table lacks NAE and SRE columns, making it difficult to assess the method's full competitive position in this important setting.

- **No analysis of per-category variance on FSC-147**: The 147 categories span very different object types, and aggregate MAE can be dominated by easy categories. Without per-category or density-stratified results, it's unclear whether the method generalizes well across the full diversity of FSC-147 or is carried by a subset of easier categories.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Density/size-stratified error analysis on FSC-147 to quantify exactly where and how badly the method fails on dense scenes, enabling targeted future improvements.
- Runtime/FLOPs comparison table against TFCAC, OmniCount, and TFOC to substantiate efficiency claims.
- Sensitivity analysis for the verification threshold (300) and temperature τ, which are critical hyperparameters set via "validation experiments" without further analysis.

## Removed Points

- **"Circular use of SAM masks" (from harsh critic #1)**: Removed because the mask M in Eq. 1 is generated from the user-provided exemplar prompt e, not from pre-segmented target objects. The process follows standard exemplar-based counting: (1) user provides an exemplar, (2) SAM segments it, (3) exemplar features guide probability distribution over the query image. There is no circularity—verified positive objects are only added in subsequent iterations, and their mask regions are zeroed out (reducing probability, not creating it). This is a fundamental misunderstanding of the pipeline.

- **"Comparisons not fully fair because prompt counts and hyperparameters not detailed for baselines"**: The paper organizes comparisons into "SAM+α" and "SAM only" categories, which is a useful distinction. TFCAC's use of DINO is explicitly acknowledged as the reason for its higher accuracy. The comparison is reasonably standard for the field; demanding prompt-level matched comparisons across competing methods is beyond what's typical for this venue.

- **"No confidence intervals or statistical tests"**: Single-run evaluation without variance reporting is standard practice in the counting literature (all competing methods in Table 1 report single numbers). Flagging this as a minor concern is reasonable, but it should not be elevated to a major weakness when it reflects community norms.

- **"Conceptual departure claim is misleading" (from harsh critic #6)**: While the framing of "bypassing uncertainty" is somewhat overclaimed, this has been moved to Minor weakness #4 with tempered language. The claim that the method "bypasses uncertainty of similarity" is overclaiming, but it is not dishonest—the method does operate in a different space with a different mechanism, yielding modest gains. Calling it "misleading" overstates the issue.

- **"Unpublished baselines (OmniCount, TFCounter, TFCAC)"**: Removed per the rule that cited references are assumed to exist and be available.

## Novel Insights

The output token representation offers a genuinely useful alternative to image embedding similarity for SAM-based counting, and the PCA visualization confirms measurably better cluster separability. However, the marginal improvement from TS-SS over simpler metrics suggests that the primary value lies in choosing the right representation (output tokens vs. image embeddings), not in the specific similarity metric. Future work might benefit more from improving prompt coverage and early stopping robustness than from further metric refinement.

## Suggestions

- Report RMSE alongside MAE in all comparisons and add a density-stratified analysis on FSC-147 to honestly characterize the method's failure modes, especially for dense/small objects.
- Conduct an ablation that replaces probabilistic prompt generation with grid/superpixel in the same output-token verification framework, to cleanly isolate the contribution of each proposed component.
- Provide a wall-clock time and prompt count comparison table against at least TFCAC and TFOC to substantiate efficiency claims.
- Tone down the "bypassing uncertainty" and "superior accuracy" claims in the abstract and introduction to accurately reflect the modest but real improvements observed.
- Add the missing one-shot baselines and metrics to Table 2 for completeness.
