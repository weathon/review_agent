Let me now compose the final review.</think>## Summary

The paper introduces a "self-knowledge evaluation framework" for LLMs and LMMs, inspired by Feynman's principle that understanding is demonstrated through creation. The framework uses a "First Generate, Then Evaluate" pipeline: a model first generates content, then in a separate call is asked to verify properties of what it generated. Across 7 LLMs, 2 LMMs, and 9 tasks (word counting, designated word counting, facts, ArXiv recall, math, theorem proving, code, grammar, SQL-type operations), the paper finds significant gaps in models' ability to answer questions about their own generations, and proposes that attention misalignment may explain these gaps. It also explores in-context evaluation and fine-tuning on self-generated math data.

## Strengths

- **Novel and intuitive evaluation paradigm**: The "First Generate, Then Evaluate" framework (Section 3, Equations 1–2) is conceptually straightforward and requires no expert-annotated benchmarks — only paired generating/verifying prompts. This makes it easy to implement and extensible to new tasks, which is a genuine practical contribution.

- **Consistently striking evidence of model failures on simple self-referential tasks**: Table 1 shows near-zero accuracy on total word counting (0.00–0.03 across all 7 models), revealing that even state-of-the-art LLMs fail dramatically on a task that should be trivial for any entity that "understands what it creates." This is a surprising and informative finding regardless of interpretive framing.

- **Clever consistency-based evaluation (Equation 3)**: The transformation τ (e.g., moving the first sentence to the end preserves preposition count) enables self-knowledge scoring without ground-truth answers, extending the framework to tasks where answers are unknown. This is a creative methodological contribution (Section 4.4, Figure 2).

- **In-context evaluation experiment provides valuable insight (Table 6)**: Showing that GPT-4 and Gemma achieve 100% when the generation transcript is in-context (versus 0.03 and 0.00 without) reveals that the bottleneck is processing context rather than possessing latent "self-knowledge." Though the authors frame this as supporting their framework, the result is genuinely informative regardless of interpretation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The "self-knowledge" metric conflates instruction-following failures with genuine self-assessment failures, making scores hard to interpret.** The core metric is 𝟙(a = â) (Section 3, Eq. after Eq. 2), where a is the prompt-specified answer and â is the verification answer. In the word counting example (Figure 1), the model generates ~63 words instead of the requested 56, then correctly answers "63" when asked to count. The model *accurately reports what it produced*, yet scores 0 because 63 ≠ 56. The paper never decomposes scores into (a) whether the model's generation met the specified constraint, vs. (b) whether the model's verification answer matches the *actual* properties of what it generated. Without this decomposition, the near-zero total-count scores (Table 1) are ambiguous — they could reflect instruction-following failures rather than a failure to "understand what one creates." This issue affects any task where the generation constraint is pre-specified (Sections 4.2.1, 4.2.2, 4.2.5, 4.2.7), which covers the majority of reported results.

- **The in-context evaluation results (Table 6) undermine the "self-knowledge" interpretation without adequate reconciliation.** When the generation process is provided in-context, GPT-4 scores 1.00 (up from 0.03) and Gemma scores 1.00 (up from 0.00). If the framework genuinely measured whether models possess latent "self-knowledge" of their creations, access to the generation transcript should be irrelevant — the model already "created" the content and should "understand" it. The dramatic improvement instead demonstrates these tasks primarily measure context-dependent processing ability, not a latent self-knowledge property. The paper presents this as an ancillary finding (Section 6.2) but does not reconcile it with the central "self-knowledge" framing.

- **Fine-tuning claims about self-improvement lack necessary baselines (Section 6.3, Table 7, Figure 3).** The paper concludes that "self-improving is a promising direction" based on fine-tuning on self-generated math data. However, there is no comparison to fine-tuning on *externally sourced* math data of similar format and difficulty. Without this baseline, improvements from self-generated data cannot be attributed to "self-knowledge" exploitation rather than simple domain exposure — especially since fine-tuning on *uncorrected/wrong* self-generated data also improves GSM-8k accuracy (Llama2: +1.21% on wrong data vs. +0.80% on correct data; Gemma: +0.19% on wrong vs. +0.11% on correct, Figure 3). This pattern suggests domain familiarity rather than genuine self-knowledge exploitation.

### Minor

- **The "attention misalignment" analysis (Section 6.1, Table 5) is speculative.** The attention-based score uses an arbitrarily chosen top-15% threshold, is applied to only one task (designated word counting) across five open-source models, and has no significance tests or ablation over the threshold choice. The "additive effect" narrative is post-hoc interpretation of a handful of data points. This weakens the mechanistic claim but does not invalidate it as an initial hypothesis.

- **The consistency-based evaluation (Equation 3) does not empirically verify that the transformation τ preserves the target property.** For the grammar task (Section 4.4.1), moving the first sentence to the end of a paragraph *should* preserve preposition count in theory, but boundary effects (e.g., "in" at the start of a standalone sentence vs. mid-paragraph) could alter the count for specific generated texts. The paper notes this property but does not validate it programmatically. This is a minor methodological gap since errors from occasional violations would likely be small.

- **The LMM evaluation (Section 5, Table 4) is thin:** only 2 models and 3 tasks with no variance reported and limited analysis. This limits the contribution of the LMM extension but does not affect the LLM results.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Decomposing the self-knowledge score into generation accuracy vs. verification accuracy for each task would significantly strengthen interpretability and help disentangle the two failure modes.
- Adding a fine-tuning baseline using externally-sourced math data of comparable difficulty would clarify whether self-generated data has special value.
- Cross-model consistency experiments (Model A generates, Model B verifies) would establish whether "self-knowledge" outperforms mere "knowledge."

## Removed Points

- **Feynman analogy is misleading (Harsh Critic)**: The critic argues the Feynman quote doesn't apply because the evaluation uses separate calls (not a single entity with memory). While there is a conceptual gap between the analogy and the two-call framework, this is a framing/decorative issue, not a methodological flaw. The framework stands on its own regardless of the quote. Moved because it's an interpretive preference, not a substantive error.

- **Task collection lacks theoretical unity (Harsh Critic)**: The critic argues the tasks are a "miscellaneous collection" and shouldn't be averaged. However, the paper includes an "Avg" column as a convenience, and each task is reported separately. The individual task results are informative. Averaging diverse tasks is common in benchmark papers. This is a minor presentation concern, not a major issue.

- **Stochastic resonance explanation is hand-waving (Harsh Critic)**: The critic calls the "stochastic resonance" explanation for noise improving GPT-3.5 and Qwen results hand-waving. The paper itself treats this as a conjecture ("We conjecture that this weird phenomenon may be attributed to..."), and the main point is that noise reduces performance of perfect in-context evaluators. This is a minor observation, not a core claim.

- **Missing significance tests/variance for LMM results (Harsh Critic)**: The LMM evaluation is limited (2 models, 3 tasks), but this is acknowledged by the paper as a preliminary extension due to the scarcity of text+image generating LMMs. The lack of variance/significance is a minor concern given the small scale.

- **Fine-tuning is underpowered (100 samples, 3 epochs) (Harsh Critic)**: The fine-tuning is presented as an exploratory study ("We are also interested in the following question"), not a definitive claim. The setup is reasonable for a proof-of-concept, and the main claims of the paper do not rest on it.

- **Strength Finder's claim about mechanistic explanation for self-knowledge failures (Supporting strength #1)**: The attention analysis (Section 6.1) is speculative with arbitrary thresholds and minimal data points. Promoting this as a strength overstates the evidence, especially given the verified weakness on this point.

- **Strength Finder's claim about "human-like forgetting" (Supporting strength #2)**: The in-context evaluation with noise only shows this for 2 of 7 models (GPT-4 and Gemma). The other models show no such pattern (Llama2 and Llama3 score 0.00 in all conditions). This is not a general finding about "human-like" behavior.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most interesting finding is not the "self-knowledge gap" (which, as argued above, conflates two distinct phenomena) but rather the decomposition implied by Table 6: when models can see their own generation process, some achieve perfect accuracy while others still fail completely. This suggests the bottleneck is not a single unified "self-knowledge" deficit but rather two separable problems — (a) the model's ability to process context and count/reason about visible text, and (b) the instruction-following precision to generate content matching specified constraints. These are conflated in the current metric but are conceptually distinct.

## Suggestions

- Decompose the self-knowledge metric into two components: (1) generation fidelity (whether the model's output matches specified constraints) and (2) verification accuracy (whether ẑ matches the true properties of the generated text x, independent of whether x matches the prompt's specifications). This would make the results interpretable and isolate genuine self-assessment failures.
- Reframe the contribution: "cross-call consistency" or "generation-verification consistency" more accurately describes what is measured than "self-knowledge." The Feynman framing could be presented as inspiration rather than direct analogy.
- Add an external-data fine-tuning baseline (e.g., GSM-8k training set) to determine whether self-generated data has any special value beyond domain exposure.
