## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content and global performance style representations. The model is trained on sequence-aligned data without note-level alignment, and a separate diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module generates style embeddings directly from score content. Experiments show competitive results on both tasks, along with demonstrated disentanglement and style transfer capabilities.

## Strengths

- **Novel unified formulation**: Jointly modeling EPR and APT—two inverse tasks—in a shared architecture with content/style disentanglement is a principled and creative contribution that mirrors successful approaches in ASR/TTS and opens a clear path to leveraging both paired and unpaired data.

- **Alignment-free Seq2Seq formulation for EPR**: Removing the need for note-level alignment in training is a meaningful practical simplification. The paper demonstrates competitive EPR results without this requirement, which is a genuine advantage for scalability.

- **PSR module concept**: The idea of a diffusion model that generates stylistically appropriate style embeddings from score content alone—mimicking a pianist selecting appropriate interpretation—is conceptually appealing and practically useful for non-expert users.

- **Comprehensive evaluation**: Objective metrics (MUSTER, ScoreSimilarity, alignment/insertion/missing rates, variance, KL, MAE) alongside subjective listening tests and disentanglement analysis (performer/composer classification, style transfer, latent visualization) provide multiple evaluation angles.

- **Use of unpaired data**: The masked reconstruction objective enables leveraging large-scale unpaired score and performance corpora, aligning with modern self-supervised training trends and addressing data scarcity.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **Missing ablation isolating joint training benefits from unpaired data benefits**: The paper's central narrative claims that joint EPR+APT modeling provides "mutual supervision." However, the EPR-Only baseline is trained only on ASAP (paired data), while the full joint model uses both paired and unpaired data. The stated improvement of "Ours-Target over the EPR-Only variant" conflates two factors: joint training and additional unpaired data. No controlled experiment separates these. This is a significant gap because the paper's primary framing is about the *duality* benefit, but the evidence does not isolate it. At best, the paper shows that EPR and APT can coexist in one model—not that doing both jointly is better than doing either alone.

- **PSR "stylistically appropriate recommendation" claim is oversold relative to evidence**: The core promise is that PSR recommends styles *appropriate to the specific score*. The evidence consists of: (1) subjective listening tests that evaluate quality, not appropriateness per se (no condition testing whether PSR-assigned styles are better than random or era-averaged styles); (2) 2D cluster visualizations showing rough era-level correspondence between PSR-generated and real style distributions (Figure 4), which demonstrates population-level distribution matching but not piece-level appropriateness; (3) no comparison to simple baselines (e.g., conditioning on composer label, sampling from a prior, or using a mean style per era). Without these, it is unclear whether PSR does more than broad era/composer matching, which a much simpler method could achieve.

- **Disentanglement evaluation confounds architectural choices with actual separation**: Table 4 shows that style representations (global CLS token) achieve much higher performer/composer identification than content representations (note-level sequence). However, zc is note-level while zs is a global vector—this architectural asymmetry inherently makes classification from zs easier (aggregation is built-in). The KL regularization on zs but not zc further biases the comparison. No experiment controls for these confounds (e.g., projecting zc to a global representation before classification, or comparing mutual information between the spaces). The Cont→Comp accuracy of 29.99% also suggests incomplete disentanglement, which the paper acknowledges but does not rigorously investigate.

### Minor:

- **Subjective evaluation is small-scale**: 11 participants rating 5 pieces provides limited statistical power. No inter-rater reliability measure (e.g., Krippendorff's alpha) or significance tests on subjective ratings are reported, making it hard to draw confident conclusions from the listening tests.

- **The "alignment-free" narrative is slightly misleading for practitioners**: While training does not require note-level alignment, evaluation metrics (alignment rate, MAE) still depend on alignment. This is a conceptual clarification rather than a flaw in the method itself.

- **Unpaired performance data introduces transcription bias**: The unpaired performance dataset is generated via an audio-to-MIDI model, which introduces systematic transcription errors. The paper acknowledges selecting the model via a "pilot study" but does not discuss how these errors may propagate into the learned style distribution or masked reconstruction objectives.

- **EPR-Only baseline specification is insufficient**: The paper does not clearly state whether EPR-Only shares the same architecture (parameter count, hyperparameters) and training data as the EPR branch of the joint model. This makes the comparison in Table 2 hard to interpret.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most interesting insight is the conceptual symmetry between EPR and APT as inverse transformations bridged by a shared content space, and the practical observation that global style embeddings (conditioned via CLS tokens with KL regularization) carry strong composer and performer identity—suggesting that expert pianists do encode composer-specific stylistic conventions into their performances, which can be partially recovered and transferred. The finding that Cont→Comp accuracy is non-trivially high (29.99%) while still much lower than Style→Comp (77.46%) points to a genuine partial overlap between content and style that could inform future work on more fine-grained disentanglement.

## Suggestions

- Add a controlled ablation: EPR-Only with unpaired data vs. joint model with paired data only vs. joint model with all data, to isolate the contribution of joint modeling from data scaling.
- Evaluate PSR against simpler score-to-style mappings (e.g., per-composer mean style, deterministic regression) to demonstrate when and whether the DDPM formulation adds value.
- Report inter-rater reliability for subjective evaluations and increase the number of test pieces if possible.
