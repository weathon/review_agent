## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content and global performance style representations. The model is trained on both paired and unpaired data using a Seq2Seq formulation that eliminates the need for note-level alignment. An independent diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module generates style embeddings directly from score content, enabling automated and controllable rendering.

## Strengths

- **Novel joint EPR/APT framework well-motivated by ASR/TTS analogy.** The conceptual insight that EPR and APT are inverse tasks that can benefit from shared representations is genuinely original. The paper (Section 1) draws a clear analogy to joint ASR/TTS modeling and motivates the dual-task perspective convincingly.

- **Seq2Seq EPR formulation eliminates note-level alignment.** Unlike all alignment-dependent baselines (Rhyu et al., 2022; Borovik & Viro, 2023; Zhang et al., 2024), the model requires only sequence-level supervision. Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) in Table 3, demonstrating that competitive EPR accuracy is attainable without costly alignment preprocessing.

- **Strong subjective evaluation results.** Figure 2 shows Ours (Target) achieves the highest ratings across all four dimensions (dynamics, tempo, style, overall human-likeness) against human renditions and strong baselines, providing direct evidence that the generated performances are perceptually convincing.

- **Meaningful disentanglement evidence via probing.** Table 4 shows style representations achieve 77.46% composer and 42.07% performer identification accuracy versus only 29.99% and 9.94% for content representations—a large gap confirming meaningful separation. Figure 3 shows clear clustering by composer and performer in the style embedding space.

- **PSR module addresses a genuine usability gap.** The concept of automatically inferring stylistically appropriate performance parameters from score content alone (Section 3.4) is novel and removes the need for manual composer/performer labels or expressive parameter control, making EPR accessible to non-expert users.

- **Competitive APT results despite alignment-free formulation.** In Table 1, the model achieves the best Eavg (12.48) and Espell (6.24) among all systems, and the lowest Eextra (7.33), demonstrating that the alignment-free approach captures key musical attributes.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Confounded comparison between EPR-Only and Ours (Target) undermines the core "joint modeling benefit" claim.** The EPR-Only variant is "trained solely on the ASAP dataset" (Section 5.1), while Ours (Target) additionally uses 75,913 unpaired scores and YouTube-sourced performances. The paper itself acknowledges the improvement comes from "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data" but presents this as validation of the joint framework. Without an EPR-only model trained on the same combined data (paired + unpaired), it is impossible to determine whether improvements in Table 2 (e.g., MAE(V): 10.33 vs 10.65; KL(V): 1.76 vs 2.05) stem from the joint modeling objective or simply from data volume. This is not a minor gap—it concerns the paper's central motivation.

- **PSR module—one of three stated contributions—lacks quantitative evaluation.** Section 5.3 evaluates the PSR module through 2D cluster visualizations (Figure 4) and the indirect subjective results of Figure 2, but no quantitative metric is provided: no cosine similarity, L2 distance, or distributional divergence between PSR-generated and ground-truth style embeddings. The claim that PSR "produces stylistically appropriate outputs conditioned solely on the score" (Abstract) rests on visual similarity in 2D projections, which is unreliable evidence for high-dimensional embedding quality.

### Minor

- **No APT-only variant to verify joint modeling benefit for transcription.** Without an APT-only version of the same architecture, we cannot determine whether joint EPR/APT training helps, hurts, or is irrelevant for APT. The APT comparison only shows performance relative to external baselines, not relative to what the same architecture achieves in isolation.

- **Pitch error gap in APT not thoroughly analyzed.** Ep = 3.08 versus 2.02–2.11 for note-aligned methods (Table 1) represents a ~50% increase. The paper attributes this to the alignment-free formulation simplifying pitch prediction for baselines but "limiting flexibility," yet does not provide analysis of what types of pitch errors occur or whether they correlate with the claimed flexibility benefits (e.g., ornaments, trills).

- **Ours (Target) has the highest miss rate among EPR models in Table 3** (4.32% vs DExter's 3.62%, VirtuosoNet's 3.90%, and Ours PSR's 3.96%). While this is offset by strong performance on other metrics and the PSR variant, it warrants acknowledgment as a limitation of the target-style condition.

- **Style transfer evaluation is anecdotal (3 samples, mixed results).** Figure 5 shows only 3 test cases, and Sample 2 shows the Target condition receiving lower style similarity than Original, contradicting the "successful transfer" claim. The paper bases its conclusion on Samples 1 and 3 only. Three anecdotal samples do not establish "reliable style transfer."

- **Partial disentanglement: content representations encode some composer information.** Cont→Comp accuracy of 29.99% in Table 4 is well above chance (4% for 25 composers), indicating content representations carry composer-correlated information. The paper's explanation—that "professional pianists align their style with the composer"—is reasonable for style→composer correlation but does not explain why content (pitch/rhythm) predicts composer identity. This suggests disentanglement is meaningful but incomplete.

## Novel Insights

The paper's observation that content representations encode non-trivial composer identity (29.99% accuracy with 25-class chance at 4%) despite being architecturally constrained to note-level features raises an interesting question: in music, content and style may be inherently entangled at the compositional level (Bach writes different content than Chopin), making complete disentanglement potentially unattainable—and perhaps unnecessary. The real metric should be whether the disentanglement is *functionally* sufficient for the downstream tasks (style transfer, PSR), not whether it achieves perfect statistical independence.

## Suggestions

- Train an EPR-only model on the same data (ASAP + unpaired scores + unpaired performances) to disentangle the joint modeling effect from the data volume effect. This single experiment would substantially strengthen or clarify the core claim.

- Add a quantitative PSR evaluation metric (e.g., average cosine similarity between PSR-generated and ground-truth style embeddings, or Fréchet Style Distance) to empirically validate the PSR module's generation quality.

- Expand style transfer evaluation beyond 3 samples and include an objective content preservation metric (e.g., onset/duration correlation between original and style-transferred performances) to substantiate the "reliable style transfer" claim.
