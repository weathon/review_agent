## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style representations. The model uses a Seq2Seq formulation that eliminates the need for note-level alignment, and introduces a separate diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates stylistically appropriate style embeddings from score content alone. Experiments on ASAP and ATEPP datasets show competitive EPR and APT performance, successful content-style disentanglement, and effective style transfer.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated and coherent framework**: Jointly modeling EPR and APT as dual/inverse tasks, analogous to the ASR/TTS unification in speech, is a natural and meaningful direction. The architectural design—dual content encoders sharing a latent space, a global style vector conditioned via broadcast addition, and separate decoders—is clean and well-specified.

- **Practical contribution of alignment-free EPR**: Reformulating EPR as Seq2Seq to avoid note-level alignment is a tangible practical advance. Prior EPR systems require alignment tools that struggle with ornaments, trills, and one-to-many mappings. Table 3 shows the model achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%), supporting this design choice.

- **Comprehensive multi-faceted evaluation**: The paper includes objective EPR metrics (variance, KL divergence, MAE, alignment/insertion/missing rates), APT evaluation against multiple baselines, subjective listening tests with 11 musically trained participants, composer/performer identification experiments on ATEPP, style transfer evaluation, and PSR visualization. This is a thorough evaluation package for this domain.

- **Strong EPR results**: Ours (Target) achieves the best or second-best results on most EPR metrics in Table 2 (lowest MAE(V) of 10.33, best KL(D) of 1.76 after DExter), and the subjective evaluations (Figure 2) show clear preference over baselines.

- **Useful PSR concept**: The idea of automatically recommending performance styles from score content—mimicking a pianist's interpretation—is practically valuable for non-expert users and creative applications.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **Incomplete evidence for *mutual* benefit of joint EPR–APT learning**: The paper's central claim is "leveraging the duality between the two tasks for mutual supervision" (Abstract). However, the only ablation is EPR-Only vs. Ours(Target), which changes *both* the architecture (joint vs. single-task) *and* the data (paired-only vs. paired+unpaired). There is no APT-Only variant, so we cannot tell whether APT benefits from joint training at all, nor whether the EPR gains come from multi-task learning vs. simply adding unpaired data. The phrase "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data, leads to better EPR performance" (§5.1) honestly concedes this confound, but the framing elsewhere implies "mutual" benefit that is not demonstrated.

- **Disentanglement evidence is confounded with architectural asymmetries**: The disentanglement claim rests on classification results (Table 4) showing style vectors predict composer/performer much better than content vectors. However, the architecture itself hard-codes two asymmetries: (1) z_c is a *sequence* of note-level embeddings averaged for classification, while z_s is a single *global* [CLS] embedding—these are inherently different representations; (2) z_c is extracted from *scores*, z_s from *performances*. The classification results are almost inevitable under these design choices regardless of any disentanglement loss. The paper acknowledges the global-vs-sequence point (§5.2), but does not control for it. Without ablations testing content encoders that produce global representations, or style encoders that produce sequence-level ones, the "disentanglement" claim overstates the contribution of the disentanglement mechanism per se.

- **PSR module lacks comparison to simpler baselines**: The PSR is presented as a key contribution, yet there is no comparison against trivial alternatives such as sampling from the learned Gaussian prior N(0,I), a simple feed-forward regression from score to style embedding, or nearest-neighbor retrieval of style vectors from the training set by composer/era. Without these baselines, it is unclear whether the DDPM formulation is necessary or whether any competent score-conditioned global embedding would suffice. The visual clustering in Figure 4 (era-level separation in 2D) is a very coarse success criterion, and the gap between Ours(PSR) and Ours(Target) in Table 2 (MAE(V): 15.24 vs. 10.33; KL(V): 2.67 vs. 1.76) is substantial.

### Minor:

- **Content alignment between z_x and z_y is underspecified**: Figure 1 caption states that "score content features zx and zy are encouraged to align," but no explicit alignment loss (e.g., contrastive loss, L2 distance) appears in Section 3.2 or 3.3. If alignment is achieved implicitly via shared decoder supervision, this should be stated clearly; if there is an explicit term, it needs to be documented.

- **Subjective evaluation covers only 5 pieces by 5 composers with 11 participants**: While acceptable as preliminary evidence, this is a limited sample for claims about generalizability. No inter-rater reliability or statistical significance tests between systems are reported.

- **Style transfer evaluation is limited to 3 hand-picked examples**: Figure 5 shows results for just 3 sample pairs. No systematic cross-composer or cross-genre evaluation with automated metrics (e.g., style classifier accuracy on transferred outputs) is provided.

- **The claim that the model handles "musically complex, one-to-many contexts (e.g., ornaments, trills, or expressive deviations)" (§1, §5.1) is not empirically demonstrated**: No analysis focuses on these specific musical phenomena, which is exactly where alignment-based methods should struggle most. Showing this advantage would significantly strengthen the paper.

### Trivial:

- The KL regularization weight (β or annealing schedule) is not specified; presumably it is 1. This minor omission does not affect the core claims.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Clean ablation study** isolating: (a) joint training vs. single-task, (b) paired-only vs. paired+unpaired data, (c) impact of KL regularization on style space smoothness. This would substantiate the claimed contributions more precisely.

- **Quantitative disentanglement metrics** (e.g., MIG, DCI, or a swap reconstruction test) to complement the classification-based evaluation.

- **Comparison of PSR to simpler alternatives** (prior sampling, nearest-neighbor, linear regression) to justify the DDPM choice.

- Evaluation on at least one non-classical-piano dataset to assess generalizability.

- Report inference cost/latency of the diffusion-based PSR module.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.

- **"ASAP is note-aligned, so the alignment-free claim is weakened"**: The paper's claim is that the model does not *require* note-level alignment for *training* (it uses Seq2Seq). The fact that ASAP happens to have alignments (used for evaluation) is irrelevant—the model never consumes them during training. This is the whole point: you can train on simple sequence pairs without alignment, and the evaluation uses alignment as an external metric. This is a legitimate design, not a weakness.

- **"Evaluation metrics for EPR implicitly rely on note alignment"**: This is standard practice. All EPR baselines use the same alignment-based evaluation protocol. The paper uses alignment rate and insertion/missing rates as evaluation metrics, which is the accepted practice in this field. The alignment for evaluation is derived from the sequence-level predicted tokens matched against references—it does not require a separate external aligner.

- **"Unfair comparison with alignment-based baselines because the model doesn't use alignment"**: The asymmetry actually *favors* the baselines (they use alignment supervision during training), making the model's competitive results a stronger claim, not a weaker one.

- **"Missing comparison with Jung et al. (2025)"**: The paper references Jung et al. as concurrent work motivating the direction, but it was published after this work was likely developed. Demanding a direct comparison with very recent concurrent work is scope creep.

- **"Reproducibility concerns about hyperparameters"**: The paper describes architecture, training schedule, learning rates, mask rates, and dataset splits. The claim that MASK function details are missing is incorrect—Section 3.2 defines it clearly as replacing tokens with a special ⟨MASK⟩ token.

- **"The content encoder for ATEPP is derived from scores while style encoder from performances—this is a modality confound"**: This is by design. EPR takes a score and produces a performance; APT takes a performance and produces a score. The content representations from both domains should capture the same score-level content (pitch, rhythm), while style comes from the performance domain. This is the intended asymmetry, not a confound.

## Novel Insights

The framing of EPR and APT as inverse tasks sharing a content space, with a disentangled style factor conditioned via broadcast addition, is a clean architectural contribution. The most interesting empirical finding is that a single global style vector (z_s ∈ R^D) added to all time steps achieves competitive EPR results—suggesting that much of expressive variation can be captured by a global conditioning signal rather than per-note style features as used in prior work like DExter. However, the 30% composer accuracy from content vectors suggests significant style information leaks into the content representation, qualifying the disentanglement claim.

## Suggestions

- **Add two critical ablations**: (1) Joint model trained on paired data only (no unpaired), vs. EPR-Only trained on paired+unpaired data. This isolates the effect of joint training from the effect of additional data. (2) An APT-Only variant to demonstrate whether APT benefits from joint training.

- **Add a simple PSR baseline**: Replace the DDPM with a mean prediction (or prior sampling + nearest-neighbor) to show whether diffusion is necessary for PSR.

- **Moderate the language**: Change "mutual supervision" to "joint training" until mutual benefit for APT is demonstrated; change "disentangled" to "separated" or add caveats about the architectural confounds.

- **Analyze performance on musically challenging passages** (ornaments, trills) to concretely demonstrate the advantage of alignment-free EPR.
