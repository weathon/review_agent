## Summary

The paper introduces a comprehensive framework for modeling and evaluating episodic memory capabilities in LLMs, grounded in cognitive science concepts (Tulving's encoding specificity, cue-based recall). Events are modeled as structured tuples (time, space, entity, content), and all possible cue compositions are systematically varied to create 36 task types. A contamination-free synthetic benchmark is generated using LLM-based chapter creation with controlled ground truth, and state-of-the-art LLMs are evaluated across in-context, RAG, and fine-tuning strategies. The benchmark reveals that even top models achieve F1 ≤ 0.60 on multi-event recall and identifies a novel cue-type difficulty gradient (content > entity > space > time).

## Strengths

- **Systematic cue composition framework (Table 1, Section 3.2):** By exhaustively varying which tuple elements serve as cues, the benchmark provides structured coverage of recall types that no prior benchmark offers. The 36 task types derived from the tuple schema go well beyond ad-hoc question design in prior work like bAbI or needle-in-a-haystack benchmarks.

- **Novel cue-type difficulty gradient (Figure 4):** The consistent finding that content cues yield highest F1, followed by entity, space, and time cues—across all models and numbers of matching events—is a concrete, reproducible empirical result that future work can build on. This was not revealed by prior episodic memory evaluations and is the paper's most interesting finding.

- **Contamination-free synthetic generation with controlled difficulty (Section 4.1):** The geometric sampling strategy to control cue overload levels (0, 1, 2, 3–5, 6+ matching events) is a clever design choice enabling controlled evaluation. The automatic quality control pipeline (uniqueness constraints, meta-data adherence, LLM-based verification) makes the benchmark scalable.

- **Multiple memory strategy evaluation (Section 5):** Testing in-context, RAG, and fine-tuning on the same benchmark provides a more complete picture than evaluating only one strategy. The finding that RAG generally outperforms in-context for most models (Figure 3), yet still achieves modest F1 on multi-event questions (Table 3), shows the problem goes beyond context window limitations.

- **Confabulation evaluation via 0-match queries (Section 3.2, Table 3):** Testing models on questions with zero matching events to assess familiarity awareness is a useful addition most benchmarks lack.

- **Open-source release of code, datasets, and generator (Section 1):** Enables reproducibility and extension, including 11 datasets and a demonstrated ability to scale to 1M+ tokens (footnote 3).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Terminology overclaim: "episodic memory" framing inflates what the benchmark actually measures.** The primary in-context paradigm places the entire document in the context window and asks structured questions about it—this is reading comprehension on synthetic documents with event-structured content, not episodic memory as defined in the cognitive science literature the paper draws from. The paper borrows extensively from hippocampal neuroscience (place cells, grid cells, encoding specificity) but never establishes that its tasks require the "encoding, consolidation, and retrieval" processes that define episodic memory. The RAG and fine-tuning experiments are closer to testing memory, but even RAG reduces to retrieval + reading comprehension. The headline conclusion that "even the most advanced LLMs struggle with episodic memory tasks" overstates the evidence—they struggle with structured event-tracking QA on synthetic text, which is a real finding but not the same as "episodic memory" as cognitively defined. The benchmark itself is useful regardless of this framing; the overclaim is the issue.

- **Fine-tuning experiment is configured as a strawman (Section 5.1, Table 3).** Fine-tuning is performed only on "all single-event question-answer pairs" (line 207) and then tested on multi-event questions. The unsurprising result—that single-event-trained models fail on multi-event questions—is used to conclude that "naive finetuning fails to achieve a deep understanding of episodic events" (Section 6). This conclusion is not supported: the experiment tests generalization from one question type to another, not whether fine-tuning on a more representative distribution could work. A fairer test would include multi-event QA pairs in training. As presented, the experiment cannot distinguish between "fine-tuning fundamentally cannot embed episodic knowledge" and "the training distribution was mismatched to the test distribution."

- **o1-mini's anomalous response pattern is not investigated (Table 3).** o1-mini achieves 0.97 F1 on 0-match queries (best confabulation avoidance) but 0.05 F1 on 1-match queries (worst recall)—an extraordinary gap that strongly suggests the model is refusing to provide substantive answers or defaulting to uncertainty, which coincidentally prevents hallucination but also prevents any correct recall. The paper reports both numbers at face value without analysis. This matters because: (a) the "best confabulation avoidance" claim for o1-mini is arguably misleading—it may reflect response refusal rather than genuine familiarity awareness; (b) without understanding the response pattern, the model ranking (Figure 3) is unreliable for this model; (c) it raises questions about whether other models' confabulation scores similarly reflect response reluctance. A simple analysis of response length or refusal rate would resolve this.

### Minor

- **LLM-as-judge evaluation without human validation baseline (Section 4.3).** The F1-score depends on an LLM evaluator extracting items and scoring relevance against ground truth. The paper claims this is "simple semantic comparison, not subjective assessment," but extracting items from free-form text and scoring partial matches or paraphrases is inherently subjective. No human annotation baseline validates the LLM judge's accuracy. Without this, systematic bias in the evaluator cannot be ruled out.

- **Cue-type difficulty gradient receives no explanation (Figure 4).** This is the paper's most novel empirical finding, yet it is presented without hypothesis or analysis. Why do models systematically struggle more with temporal cues? Is it the representation (dates vs. names), the distribution of temporal information, or something else? Ablating the temporal representation (e.g., using "Day 1, Day 2" instead of "March 23, 2024") would help isolate the cause and significantly strengthen the paper.

- **No human performance baseline.** Claims like "LLMs struggle" are uncalibrated without knowing how humans perform. If humans also find temporal cues harder, the difficulty gradient may be a general property of the task rather than an LLM-specific failure.

- **Event independence across chapters limits ecological validity (acknowledged in Section 6).** Real episodic memory involves connected, causal narratives. The independent-chapter design may substantially underestimate the difficulty of genuine episodic reasoning.

- **Claude 3.5 Sonnet appears in the ranking (Figure 3) despite a known bug affecting its in-context results (footnote 6).** The paper notes the bug but does not flag the model's ranking position, which could mislead readers about its true performance.

## Novel Insights

The cue-type difficulty gradient (content > entity > space > time) is genuinely novel and not anticipated by prior episodic memory benchmarks. If this pattern reflects something fundamental about how LLMs encode different attribute types in text (e.g., temporal markers being less discriminable than content descriptions), it could inform how future training data or retrieval systems should weight different event attributes. The o1-mini anomaly (extreme confabulation avoidance paired with near-zero recall) may be an early signal that reasoning-oriented models trade recall for conservatism, a pattern worth monitoring as more such models are deployed.

## Suggestions

- Rename or soften the "episodic memory" framing in the title and abstract—e.g., "Episodic Memory-Inspired Evaluation" or "Structured Event Recall Benchmark"—to avoid the conflation with cognitive episodic memory while preserving the motivational connection.
- Add at least a small human baseline (even 10–20 participants on a subset) to calibrate the difficulty claims.
- Investigate the o1-mini response pattern by reporting average response lengths and refusal indicators across match conditions.
- Retrain the fine-tuned model on a mix of single- and multi-event QA to make the fine-tuning conclusion defensible, or explicitly qualify the conclusion as only applying to single-event training.

## Evaluation

**Originality:** The systematic cue composition framework and contamination-free synthetic generation are novel. The cognitive science grounding is interesting but partially appropriated rather than operationalized. Moderate-to-good originality.

**Importance of research question:** Understanding LLM limitations on structured event tracking and temporal reasoning is important and timely. The benchmark fills a real gap.

**Claims well supported:** The core finding (models struggle with multi-event recall, temporal cues are hardest) is well supported by evidence. The "episodic memory" claim and the fine-tuning conclusion are overclaimed relative to the evidence.

**Soundness of experiments:** The main in-context and RAG experiments are solid. The fine-tuning experiment has a design flaw, and the o1-mini anomaly is unexplained. The LLM-as-judge lacks human validation.

**Clarity:** The paper is generally well-written with clear structure, though the cognitive science framing creates some conceptual overhead that isn't fully delivered on.

**Value to community:** The open-sourced benchmark and generator are likely to be useful. The cue-type gradient finding is a valuable empirical contribution. However, the overclaim means researchers should interpret the framing carefully.
