## Summary

This paper introduces a framework for modeling and evaluating episodic memory capabilities in LLMs. The authors represent episodic events as tuples $(t, s, ent, c)$, systematically enumerate all cue compositions (36 question types), and generate two contamination-free synthetic narrative benchmarks (short: ~10k tokens, long: ~100k tokens) using controlled ground-truth placement. State-of-the-art models including GPT-4o, Claude variants, Llama 3.1 405B, and o1-mini are evaluated under in-context, RAG, and fine-tuning memory strategies, revealing consistent failure on multi-event and spatio-temporal queries.

---

## Strengths

- **Principled (t,s,ent,c) cue-composition framework (Table 1, Tab. 10)**: Systematically enumerates all 36 combinations of time, space, entity, and content cues, including wildcards. This goes well beyond existing needle-in-a-haystack benchmarks by varying cue dimensionality and specificity in a principled manner grounded in Tulving's encoding specificity principle.

- **Controlled ground-truth placement (Section 4.1, Figure 2)**: The use of event meta-data to specify exact paragraph positions for key information is a concrete methodological contribution that makes the benchmark's questions uniquely verifiable — something impossible with real-text benchmarks.

- **Cue-overload finding (Table 3)**: GPT-4o drops from F1=0.81 at 1 event to ≤0.60 at 2+ events; fine-tuned GPT-4o-mini drops from 0.83 to 0.19. This is a specific, quantified, and interpretable result.

- **Cue-type performance gradient (Figure 4)**: The consistent ordering content > entity > space > time across all models and event-count levels is a specific and generalizable finding that adds diagnostic value.

- **Statistical rigor (Figure 3)**: Use of the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm correction and a Critical Difference plot is appropriate and correctly identifies which performance differences are and are not significant.

- **Confabulation testing design (Section 4.2)**: Including 150 zero-matching-event questions as a hallucination probe is a meaningful addition not present in most long-context benchmarks.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None.*

### Major

- **Generator-evaluee identity overlap (Table 2, Section 5.2)**: Both the short and long books are explicitly stated to be "produced with Claude 3.5 Sonnet 2024-06-20" (Table 2 caption), yet Claude 3.5 Sonnet is also a primary evaluated model in all main results (Tables 3, 4, Figure 3). The "contamination-free" claim addresses training-data leakage but does not cover this direction of circularity: a model evaluated on text it generated has a systematic stylistic advantage, especially in RAG conditions where it retrieves and reasons over chunks of its own prose. No ablation using an out-of-set generator (e.g., Llama 3.1-only generation) is provided to characterize or bound this effect. The Claude 3.5 Sonnet RAG results — which appear at the top of Figure 3 — cannot be fairly interpreted without this control. This is further compounded by footnote 6, which acknowledges that "Claude 3.5 Sonnet with in-context memory is knowingly affected by a bug," yet the buggy results remain in the main results without annotation in Figure 3.

- **o1-mini confabulation metric conflates refusal with calibrated uncertainty (Table 3)**: o1-mini achieves F1=0.97 on 0-event questions but F1=0.05 on 1-event queries and near-zero on all positive-recall categories. This score pattern is fully consistent with a systematic near-universal refusal strategy: a model that declines to answer almost everything will trivially score well on 0-event queries (returning empty answers that match empty ground truth) while failing on all positive-recall queries. The paper concludes "o1-mini performs the best" at confabulation avoidance without investigating whether these scores reflect genuine uncertainty detection or simply blanket refusal. The F1 metric as applied cannot distinguish these two behaviors. This invalidates the o1-mini confabulation finding as stated and calls into question whether the 0-event metric reliably captures its intended construct for any model.

### Minor

- **LLM judge not identified in the main text (Section 4.3)**: The evaluation's primary metric is derived from an LLM-as-judge, but the identity of the judge is deferred to Appendix B.3, which is stripped from the parsed version. There is also no inter-annotator agreement experiment with human raters reported in the main paper. For a benchmark paper whose central contribution is the evaluation framework, this is a gap in the main text.

- **RAG baseline is handicapped by design without disclosure in the main results (Section 5.1, footnote 4)**: The main RAG results use paragraph-level chunking, and footnote 4 explicitly acknowledges this is suboptimal and that chapter-level RAG is "an ideal upper bound" relegated to Appendix E.2. The main results discussion presents these paragraph-level results as though they are the representative RAG findings, which skews the apparent in-context vs. RAG comparison in favor of in-context. This should be stated prominently in Section 5.2.

- **Kendall's τ computed on very small, non-representative subsets (Table 4)**: τ is computed only on answers that exactly match the full ground truth ("All" row), which ranges from 0–18% of questions. For several models (llama, o1-mini, fine-tuned GPT-4o-mini) τ is undefined ("n.a."). No sample sizes or confidence intervals accompany the reported τ values, making the reliability of this analysis unclear.

- **Fine-tuning claim is somewhat overclaimed (Section 5.2, Section 6)**: Section 6 concludes that "naive fine-tuning fails to achieve deep understanding of episodic events," but the fine-tuning setup (Section 5.1) trains exclusively on single-event QA pairs. Multi-event failure is therefore at least partially a training-set design consequence rather than a general limitation of fine-tuning methodology. The paper notes "In principle, this approach enables models to acquire knowledge for all benchmark questions," but does not test the complementary condition of training on the full QA distribution. The claim should be scoped accordingly.

- **"Episodic memory" framing partially overstates what is tested**: The full narrative book is present in the model's context window during all evaluations. No evaluation tests memory *formation* or *retrieval* across sessions, and no architecture with persistent external memory is evaluated. What is measured is structured long-context reading comprehension with temporal/spatial query variation. While this is a valid and useful benchmark contribution, the cognitive-science "episodic memory" framing should be more carefully scoped in the paper.

### Trivial

- The cue type gradient described in Section 5.2 (Figure 4) reports "from top to bottom context, entity, space, time" but the paragraph also says "performance degrades from context, to space, to time," omitting entity — minor inconsistency in the prose description.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Add a rate-of-refusal measurement for o1-mini across question categories to separate genuine uncertainty calibration from blanket refusal.
- Fine-tune GPT-4o-mini on the full QA distribution (not just single-event pairs) to test whether the multi-event failure persists under a fair training regime.
- Provide inter-annotator agreement statistics (LLM judge vs. human) on a sample of questions in the main paper.
- Expand the benchmark to additional generated universes or multiple books per setting to improve statistical reliability of comparisons.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation across both reviewers is the *refusal-vs-uncertainty conflation problem* in the confabulation metric: for any benchmark that uses F1 on zero-answer queries to measure "abstaining behavior," a model that systematically refuses to answer will achieve perfect scores on that sub-metric while failing catastrophically on all positive-recall queries. This conflation is not unique to this paper — it is a general flaw in how confabulation avoidance is evaluated in long-context benchmarks — and the o1-mini pattern in Table 3 provides a striking empirical illustration. Future work should decompose the 0-event metric into refusal-rate and true-negative-rate components to make confabulation evaluation interpretable.

## Suggestions

1. **Add an out-of-set generation control**: Regenerate the two books using only Llama 3.1 (not evaluated) and rerun all model evaluations. Report the difference in Claude 3.5 Sonnet F1 between the two conditions. This directly tests whether the generator-evaluee overlap inflates Claude's results.
2. **Fix the o1-mini confabulation interpretation**: Measure the empirical refusal rate for o1-mini (fraction of empty-response answers) across all question categories and report it alongside F1. If refusal rate is ≥ 90% on 1-event questions, the 0.97 confabulation F1 is an artifact.
3. **Clarify fine-tuning scope**: Either add a full-distribution fine-tuning condition or explicitly restate the Section 6 conclusion as "fine-tuning on single-event QA pairs does not generalize to multi-event queries," rather than making a claim about fine-tuning methodology in general.
4. **Name the LLM judge in the main text** and provide a brief human-correlation validation (even 50–100 examples) to anchor the primary metric.
