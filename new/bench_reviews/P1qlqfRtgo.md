This paper compares three neural network architectures—a plain MLP, a "DeepONet-inspired" model, and a "U-Net-style" residual network—for data-driven approximation of thermal explosion dynamics in hydrogen–oxygen–air mixtures using a reduced 11-species kinetic mechanism. Using a dataset of 70,000 samples generated by a stiff ODE solver, the authors find that the U-Net model achieves substantially lower MSE (1.37×10⁻³) than the DeepONet (1.81×10⁻²) and MLP (2.03×10⁻²), concluding that architecture choice is critical for combustion kinetics surrogates and that U-Net-style designs outperform operator-learning approaches like DeepONet.

## Strengths

- **Practically important problem domain**: Accelerating stiff chemical kinetics solvers via neural surrogates is a real and significant bottleneck in combustion CFD. The 11-species hydrogen–air mechanism and the wide parameter ranges (T∈[250,5000]K, p∈[10⁴,2×10⁷]Pa) cover practically relevant combustion regimes and go beyond the simplified settings common in operator-learning papers.
- **Controlled experimental comparison**: All three architectures are trained on the same dataset with identical optimizer settings (Adam, lr=1e-3, batch 5000, 100 epochs) and evaluated on the same test set. The inclusion of 95% confidence intervals is a meaningful statistical contribution.
- **Honest acknowledgment of limitations**: The paper explicitly states that "neural networks are not always able to accurately approximate the various modes of the combustion process" and that "the problem remains unresolved," avoiding overclaiming universal success.
- **Multi-step loss formulation**: The use of a 30-step recursive prediction loss with 1/k weighting is an appropriate design choice for time-evolution surrogates, penalizing error accumulation in a physically meaningful way.

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **Misleading DeepONet "comparison" undermines the central narrative**: The paper's core claim is that architecture matters and that U-Net surpasses DeepONet-style operator learning for stiff combustion dynamics. However, the "DeepONet-style" model (Section 4.3) is not actually a DeepONet in any recognizable sense: it splits a 13D input into a 12D state vector and a scalar dt, processes them through separate small MLPs, and fuses via a bilinear product. This is a structured MLP with bilinear interaction—*not* an operator-learning architecture that maps input functions to output functions, which is what DeepONet is designed for. The introduction (Section 1) explicitly sets up the question of whether "operator-learning architectures such as DeepONet provide superior accuracy and robustness compared to conventional hierarchical models," yet the experiment never evaluates this question because the baseline is not an operator-learning setting. The conclusion that U-Net outperforms DeepONet for realistic combustion is therefore **not supported by the experiment** — only the weaker claim that a specific bilinear MLP underperforms a residual MLP is established.

- **"U-Net-style" is a misnomer**: What Section 4.2 describes is a dense MLP with local and global additive skip connections. The canonical U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) features an encoder–decoder with progressive spatial downsampling/upsampling and channel-wise concatenation of skip features. The paper's architecture has no downsampling, no upsampling, and no spatial structure whatsoever. The performance improvement is attributable to **residual/skip connections**, not U-Net design. This misattribution weakens the paper's architectural interpretation.

- **Insufficient experimental controls for architectural comparison**: No parameter counts are reported for any model. From the layer descriptions, the three architectures almost certainly have different numbers of trainable parameters. Only one configuration per architecture is tested, with one random seed, using a single set of hyperparameters. The claim that "network architecture is an important factor" is plausible, but without capacity matching (e.g., scaling MLP width to match U-Net parameters), ablation studies, or multi-seed experiments, it cannot be disentangled from capacity effects or training variability. The large standard deviations (U-Net STD 0.022 vs. mean MSE 0.0014) also raise concerns about stability.

- **What the neural network learns is underspecified**: Eq. (1) defines ∂X/∂t = f(p,T,X), but the dataset vectors are (Δt, T, C₁…C₁₁). It is never clearly stated whether the network predicts next-state, state increment, or the ODE RHS f. The multi-step prediction loss (Eq. 4) involves "recursively forecasting," but the recursive composition rule is never defined. Pressure p appears in Eq. (1) but is absent from both input and output. This ambiguity makes it impossible to evaluate whether the models solve the same problem correctly, and undermines interpretability of the reported MSEs.

### Minor:

- **No physics-relevant evaluation metrics**: Only aggregate MSE is reported. For combustion applications, errors in ignition delay time, peak temperature, and species conservation are far more informative than a single normalized MSE value. A model with low MSE could still mispredict ignition timing—arguably the most physically critical quantity.
- **No computational speedup data**: The paper's stated motivation is reducing the "about 90 percent" computational cost of solving the ODE, yet no inference time, parameter count, or FLOP comparison is provided. Practical applicability remains unquantified.
- **Unexplored generalization**: All train/test data come from the same i.i.d. sampling of initial conditions. No out-of-distribution or long-horizon rollout evaluation is conducted. The broad claims about "combustion and reactive-flow applications" are thus only demonstrated within a narrow in-distribution regime.
- **Inconsistent species description**: Section 2 says 9 hydrogen-oxygen compounds plus N₂ and Ar (=11 species), but the number "11 reagents" and "11 chemical species" are used inconsistently, and it's unclear whether all 11 are dynamic or some are constant. The paper both says N₂ and Ar are constant (and copied from input) while listing them among the 11 predicted outputs.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most interesting finding is the large error spread (high STD relative to mean MSE) across all architectures including U-Net, even with 50,000 training samples. This suggests that certain combustion regimes—likely near ignition boundaries—remain fundamentally challenging for generic feedforward architectures, regardless of skip connections. This is an observation that deserves more analysis than the paper provides. Additionally, the DeepONet-style model performing comparably to the MLP (with overlapping CIs) despite its more complex bilinear structure is itself an informative null result, though the paper frames it as confirming U-Net superiority.
