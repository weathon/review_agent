## Summary
This paper presents a large-scale empirical study computing pairwise correlations among 11 machine translation (MT) evaluation metrics (7 morphological, 4 semantic) using Pearson, Kendall, and Spearman coefficients. The analysis spans 40 neural MT models across 20 typologically diverse language pairs aligned with Chinese. The core findings claim that morphological metrics are highly redundant, that semantic embedding similarity strongly correlates with morphology (leading to the assertion that "deep semantics is just another high-level morphology"), and that correlation strength varies systematically across languages, suggesting evaluation should be personalized.

## Strengths
- **Systematic cross-lingual scale:** The paper applies a uniform pipeline across 20 language pairs (bidirectional with Chinese) and reports full correlation matrices and heatmaps for each. This provides a broad empirical view of how surface-level and embedding-based metrics align across different scripts and language families.
- **Transparent reporting of metric redundancy:** The numerical results clearly demonstrate extreme internal consistency among certain metric families. For example, Table 3 shows Jaccard and Dice correlating at 0.9876, and BLEU and CHRF at 0.9858. This empirical confirmation that many traditional metrics yield nearly identical rankings is a useful, reproducible observation for practitioners.
- **Clear specification of the experimental pipeline:** The Multiloop Incremental Bootstrapping (MIB) framework and hyperparameters for the 40 NMT models are described in detail (Section 4.1), along with explicit formulas for the morphological and semantic similarity calculations. This level of implementation transparency facilitates exact replication of the reported correlations.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
// None. The paper presents a coherent, albeit limited, empirical correlation study.

### Major
- **Absence of human-grounded validation invalidates metric assessment claims.** The study exclusively computes correlations *between automatic metrics* applied to the same `(hypothesis, reference)` pairs. In MT evaluation, metric validity is established by correlating metric outputs with **human quality judgments** (e.g., MQM, Direct Assessment). By omitting human annotations, the paper only demonstrates that different similarity functions produce consistent rankings of its own custom MT outputs. It does not determine which metrics are correct or whether the observed correlations reflect true translation quality. This renders the abstract's claims about the "economy of knowledge representation" and metric equivalence scientifically ungrounded.
- **The headline claim "semantics is high-level morphology" is a methodological tautology.** Section 2.2 defines "semantic evaluation" as max-min normalized cosine similarity of raw Sentence-BERT embeddings. Modern semantic MT evaluation relies on supervised, human-aligned models (e.g., COMET, BLEURT, MetricX) optimized to predict translation quality, which incorporate source context and error-type sensitivity. Unsupervised sentence embeddings primarily capture distributional surface statistics, which inherently correlate with n-gram morphology. Therefore, the finding that Sentence-BERT cosine correlates strongly with BLEU/CHRF is an expected artifact of using distributional similarity as a proxy for semantics, not an empirical discovery that semantic evaluation is equivalent to morphology. The omission of contemporary supervised metrics from the correlation matrix prevents the paper from testing its own central hypothesis.
- **Language-tier analysis conflates MT system quality with intrinsic linguistic attributes.** Section 4.3 stratifies 20 languages into three correlation tiers and attributes the gradient to "inherent attributes of languages" and inherent morphological/semantic properties. However, the authors' custom MT system exhibits massive performance variance across these tiers: Table 2 reports BLEU scores ranging from 23.08 (ZhoLao) to 48.54 (EngZho). Correlation strength among automatic metrics is known to scale with translation quality (noisier translations yield lower inter-metric agreement). Furthermore, script/tokenization differences (Latin vs. Cyrillic vs. logographic) and how pre-trained encoders handle non-Latin scripts heavily influence both BLEU and embedding cosine. Attributing the correlation gradient solely to linguistic semantics while ignoring the confounding effects of the model's own quality variance and tokenization processing is a fundamental misinterpretation.

### Minor
- **Philosophical overreach in the conclusion.** The paper concludes by asking, "can we further guess that 'The semantics of language do not exist at all?'" This speculative metaphysics is inappropriate for an empirical systems paper and misrepresents the scope of the statistical observations. It conflates the mathematical similarity between distributional embeddings and n-gram overlap with the broader philosophical concept of linguistic semantics.
- **Excessive inclusion of textbook definitions.** Section 3 devotes significant space to deriving the formulas for Pearson, Kendall, and Spearman correlation coefficients from first principles. This material is standard statistical knowledge and inflates the methodology section without adding peer-reviewed value. Space would be better utilized discussing statistical significance testing or confidence intervals for the reported correlations, which are currently absent despite the large sample sizes.

## Recommendations for Improvement (Nice-to-Haves)
- **Include contemporary supervised baselines.** Adding COMET or BLEURT to the correlation matrix would meaningfully test whether the morphology-semantic equivalence holds for metrics trained on human judgments.
- **Report statistical significance.** While the sample sizes are large, reporting p-values or bootstrap confidence intervals for the correlation coefficients would add rigor and clarify whether the differences between language tiers are statistically significant or fall within noise margins.
- **Error-type stratified analysis.** Analyze how metric correlations shift under specific error categories (e.g., lexical substitution vs. omission) to test whether embedding metrics capture meaning preservation that surface metrics miss.

## Novel Insights
The paper raises an interesting empirical question about the redundancy of surface-level evaluation metrics in the era of neural translation. The observation that Jaccard/Dice and BLEU/CHRF are virtually interchangeable on modern MT outputs is a practical takeaway for benchmark designers who might otherwise run computationally expensive variants. However, the paper fails to deliver a genuinely novel insight about "semantics" because it equates semantic evaluation with naive vector similarity. The core finding—that embedding similarity correlates with morphology—is well-understood in the distributional semantics literature and does not survive the introduction of supervised, human-aligned metrics. The paper's true contribution is the scale of its negative result for unsupervised embedding metrics, but this is obscured by overclaiming and methodological gaps.

## Suggestions
- **Reframe the paper's scope.** Remove the philosophical claims about the non-existence of semantics and the "Turing computing system" rhetoric. Reposition the paper as a practical benchmarking study demonstrating that for unsupervised embedding metrics, high cross-lingual correlation with traditional morphology metrics is the norm, suggesting limited complementary diagnostic value for these specific unsupervised semantic proxies.
- **Add human-correlation analysis immediately.** Even if retrospective, computing the correlation of your 11 metrics against standard WMT/FLORES human scores (which are publicly available for many of these language pairs) would ground the study and prevent the "internal consistency only" critique.
- **Control for MT quality in the tier analysis.** Re-run the language-tier analysis after normalizing for the BLEU scores reported in Table 2, or explicitly state that the correlation gradient is partially driven by model performance variance rather than intrinsic language properties.
