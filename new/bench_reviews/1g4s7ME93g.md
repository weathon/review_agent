## Summary

S-RVT (Super Robot View Transformer) proposes a three-component framework — Super Point Renderer (S-PR), Super-resolution Multi-View Transformer (S-MVT), and Hierarchical Sampling Policy (HSP) — to improve the precision and robustness of virtual-view-based multi-task manipulation policies, building directly on RVT and RVT-2. The method achieves 87.8% average success over 18 RLBench tasks (up from 81.4% for RVT-2), with especially large gains on precision-demanding tasks like Insert Peg (40% → 86%) and Sort Shape (35% → 71.3%). Real-world validation on four tasks is included.

---

## Strengths

- **Substantial empirical gains on a well-established benchmark.** S-RVT2 improves average success by +6.4% over the prior SOTA (RVT-2) across 18 tasks, with ~2× improvement on Insert Peg and Sort Shape — tasks that have historically been a known bottleneck. The gains are large enough to be practically significant, not just statistical noise.

- **Coherent engineering package targeting an identified bottleneck.** The three modules are each motivated by concrete observable failures of RVT: catastrophic occlusion in rendering (→ S-PR), coarse heatmap resolution limiting positional accuracy (→ S-MVT), and memory constraints preventing dense 3D sampling (→ HSP). The design pipeline is internally consistent.

- **Broad ablation study (Table 2).** The ablation over S-PR, HSP, focal loss, uncertainty weighting, number of views, and super-resolution factor is one of the more comprehensive ablation tables in this line of work. Each component shows a measurable contribution.

- **Real-world deployment.** The paper trains a single model for four qualitatively different real-world tasks (from semantic understanding to high-precision plug insertion) using modest numbers of demonstrations, achieving 65% overall success — a reasonable result that demonstrates sim-to-real transferability.

- **Correct use of prior method (Kendall et al. 2018).** The uncertainty weighting loss (Eq. 3) is appropriately cited and is a principled approach for multi-task loss balancing.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None.* The core empirical contributions are real and sufficiently demonstrated.

### Major

- **Overloaded "epistemic/aleatoric uncertainty" framing that is not technically validated.** The paper's introduction, abstract, and method framing repeatedly attribute every component to reducing "epistemic" or "aleatoric" uncertainty (e.g., S-PR "mitigates aleatoric uncertainty," S-MVT and HSP "reduce epistemic uncertainty"). However: S-PR is color-space DBSCAN filtering; S-MVT is a super-resolution decoder; HSP is coarse-to-fine 3D sampling; Eq. (3) is a multi-task loss balancing scheme. None of these produce calibrated uncertainty outputs, uncertainty decompositions, or any quantitative measure of epistemic vs. aleatoric uncertainty. The conclusion itself quietly abandons this language in favor of the more honest: "focusing on occlusion problems and resolution constraints in the action space." The uncertainty framing adds no technical substance and may mislead readers. This is a presentation problem that weakens the scientific contribution narrative without invalidating the empirical results.

- **Ablation evidence does not isolate the claimed precision mechanism.** The paper's central claim is that S-RVT specifically advances *high-precision* manipulation. Yet Table 2 ablations report only *average success across 18 tasks*. Without per-task or precision-task-subset ablations, the reader cannot determine which components (S-PR? SR factor? HSP?) are actually responsible for the precision gains vs. general gains. This matters because rows 8–9 of Table 2 show that even 1× super-resolution (i.e., no upsampling, equivalent to baseline resolution) drops performance by only 0.9% in S-RVT2 — a surprisingly small effect if super-resolution is the critical precision-enabling mechanism. A breakdown by task type (precision vs. non-precision) is essential for the paper's central causal claim.

- **Real-world section lacks any baseline comparison.** Table 3 reports S-RVT in isolation. No comparison to RVT, RVT-2, or any other method is provided. With only 10 test episodes per task and no baseline, it is impossible to attribute the real-world performance to the proposed improvements rather than to the general capability of the underlying architecture. This is a significant omission given the paper emphasizes real-world effectiveness.

### Minor

- **S-RVT2 underperforms RVT-2 on select tasks.** Table 1 shows S-RVT2 regressing on Slide Block (84.0% vs. 92.0%) and nearly matching rather than improving Sweep to Dustpan (97.3% vs. 100.0%). The paper does not discuss these regressions or characterize the conditions under which the method hurts performance, which limits understanding of when the proposed improvements apply.

- **S-PR's DBSCAN filtering is under-specified and potentially brittle.** The paper states DBSCAN is used "in the color space to filter out occluding elements like the tabletop." No specification is given for the cluster parameters, what constitutes "task-irrelevant" geometry, or how the filtering behaves in scenes where the table or background is the task-relevant surface. The single visualization (ring-on-spoke task) does not cover cases where color-based separation could fail (e.g., same-colored objects and table). This heuristic may not generalize reliably to diverse real-world environments.

- **Inference/computational cost not reported.** S-RVT adds DBSCAN preprocessing, 4× super-resolution upsampling, and two-stage hierarchical 3D sampling. For a robotics system paper, reporting latency or inference FPS is important for assessing real-time viability, yet it is absent from the paper.

- **Training detail for rotation prediction head is ambiguous.** Section 3.3 states rotation/gripper are predicted by sampling features at "the projected 3D position of the predicted next key pose." It is not stated whether ground-truth or predicted translation is used during training. This conditional dependency is architecturally important and should be clarified in the main text.

### Trivial

- **Abstract's "nearly two-fold improvement" needs scoping.** The aggregate gain is 6.4%. The two-fold claim holds only for specific tasks (Insert Peg, Sort Shape). The abstract presents this appropriately in context, but could be made clearer that this refers to the precision-task subset rather than the overall metric.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Provide per-task ablations (or at minimum a precision-task subset) in Table 2 to substantiate the claim that SR factor and HSP specifically drive precision improvements.
- Include RVT-2 as a real-world baseline on the same 4 tasks to demonstrate that the gains observed in simulation transfer.
- Reframe the introduction around "precision-limited manipulation" rather than uncertainty reduction, as this more accurately describes the method and contributions.
- Consider supplementing or replacing color-space DBSCAN with depth-discontinuity or lightweight semantic segmentation for more robust occlusion handling.

## Removed Points

- **Act3D comparison resolution asymmetry (Neutral Reviewer).** The paper explicitly acknowledges (Section 4.2): "All baselines, S-RVT and S-RVT2 are trained and evaluated using 128×128 input images, whereas Act3D employs 256×256 images." Since Act3D uses *higher* resolution (favoring Act3D), but S-RVT2 still outperforms Act3D, the asymmetry actually makes the authors' result *stronger*, not weaker. Per the Hard Rules, unfair comparisons that asymmetrically favor the baseline cannot be held against the authors.

Demanding formal significance testing or confidence intervals for large-scale benchmark evaluation is not standard practice in this field's community. Moved to Nice-to-Have territory at best.

None beyond the paper's own contributions. ## Suggestions

1. **Per-task ablation table for precision tasks.** Split Table 2 into "all tasks" and "precision tasks" (e.g., Insert Peg, Sort Shape, Stack Cups, Place Cups). This is the single most impactful addition to substantiate the core claim.
2. **Clarify the S-MVT rotation prediction training protocol.** One sentence in Section 3.3 clarifying whether ground-truth or predicted translation is used during training is sufficient.
3. **Report inference FPS.** Even a single line reporting wall-clock inference time (e.g., "S-RVT2 runs at X fps on a single GPU") would address a natural reader concern for a real-time robotics system.
4. **Tone down the uncertainty narrative.** Replacing "mitigate epistemic/aleatoric uncertainty" language with concrete descriptions ("reduce positional prediction error due to resolution limits," "reduce information loss from self-occlusion") would make the paper more accurate and still compelling.
