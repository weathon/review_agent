## Summary
GUI-Spotlight introduces an iterative, tool-based visual grounding model for GUI agents that dynamically invokes crop, extract, and find-color tools to progressively narrow focus on screen elements. Trained with a three-stage pipeline (SFT warm-up from Qwen2.5-VL-72B trajectories, then RL with a modified GSPO objective), the 7B model achieves 52.8% on ScreenSpot-Pro with only 18.5K curated training samples, surpassing models trained on millions of examples.

## Strengths
- **Novel iterative tool-use framework**: The "spotlight" metaphor of progressively narrowing attention via coordinated tool calls is a natural and well-motivated approach to the important problem of fine-grained GUI grounding on high-resolution screens. The three distinct tools (crop, extract, find color) provide complementary mechanisms for coarse-to-fine localization.
- **Strong empirical results**: Surpassing V2P-7B (50.6%) and GTA-1-7B (50.1%) at the 7B scale on ScreenSpot-Pro, and achieving consistent improvements over base models (UI-TARS-1.5-7B: 38.7%→52.8%) across three benchmarks, demonstrates meaningful gains.
- **Practical training stability contribution**: The auxiliary cross-entropy loss J'(θ) on filtered positive samples to prevent format collapse during multi-turn RL is well-motivated and empirically validated (Figure 3 right panel showing stabilization), addressing a real and recurring challenge in agentic RL training.
- **Valuable negative results documentation**: Section 4 systematically evaluates multiple RL variants and reward designs, including findings that dense answer rewards underperform sparse ones and that certain GRPO modifications degrade performance. This transparency benefits the community.
- **Data efficiency**: Achieving competitive results with 18.5K samples vs. millions for some baselines is noteworthy, though the effective training signal is larger than this number suggests (see Weaknesses).

## Weaknesses

### Major:
- **Misleading data efficiency framing**: The paper prominently claims training with "only 18.5K samples," but the effective training investment is substantially larger. The base model UI-TARS-1.5-7B was itself pre-trained on large-scale GUI data, and Stage 1 relies on 2561 multi-turn trajectories generated by the much larger Qwen2.5-VL-72B. Additionally, the data cleaning pipeline uses Qwen2.5-VL-72B to define label correctness (IQ, BA, CON filters), effectively baking that large model's knowledge into the training signal. The 18.5K figure understates the total supervision, making the comparison with models trained on millions of human-annotated samples potentially misleading. The Qwen2.5-VL-7B variant's lower performance (38.7% vs 52.8%) underscores how much the method depends on a GUI-specialized backbone.

- **Insufficient attribution of gains to RL vs. SFT vs. iterative architecture**: The paper claims "substantive post-training gain" from RL, but the evidence is incomplete. Figure 2 shows accuracy progression across stages, but no table reports SFT-only performance on all three benchmarks. Section 5.4 compares the full model against untrained multi-turn and repeated single-turn baselines, but does not include a critical control: an SFT-only model (without RL). Without quantifying how much of the 52.8% comes from Qwen-72B-supervised tool imitation versus the GSPO RL fine-tuning, the narrative that RL is the primary driver cannot be fully verified.

- **No per-tool ablation or analysis of tool usage patterns**: The paper's core claim is multi-tool coordinated reasoning, yet it never removes individual tools or reports how often each is actually invoked at inference time. If the model predominantly uses one tool, the multi-tool coordination story is weakened. Similarly, the average number of tool invocations per query is not reported, making it impossible to assess the cost-accuracy tradeoff.

### Minor:
- **Large performance gap between base models**: The Qwen2.5-VL-7B initialization yields only 38.7% on ScreenSpot-Pro (vs. 52.8% for UI-TARS-1.5-7B), and 8.3% on UI-Vision (vs. 23.4%). While the paper claims the method "transfers beyond UI-specialized backbones," the gap is substantial enough that the generality claim should be tempered—this approach clearly works best when building on a GUI-specialized foundation.

- **Some overclaimed conclusions**: The abstract mentions "pointer-level actions such as clicking or dragging," but all evaluations are single-click localization. No experiments involve dragging or region selection. The claim that ~50% accuracy is "not practical" is asserted without downstream task-completion evidence.

- **Missing inference cost analysis**: Multi-step iterative inference has latency implications for real GUI agent deployment. No analysis of wall-clock time, FLOPs, or average turns per query is provided.

- **Absence of qualitative examples and failure analysis**: The paper lacks visualization of actual tool-use trajectories or systematic analysis of failure modes (e.g., does the model get stuck in crop loops? when does iterative refinement fail?). This makes it hard to understand the method's behavior patterns and failure modes.

### Trivial:
- The reward weights (0.30, 0.25, 0.05, 0.20, 0.20) are not thoroughly justified via sensitivity analysis, and center-shaped reward hyperparameters (bonusmax, γ) are not specified.

The data cleaning filters samples based on quality criteria (clarity, annotation accuracy, consistency)—this is a standard quality control practice, not "label leakage." The evaluation benchmarks (ScreenSpot-Pro, UI-Vision, OSWorld-G) are separate from the training data, so there is no direct leakage. The concern that Qwen2.5's biases may affect which samples are retained is legitimate but does not constitute "leakage." Intermediate rewards guide learning but the model must ultimately produce a precise click coordinate to receive r1. The concern would be more valid if the paper claimed superior IoU metrics, but the evaluation is point-in-box accuracy, which aligns with r1.

## Novel Insights
The finding that sparse binary answer rewards outperform center-shaped dense rewards for GUI grounding (Section 4.2) is counterintuitive and useful—it suggests that for this task, sharp binary feedback may provide clearer learning signals than smooth gradient-shaped rewards. The documentation that vanilla GRPO/GSPO collapses due to format drift in multi-tool settings, and that a filtered positive-sample cross-entropy auxiliary loss prevents this, is a practical insight that will save future practitioners significant effort.
