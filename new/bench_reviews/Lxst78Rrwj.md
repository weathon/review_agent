## Summary
This paper introduces **GLIDE**, a novel causal discovery framework that identifies causal parents by testing the distributional invariance of the effect-cause conditional across multiple data augmentations. By shifting the priors over source (basis) variables and measuring the resulting variance of $P(X|Z)$, GLIDE avoids the exponential complexity of constraint-based methods and the brittle heuristic scores of score-based approaches. Coupled with a graph-theoretic reduction that limits parent candidates to maximal cliques over Markov blankets, the method achieves $O(d^2)$ complexity. Empirical results demonstrate strong performance and superior scalability on both synthetic and large-scale real-world categorical datasets, notably running efficiently on the 1041-variable Munin graph where baselines time out.

## Strengths
- **Principled and novel invariance-based causal test:** By formalizing parent finding as a test of the variance of $P(X|Z)$ under simulated prior shifts (Theorem 1, Eq. 1-2), the paper provides a theoretically grounded alternative to the exhaustive conditional independence checks of PC-style algorithms or the opaque score functions of optimization-based methods. This directly targets the distributional stability inherent to true causal mechanisms.
- **Elegant and provably scalable candidate enumeration:** The reduction of the parent search space to maximal cliques in an augmented bidirectional graph over each node's Markov blanket (Theorem 7) is a clever application of graph-theoretic sparsity. The empirical verification of low graph degeneracy ($p \le 13$ across benchmarks) rigorously justifies bounding the candidate search to $O(d)$ per node and achieving the claimed $O(md^2)$ total complexity.
- **Strong empirical scalability and competitive accuracy:** The framework demonstrates clear practical value in large-scale settings. It achieves up to a $96.5\times$ speedup over NOTEARS/MLP-NOTEARS on 500-node synthetic graphs while maintaining the lowest spurious rates. On real-world data, it successfully processes the massive Munin dataset in ~6200 seconds with a 1.8% spurious rate, whereas PC exceeds the 48-hour timeout and GIES suffers a 42.4% spurious rate (Table 2).

## Weaknesses
### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Discrepancy between theoretical sources and practical basis set (Section 4.1, 4.2.1, Theorems 1 & 3):** Theorem 1 formally guarantees invariance when true exogenous sources (root nodes) are shifted. However, the algorithm identifies "true sources" is not possible without intervention, the algorithm substitutes them with a "basis set" constructed by a greedy pairwise independence procedure (Theorem 3). While Theorem 2 guarantees that a maximum basis set has the same cardinality as the true source set, and that basis variables share similar properties, the paper does not formally prove that shifting these *statistical* substitutes preserves the invariance property for true parents or breaks it for non-parents. Unless the basis perfectly perfectly aligns with the true causal roots (rarely guaranteed), the theoretical guarantee of Theorem 1 does not strictly carry over to the algorithm's actual resampling procedure. 

- **Confounding estimation variance with true causal shift (Eq. 2, Section 4.2.2-4.2.3):** The invariance test aggregates conditional distribution differences across augmented datasets $D_i$ (Eq. 2). By Theorem 4, the size of $D_i$ is determined by $\gamma_i = \min (P(\mathbf{B}=b)/P_i(\mathbf{B}=b))$, meaning datasets with larger prior shifts are more aggressively downsampled. Consequently, the estimated variance in Eq. 2 becomes a mixture of true causal mechanism shift and finite-sample estimation error (which scales inversely with $|D_i|$). The paper does not normalize the aggregation by sample size or incorporate a statistical significance threshold, leaving the test vulnerable to flagging genuinely invariant relationships simply because they were estimated on smaller, noisier subsets. 

### Minor
- **Lack of explicit minimality constraint in Eq. 3:** The optimization in Eq. 3 selects $Z$ that minimizes the variance, but it does not include a cardinality penalty or conditional independence pruning step. In a valid causal model, if $Z' \supset \text{Pa}[X]$ and the added variables are conditionally independent of $X$ given $\text{Pa}[X]$, then $P(X|Z') = P(X|\text{Pa}[X])$. Under finite-sample noise, ties could be broken arbitrarily, leading to non-minimal parent sets. While the clique search structure implicitly constrains this, the paper does not quantify how often this occurs or explicitly resolve it, leaving a gap in the mathematical claim of unique parent recovery.
- **Marginal resampling approximation (Section 4.2.4):** To avoid exponential costs in $|\mathbf{B}|$, the implementation resamples for each basis variable individually rather than jointly. Theorem 5 guarantees the joint distribution match only if the variables in $\mathbf{B}$ are perfectly independent, so this is an approximation. The paper frames this as an implementation detail but doesn't explicitly flag it as an approximation or evaluate the induced bias on test validity.
- **Evaluation metrics focus on SHD and spurious rate:** While SHD inherently includes false negatives (and thus relates to recall), separating precision and recall would provide a clearer picture of whether low spurious rates are achieved by correctly recovering graphs or by excessively pruning edges. Including F1 scores would strengthen the evaluation but is not standard in all causal discovery literature.

### Trivial
- **Discretization details for continuous data:** Section 4.2.2 mentions binning with fixed bin width for continuous data, but the specific bin widths or number of bins used in experiments are not detailed, which is a minor reproducibility gap.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Ablation on $\gamma_i$ distribution:** Provide a visualization of the distribution of $\gamma_i$ and resulting $|D_i|$ sizes for representative target variables. Overlaying these with estimated variance scores would directly address whether the test's behavior is driven by causal invariance or sample size.
- **Parent set cardinality analysis:** Report the distribution of inferred parent set cardinalities versus ground truth across benchmarks. Quantifying how frequently Eq. 3 selects supersets or undersets would validate the minimality and identifiability in practice.
- **Categorical baselines:** Benchmark against discrete/categorical variants of score-based methods (e.g., GES with BIC for discrete) on the real-world datasets to further validate broad applicability, though extending continuous methods to categorical domains is non-trivial and often unfair.

## Removed Points
- **Critic: "Causal Faithfulness and Causal Sufficiency assumptions are neither formally stated as prerequisites."**
  *Justification:* The paper explicitly mentions this in Section 3: "This is guaranteed under the causal sufficient assumption (Pearl et al., 2016) that there is no unobserved variables..." The paper does state it, though briefly. Removing as the paper does address it.
- **Critic: "Omitting recall or true positive rate makes it impossible to determine if low spurious rates are achieved by correctly recovering the graph or by excessively pruning edges."**
  *Justification:* SHD (Structural Hamming Distance) inherently accounts for both false positives and false negatives. A low SHD combined with low spurious rate indicates the graph is not being overly pruned. Demanding separate recall/precision is a nice-to-have for granular analysis, but the current metrics are sufficient for the core claim.
- **Critic: "Excluding NOTEARS, MLP-NOTEARS, SCORE, and DAS on categorical data as 'not applicable' unfairly narrows the competitive landscape."**
  *Justification:* These baselines are fundamentally designed for continuous data with Gaussian or non-linear parametric assumptions. Adapting them to categorical data requires significant non-standard modifications, and simply discretizing continuous methods often degrades their performance drastically. Excluding them from the categorical setting is standard practice, not an unfair comparison.
- **Critic: "Discretization method for the real-world datasets is not specified, hindering reproducibility."**
  *Justification:* The real-world datasets used (e.g., Sachs, Munin, Alarm from bnlearn) are inherently categorical/discrete. No discretization was applied. This is a misunderstanding by the reviewer.

## Novel Insights
The primary insight—framing causal parent identification as a test of conditional distribution invariance under simulated prior shifts rather than through constraint-based searches or heuristic score optimization—is theoretically elegant and practically motivated. While the core premise of invariance is well-established in causal discovery, the application of basis set substitution to make this test practical and scalable to high-dimensional graphs is a meaningful contribution, particularly in demonstrating how to avoid the factorial blowup of standard constraint-based methods.

## Suggestions
- Formally define or discuss the relationship between the statistical basis set and true causal sources, or explicitly state that the basis set serves as an empirical surrogate that approximates the theoretical guarantee under an assumption of sufficient faithfulness.
- Introduce a sample-size-weighted or variance-normalized version of Eq. 2, or derive a statistical significance threshold, to decouple true distributional shifts from finite-sample estimation noise.
- Explicitly address the non-uniqueness / superset issue in the parent-finding step, either by adding a minimality post-processing step, a cardinality penalty in Eq. 3, or by demonstrating empirically that the clique search structure inherently prevents supersets.
