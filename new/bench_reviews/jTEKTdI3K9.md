## Summary

This paper introduces AVHBench, the first comprehensive benchmark specifically designed to evaluate cross-modal hallucinations in audio-visual large language models. The benchmark comprises 5,302 QnA pairs across four tasks (Audio-driven Video Hallucination, Video-driven Audio Hallucination, Audio-Visual Matching, and Audio-Visual Captioning), built with a two-stage semi-automatic annotation pipeline (LLM disentanglement + rule-based QnA generation) with human verification. The evaluation reveals that existing audio-visual LLMs perform near chance level on joint multimodal inputs but improve substantially with unimodal or text-only inputs. The authors further demonstrate that simple LoRA fine-tuning with annotation-enriched data significantly improves robustness, with gains generalizing to external benchmarks (VAST, AVInstruction).

## Strengths

- **Fills a genuine evaluation gap**: Prior hallucination benchmarks (POPE, CIEM, NOPE, AMBER) assess single-modality hallucinations only. AVHBench is the first to explicitly evaluate whether audio signals cause visual hallucinations and vice versa (Sec. 3.1, Fig. 1-Right, Table 1), addressing a clear blind spot in multimodal evaluation.

- **Systematic multi-configuration evaluation**: The paper evaluates models across three input configurations—multimodal (Table 1), unimodal (Table 2), and text-only captions (Table 3)—providing triangulated evidence that cross-modal interactions, not inherent task difficulty, drive poor performance. For example, Video-LLaMA improves from 50.1% to 67.2% on A→V accuracy when switching from multimodal to caption input.

- **Training improvements generalize externally**: The LoRA fine-tuning combined with audio feature alignment boosts A→V accuracy from 50.1% to 83.9% (Table 4). Critically, Table 5 shows these gains transfer zero-shot to the VAST and AVInstruction benchmarks (e.g., VAST GAVIE-A from 4.04 to 5.09), validating that improvements are not mere benchmark overfitting.

- **Semi-automatic pipeline with human verification**: The two-stage pipeline using GPT-4 for disentanglement and rule-based QnA generation, verified by human annotators at each stage (Sec. 3.2, lines 154-156), offers a scalable and reproducible template for benchmark construction.

- **Balanced benchmark design**: The dataset contains exactly 2,651 "Yes" and 2,651 "No" answers (Fig. 2, Table 1 header), preventing trivial accuracy inflation from label imbalance—a deliberate design choice that strengthens evaluation validity.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

### Major

### Minor

- **Binary judgment metrics conflate answer bias with genuine hallucination** — Several models collapse into deterministic "Yes" responses (Video-LLaMA: 99.9% Yes on A→V, ImageBind-LLM: 99.3% Yes on V→A, per Table 1), yielding accuracy near 50% with inflated recall. The paper acknowledges this ("overconfidence… resulting in high recall values despite poor performance," Sec 4.2 Q1) and reports Yes(%) as a reference column, but the primary metrics (accuracy, F1) still mix perception failures with response bias. A calibration-aware metric (e.g., confidence thresholding, forced-choice alternatives, or Brier scores) would more cleanly isolate genuine cross-modal confusion from trivial answer defaults. This doesn't invalidate the benchmark but limits the discriminative power of Table 1 for models exhibiting extreme bias.

- **Unimodal ablation changes task scope rather than isolating cross-modal interference** — The comparison between multimodal (Table 1) and unimodal (Table 2) inputs shows improved performance when one modality is dropped, which the authors cite as evidence that "multimodal signals confuse the models' perception" (Sec 4.2 Q2). However, removing a modality eliminates the cross-component entirely, converting a cross-modal grounding task into a standard unimodal perception task. An ideal controlled experiment would hold both modalities present while varying only the hallucination trigger (e.g., same visual content with/without conflicting audio). The text-only caption experiment (Table 3) partially mitigates this by providing a third comparison point, but the full causal chain is not airtight.

- **Training data shares the same LLM-generated annotation source as test data** — The LoRA fine-tuning uses 87,624 QnAs generated by the exact same GPT-4 pipeline used for the test set, "without involving any human verification" (Sec 4.2, line 251). This raises the possibility of pattern-matching to annotation artifacts rather than learning genuine audio-visual alignment. The generalization to VAST and AVInstruction (Table 5) partially alleviates this concern, but a control experiment (e.g., fine-tuning on a human-verified subset versus the synthetic set) would more definitively isolate the source of improvements.

### Trivial

- **OneLLM baseline caveat** — The paper's footnote (line 181) discloses that the evaluated OneLLM version "is not jointly trained as a multimodal model," which may confound comparisons with properly trained AV-LLMs. While transparent, this should be more prominently discussed in the limitations section rather than only as a footnote.

- **Swapped audio-visual pairs test coarse scene mismatch more than fine-grained binding** — For the Audio-visual Matching task, randomly swapping audio between videos (Sec. 3.2) creates negative pairs that may be rejected by models based on coarse scene-level divergence (e.g., indoor vs. outdoor) rather than fine-grained object-sound mismatches. The paper does not distinguish between these failure modes in its analysis.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Adding error taxonomy breakdown (cross-modal mismatch vs. unimodal perception failure vs. answer bias) would provide more granular diagnostic value for the benchmark's users.
- Qualitative attention/activation visualizations for failure cases (e.g., showing whether models attend to the "correct" vs. "hallucinated" modality) would strengthen the interpretability of the results.
- Frame-level or spectrogram-segment verification for negative samples would further harden the ground-truth quality of the synthetic mismatched pairs.

## Removed Points

**Answer bias makes the central claim "statistically unsupported" (Harsh Critic, Point 1)** — The critic claims the benchmark "cannot distinguish between systematic answer bias, random guessing, and genuine cross-modal interference" and that the central claim is "statistically unsupported." This overstates the case: the paper reports Yes(%) alongside accuracy and explicitly notes degenerate behavior ("high recall values despite poor performance," Sec 4.2). A model that says "Yes" to everything IS failing at cross-modal grounding; the benchmark captures this failure, just not its precise mechanism. The criticism is partially valid but the severity is inflated beyond what the data supports.

**Unimodal ablation "invalidates the hypothesis" (Harsh Critic, Point 2)** — The critic claims the unimodal comparison "fundamentally alters the task definition, invalidating the hypothesis." This is too strong. Removing a modality does change the task, but the paper uses a triangulation strategy: multimodal (Table 1) → unimodal (Table 2) → text-only captions (Table 3), showing consistent improvement. While not a perfectly controlled experiment, this multi-pronged evidence supports the cross-modal confusion hypothesis. The paper's claim is "multimodal signals confuse the models' perception"—a reasonable interpretation of the evidence, not an overclaim.

**Ground truth pipeline systematically corrupts labels (Harsh Critic, Point 3)** — The critic claims that LLM-disentangled metadata "compromises the benchmark's validity as a hallucination detector" and that "systematic ground-truth errors will be misclassified as model hallucinations." This ignores the paper's human verification step: "we further employ human annotators at the end of each stage to verify the automatically generated outputs" (Sec 3.2). This is standard practice for LLM-assisted benchmarks and is explicitly addressed by the authors.

**PLLaVA/LTU missing from baseline (Harsh Critic, Section 4.1)** — The critic claims these models "are never listed in the primary baseline setup." They appear in Table 2 as unimodal comparators, which is precisely where they belong—PLLaVA is a video-only model and LTU is an audio-only model, so they cannot be evaluated on multimodal inputs. The criticism is factually incorrect.

## Suggestions

1. Add a calibration-aware metric (e.g., Brier score or confidence-thresholded analysis) complementing the existing accuracy/F1/Yes(%) columns in Table 1, to better isolate genuine perception failures from deterministic answer bias.

2. Include a controlled experiment where both modalities are preserved but the hallucination trigger is systematically varied (e.g., same video with original vs. swapped audio targeting specific objects), to more directly test the cross-modal interference hypothesis.

3. Report an ablation of the LoRA training data source (human-verified subset vs. fully synthetic) to definitively separate genuine audio-visual alignment improvements from pattern-matching to annotation style.

## Score and Decision

**Calibration references:**

- **High-scored benchmark paper (Accept Poster/Oral)**: TOMATO (6, 8, 8, 5) introduced principled benchmark design with three quantitative principles and strong human annotation. MMIE (8, 8, 8) was a large-scale benchmark (20K samples) with rigorous automated scoring. AVHBench is less principled in benchmark construction and smaller in scale than these, placing it below the 7+ tier.

- **Medium-scored borderline benchmarks (Accept Poster)**: MIA-Bench (6, 6, 6, 6) is a comparable benchmark with clear task definitions and systematic evaluation. AVHBench is similar in contribution quality—it introduces a novel evaluation area with rigorous enough methodology but with some methodological imperfections.

- **Low-scored benchmark (Reject)**: DataSciBench (1, 3, 6, 3, 3) suffered from severe ground truth quality issues (no human verification), poor presentation, and limited novelty. AVHBench is substantially above this benchmark: it has human verification, a genuinely novel topic (first cross-modal A-V hallucination benchmark), and generalization to external benchmarks.

AVHBench occupies the solid middle ground: a novel, timely contribution that introduces a new evaluation area with meaningful empirical findings, but with minor methodological imperfections (answer bias, imperfect unimodal evidence for the causal claim, shared annotation source for training) that prevent it from being outstanding. These are typical of early benchmark papers in a new domain and don't undermine the core value. I position this between the strong TOMATO benchmark and the acceptable-but-unexceptional MIA-Bench.
