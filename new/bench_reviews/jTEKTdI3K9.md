## Summary

AVHBench introduces the first benchmark specifically for evaluating cross-modal hallucinations in audio-visual LLMs, comprising 2,136 videos, 5,302 balanced Yes/No QnA pairs, and 1,106 captions across four tasks: Audio-driven Video Hallucination, Video-driven Audio Hallucination, Audio-visual Matching, and Audio-visual Captioning. The paper uses this benchmark to show that current audio-visual LLMs perform near random chance, improve when given unimodal inputs, and can be partially improved via audio feature alignment and LoRA fine-tuning on pipeline-generated training data.

## Strengths

1. **Important and timely problem definition.** Cross-modal hallucination—where audio cues cause models to perceive nonexistent visual events and vice versa—is a real, understudied failure mode of audio-visual LLMs. The paper's four-task decomposition (A→V, V→A, A-V matching, A-V captioning) operationalizes this concept in a structured way, going beyond single-modality benchmarks like POPE and CIEM (Section 3.1, Figure 1).

2. **Striking empirical finding: multimodal inputs hurt rather than help.** The comparison between Tables 1 and 2 shows that models like OneLLM drop from 76.5% accuracy on video hallucination (unimodal) to 53.7% on audio-driven video hallucination (multimodal), and the unimodal specialist PLLaVA achieves 81.5% on the same video task while all audio-visual LLMs are near 50%. This is a strong and memorable diagnostic result.

3. **Benchmark achievability is established.** The PLLaVA (81.5%) and LTU (67.2%) results on unimodal tasks (Table 2) demonstrate that the benchmark measures a genuine capability gap rather than being impossibly hard.

4. **Balanced evaluation design.** The exact 1:1 Yes/No balance across all judgment tasks (2,651 each, Figure 2) prevents models from gaming via response bias—a meaningful improvement over benchmarks with skewed answer distributions.

5. **Honest limitation discussion.** The paper explicitly acknowledges gaps in temporal reasoning, speech handling, dataset scale, and scene complexity (Section 5).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The causal attribution from the multimodal vs. unimodal comparison is undercontrolled.** The paper's central analytical claim is that "multimodal signals confuse the models' perception, i.e., evoking cross-modal driven hallucinations" (Section 4.2, Q2). The evidence is that models perform better on "Is {object} visible?" when given video-only vs. video+audio (Tables 1 vs. 2). However, this comparison confounds at least two factors: (a) genuine cross-modal interference (audio cues biasing visual judgment), and (b) increased input complexity/more tokens making the task harder for any model regardless of content. The paper provides no controls to disentangle these—e.g., video + irrelevant noise audio, or video + mismatched audio from a different domain. Without such controls, the specific attribution to *cross-modal content interference* (rather than general multimodal processing difficulty) is partially supported but overstated. The paper does cite prior work (Shon et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020; Senocak et al., 2023) observing similar phenomena, which provides some convergent evidence, but the causal mechanism remains under-specified. This matters because the paper frames cross-modal interference as its core contribution.

2. **The training improvement claim (Tables 4–5) has a format-overlap concern with limited generalization evidence.** The LoRA fine-tuning uses 87,624 QnA pairs generated by the same semi-automatic pipeline (same ChatGPT prompts, same rule-based QnA generation, same question templates) as the benchmark. While the source datasets' train/test splits are respected, the identical annotation methodology means the model may learn question format and answer patterns rather than genuinely learning to avoid cross-modal hallucinations. The dramatic A→V jump from 50.1% to 83.9% contrasts with the modest A-V Matching gain (50.0% → 55.6%), which is consistent with surface pattern learning. Table 5 provides generalization evidence on VAST and AVInstruction, but these are captioning/instruction tasks using different metrics and formats—not cross-modal hallucination evaluation on independent benchmarks. The paper's language ("simple training...improves robustness against cross-modal hallucinations") is stated more conclusively than the evidence warrants, though the directionality of improvement is encouraging.

### Minor

1. **The caption-input experiment (Table 3) does not cleanly identify "LLM capacity" as the bottleneck.** The paper states the instability "may be due to the LLMs' inability to perceive the multimodal signals effectively" (Section 4.2, Q3). Converting multimodal inputs to text captions offloads all perceptual work to specialized modality-specific models (LTU, PLLaVA) before the LLM receives any input. The improvement could equally reflect that dedicated encoders extract more structured information than the frozen/misaligned encoders in audio-visual LLMs—an alignment/encoding problem rather than an "LLM capacity" problem. The paper does use the word "hypothesize," which is appropriate, but the abstract and conclusion state this more strongly as established.

2. **The Audio-visual Matching task's "matching" definition is ambiguous.** The question "Are the context of video and audio matching?" (Section 3.1) does not clearly define what constitutes a match—same scene, shared objects, plausible co-occurrence? The paper creates negatives by swapping audio, but the boundary between matching and non-matching is not rigorously specified, which could penalize models for reasonable disagreements about what "matching" means.

3. **Several models produce degenerate outputs (always "Yes") that inflate F1 scores.** Video-LLaMA and ImageBind-LLM output "Yes" 99.9–100% of the time (Table 1), yielding F1 of 66.7. While the Yes% column makes this detectable, the F1 scores appear in the table without a flag, and an uninformed reader might mistake 66.7 F1 as meaningful. The paper briefly discusses this but could flag it more prominently.

4. **No quantitative characterisation of annotation pipeline quality.** The paper mentions human verification at each stage (Section 3.2) but provides no inter-annotator agreement, fraction of auto-generated annotations corrected by humans, or analysis of ChatGPT disentanglement errors in the main paper (details are in the appendix). This makes it hard to assess benchmark quality from the main text.

### Trivial

- The imbalance between A→V (1,136 pairs) and V→A (2,290 pairs) is unexplained in the main text, though it likely follows from the underlying source data distribution.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Control conditions for the multimodal vs. unimodal comparison (e.g., video + noise audio, video + irrelevant audio) to isolate cross-modal content interference from general input complexity effects.
- Cross-benchmark hallucination evaluation for the fine-tuned model to test generalization of anti-hallucination behavior.
- Per-category breakdown of hallucination errors to reveal whether certain cross-modal associations are systematically harder.
- Evaluation on more recent and capable audio-visual LLMs to verify the benchmark discriminates among stronger models.

Including it with this explicit disclosure is informative, not misleading—the asymmetric comparison actually reveals more about the benchmark. This is a scope judgement, not a factual error.

3. **Critic claim about METEOR/CIDEr fluctuations in Table 4 being "inconsistent."** Caption metrics can legitimately change non-monotonically when the model's outputs shift in content; this does not imply the judgment task improvements are invalid. The abstract does state it more strongly, which is a fair tension, but the body text is appropriately hedged.

## Novel Insights

The paper surfaces an important asymmetry: current audio-visual LLMs perform *worse* when given more information (multimodal vs. unimodal), which inverts the expected benefit of multimodal integration. The four-task decomposition (A→V, V→A, matching, captioning) is a useful diagnostic framework that could be adopted by future work, and the text-caption control experiment (Table 3) is a clever diagnostic for disentangling perception failures from reasoning failures—though it conflates encoding and alignment issues. The most valuable contribution is the benchmark itself, which gives the community a concrete tool for measuring a problem that was previously only discussed anecdotally.

## Suggestions

- Add at least one control condition (video + noise/irrelevant audio) to the multimodal vs. unimodal analysis to strengthen the causal attribution to cross-modal content interference specifically.
- Evaluate the fine-tuned Video-LLaMA on an independent cross-modal or unimodal hallucination benchmark to demonstrate that improvements generalize beyond format.
- Report basic annotation quality statistics (e.g., fraction of ChatGPT outputs corrected by humans, agreement rates) in the main text rather than relegating them to the appendix.
- Add per-category error analysis breaking down where cross-modal hallucinations are most severe.
