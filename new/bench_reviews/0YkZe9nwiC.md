## Summary

The paper proposes SIGnAL (Self-Informed Generative Active Learning), a framework that uses reinforcement learning (PPO) to optimize an LLM generator to produce informative synthetic data instances for active learning. An acquisition function combining KL-divergence-based informativeness with embedding-distance-based relevance serves dual purpose as both the selection criterion for annotation and the reward signal for generator optimization. Experiments on three text classification tasks with limited unlabeled pools (0.1% and 1% of original data) show consistent improvements over pool-based active learning baselines.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem formulation**: The paper clearly identifies two core limitations of pool-based AL (suboptimal data space coverage and dependence on large unlabeled pools) and two challenges in query-synthesizing AL (translating informativeness into learnable signals and preventing irrelevant OOD generation). The integration of RL to handle the delayed/dynamic nature of informativeness is a principled approach.

- **Elegant dual-purpose acquisition function**: The acquisition function (KL divergence divided by embedding distance) is intuitive, interpretable, and naturally serves as both the selection criterion and the RL reward—creating a coherent self-informed loop. Figure 1 effectively illustrates why relevance matters.

- **Honest and insightful analysis**: The paper candidly discusses failure modes including early-stage underperformance due to repetitive generation, label bias on QNLI (entailment-heavy generation), and the domain dependence of generator quality.

- **Consistent empirical improvements**: SIGnAL outperforms all five pool-based baselines across all six experimental settings, with larger gains on smaller dataset fractions (0.1%).

## Weaknesses

### Major:

- **Missing ablations isolate the claimed technical contributions**: The paper claims two technical contributions—(i) RL optimization of the generator and (ii) the combined informativeness-relevance acquisition function—but experiments report only a single "OUR METHOD" curve. Without testing variants such as (a) LLM generation without RL but with the same acquisition function, (b) the acquisition function applied to a mixed pool without RL-based generator optimization, or (c) informativeness-only (KL) or relevance-only denominators, it is impossible to determine whether the observed gains come from the proposed RL loop, the acquisition function, or simply from having more data generated by a powerful pretrained LLM. Given that Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is already highly capable of in-distribution text generation, it is plausible that most of the benefit stems from the LLM's pretrained knowledge rather than the RL-based optimization. This is the most critical gap: the core novelty (RL-feedback for generator optimization) is empirically unvalidated.

- **Structurally mismatched comparison with pool-based baselines**: SIGnAL can generate arbitrarily many new instances and draws from both real and synthetic pools, while all five baselines operate on a fixed pool of 0.1% or 1% of the original data. The comparison at "percentage of acquired data" does not equalize information content—by 200%, SIGnAL has access to a fundamentally larger data universe. The claim "SIGnAL consistently outperforms pool-based methods" conflates the benefit of *data generation capacity* with the benefit of the *active selection strategy*. The relevant comparison would include baselines that also use an LLM for generation (e.g., random LLM generation + same acquisition function), or at minimum the paper should explicitly scope its claims.

- **Synthetic labels from a model rather than a genuine oracle**: The synthetic data labels come from BERT models fine-tuned on the full respective datasets (91–94% accuracy), not from human annotators. This is significant because: (a) these "oracle" labels are noisy and systematically biased toward the annotator model's inductive biases, (b) the annotator and target model share the same architecture/training distribution, creating label-correlated errors, and (c) the paper's stated motivation is annotation cost in domains where human labels are expensive—yet the experiments substitute a different problem (labeling by a strong automated teacher). While the paper acknowledges this, the impact of label noise on the active learning loop is not analyzed, and the practical relevance to the human-annotation setting is unproven.

### Minor:

- **No query-synthesizing AL baselines**: The paper cites GAAL, BGADL, and ASAL as prior query-synthesizing methods but does not compare against any of them, stating they are "designed to handle image data." Since SIGnAL claims to be a general framework, at minimum a comparison against adapted generative baselines (e.g., simply generating random instances with the LLM + selection) would clarify the contribution of the RL component over simpler generation strategies.

- **Numerical stability of acquisition function**: The acquisition function divides by embedding distance d(Φ(x_i), Φ(x_j)). When neighbors are very close, this score can explode; when far, it vanishes. No discussion of clipping, normalization, or sensitivity to k is provided.

- **Computational cost not analyzed**: Each SIGnAL iteration involves PPO fine-tuning of a 7B-parameter LLM, generation of synthetic instances, KNN computation, and classifier retraining—substantially more expensive than pool-based baselines. No cost-accuracy tradeoff analysis is provided, which matters for practical deployment in the low-resource settings SIGnAL targets.

- **Early-stage underperformance**: The paper acknowledges SIGnAL underperforms pool-based methods in initial iterations because the generator produces repetitive instances. This is particularly problematic for the low-annotation-budget scenarios that motivate the work, but is only noted as motivation for future "adaptive budget allocation" rather than addressed or mitigated.

- **Limited evaluation scope**: Only three text classification datasets with 2–4 classes, one classifier architecture (BERT-Base), and one generator (Qwen2.5-7B). The claim of generality to other modalities (e.g., images) is purely conjectural.

### Trivial

- The problem definition (Section 3) formally defines pool-based AL with synthetic pool added, but the true optimization SIGnAL solves (over distributions induced by the generator) is not reflected in the formalism.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Comparison with simpler generative baselines**: Even without adapting image-domain methods, compare SIGnAL against "generate with frozen LLM + select with acquisition function" and "generate with frozen LLM + random selection" to isolate the RL contribution.
- **Ablation of acquisition function components**: Test KL-only, distance-only, and full acquisition function variants to validate the relevance denominator's necessity.
- **Annotation noise analysis**: Evaluate SIGnAL with varying levels of label noise in the oracle, or annotate a subset with human annotators.
- **Adaptive budget strategy**: Implement the suggested progressive shift from real to synthetic data, which could directly address the early-stage underperformance.
- **Evaluation on a truly data-scarce domain** (e.g., medical or legal text) where unlabeled data collection is genuinely difficult.

## Novel Insights

The paper reveals an interesting tension in generative active learning: when synthetic data is generated by an LLM pretrained on internet-scale corpora, much of the benefit likely comes from *distilling the LLM's prior knowledge* rather than from the active learning loop itself. The weaker results on QNLI (where the task distribution diverges from typical internet text) support this interpretation. This suggests that future work in this area should carefully decouple the contribution of generative model priors from the contribution of active selection/optimization—if the LLM already "knows" most of the data distribution, the active learning component may be doing less than expected. Additionally, the dual-role of the acquisition function as both selector and RL reward is a clean design pattern, but it creates a potential reward hacking risk where the classifier and reward signal co-adapt, which the paper does not address.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a "frozen LLM" ablation**: Run the full pipeline with the same acquisition function but *without* PPO optimization of the generator. This is the single most important experiment for validating the claimed RL contribution.
2. **Add "random generation + acquisition function" and "random generation + random selection" baselines** to isolate the contribution of each component.
3. **Report computational cost** (GPU hours, number of LLM calls per iteration) alongside accuracy to enable practical assessment.
4. **Scope the contribution claims**: Clearly separate the claim "generative AL outperforms pool-based AL in limited-pool settings" (supported but conflated) from "RL optimization of the generator improves data efficiency" (currently unsupported).
