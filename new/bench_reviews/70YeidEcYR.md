## Summary

The paper introduces MM‑R³, a three‑task benchmark to evaluate multimodal LLMs’ semantic consistency under question rephrasing, image restyling, and context masking, together with four metrics capturing accuracy and pairwise semantic similarity. It further proposes a lightweight adapter module, inserted between frozen vision–language encoders and LLM decoders, which is trained on MM‑R³‑style data and empirically increases the benchmark’s consistency metrics (and often accuracy) for BLIP‑2 and LLaVA 1.5M.

## Strengths

- **Well‑specified, reasonably substantial benchmark construction.**  
  The three tasks are clearly described with data sources and counts (Table 1, Sec. 3.2): rephrasing uses InfographicsVQA and OKVQA with GPT‑3.5 paraphrases tied to the same answer; restyling uses Google Landmarks v2 and Indoor Scene with four named style‑transfer models; context reasoning masks MSCOCO objects under explicit size and mask‑type constraints (Sec. 3.2, 149–150). This is enough for others to reconstruct and extend MM‑R³.

- **Explicit, multi‑metric evaluation protocol.**  
  The paper defines four metrics (Sec. 3.3): Acc (exact substring match, 161), semantic similarity to ground truth \(S_{GT}\) using Sentence‑BERT encodings (163), consistency accuracy Con via pairwise similarity threshold 0.7 (165), and consistency similarity \(S_C\) as average pairwise similarity (167). These are mechanically clear and consistently used across tasks (Tables 2–4, 6).

- **Broad and systematic empirical study.**  
  Nine MLLMs (six open, three closed) are evaluated on all tasks (Sec. 4.1, Tables 2–4), with additional analyses for model size (Table 5), image resolution (Fig. 2), and temperature (Fig. 3). The Sampling vs All distinction (Sec. 4.2, 194) appropriately separates stochastic sampling consistency from consistency across perturbations.

- **Adapter intervention is concrete and shows sizable gains on this benchmark.**  
  The adapter architecture and training are detailed (Sec. 5.1–5.2): a 2‑layer Bi‑LSTM plus max‑pool and MLP that generates a prefix over frozen encoder outputs (312–318), optimized with cross‑entropy on supervision generated by the same pipeline but with disjoint data (316–318). Table 6 shows nontrivial improvements in both consistency metrics and Acc, especially for context reasoning (e.g., LLaVA 1.5M Acc from 20.1→58.6, Con 25.9→62.0).

- **The study convincingly demonstrates decoupling between accuracy and consistency.**  
  Across Tables 2–4 and the narrative (Sec. 4.3.1–4.3.4), many examples show high‑Acc but low‑consistency models and vice versa (e.g., BLIP‑2 and LLaVA having 100% sampling Con but far lower All‑Con under paraphrasing and restyling), supporting the core qualitative claim that accuracy and consistency are distinct properties.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **“Semantic equivalence” of perturbed inputs is only weakly validated and not enforced in the benchmark.**  
  The benchmark assumes that each group of rephrasings/restylings/masks is semantically equivalent “by construction,” and all variants are treated as equally valid probes of consistency. However, the only quantitative validation is a forced‑choice human study on 100 pairs each for question rephrasing and restyling, with 92% and 86% semantic equivalence respectively (155). There is no described filtering of the 8–14% judged inequivalent; all such examples remain in MM‑R³. For image restyling, heavy style transfer at varying resolutions is explicitly shown to drop consistency (Sec. 4.3.3, 237), suggesting that some stylizations genuinely remove or distort information. For context reasoning, different mask types hide different evidence by design (149), so treating all masked variants as semantically equivalent queries to which an “ideal” model should respond identically is conceptually stronger than what is justified. This weakens the interpretation of measured “inconsistency”: some variation likely reflects genuine changes in available information, not just model unreliability.

- **Consistency metrics are decoupled from correctness, yet often interpreted as inherently desirable.**  
  Con and \(S_C\) are defined purely in terms of pairwise similarity of model outputs, independent of whether those outputs are correct (165–167). The paper acknowledges phenomena like Qwen‑VL‑Chat being consistently wrong in the example in Fig. 5 (204, 392–393), and Table 6 includes many cases where Con improves even when Acc is low or unchanged. However, much of the analysis language treats higher Con/\(S_C\) as unconditionally positive (e.g., Abstract: “absolute improvements of 5.7% and 12.5% … in terms of consistency” as meaningful for robustness, 23; Sec. 5.3: “ensuring a single consistent output,” 308–309). Without separating consistency on correct vs. incorrect groups (beyond an Appendix reference, 194) or penalizing consistent error, the metrics can reward pathological behavior such as mode collapse to a single wrong answer. This undercuts the paper’s framing that improving these consistency scores is directly advancing robustness and trustworthiness.

- **Open‑ended “accuracy” is based on substring match and likely unreliable for restyling and context reasoning.**  
  Accuracy is counted as 1 if the ground‑truth string is “encompassed within” the model response (161). For descriptive or abductive tasks—restyling (“Please describe the place in the image in two sentences.”, 82) and context reasoning (“What kind of object is in the masked region?”, 149)—many plausible answers will not lexically contain the single annotation (e.g., “classroom” vs “kindergarten”, or multiple reasonable guesses for an occluded object). The paper does introduce \(S_{GT}\) to capture semantic similarity (163), but still headlines and interprets Acc values extensively (e.g., “BLIP‑2 outperforms other models in accuracy” on restyling, 223; analyses of “Accuracy Masking” in Fig. 1). Without human scoring or a calibrated threshold on \(S_{GT}\), these binary Acc numbers for the open‑ended tasks are brittle and may not track actual task competence, weakening the strength of cross‑model comparisons and the claim that the adapter meaningfully improves “accuracy” in a substantive sense.

- **Adapter objective does not explicitly encode a consistency loss, so the mechanism behind consistency gains is under‑justified.**  
  The adapter training is described as standard supervised learning: the model is optimized with CrossEntropyLoss (318) on training data generated via the same pipeline as MM‑R³ (316), with only the adapter parameters updated and encoders/decoders frozen (312–318). Nowhere does the paper specify a loss that directly couples different variants of the same group (e.g., enforcing similar output distributions across rephrasings/restylings/masks). In the absence of such a term, consistency improvements could plausibly arise from generic effects (e.g., entropy reduction, stronger priors on frequent labels, or better single‑prompt accuracy) rather than true invariance to surface form. The paper briefly cites Newman et al. (2022) as inspiration (312, 318) but does not spell out how its objective differs or why it should specifically target consistency. There are no ablations (e.g., same adapter trained only on original, not perturbed data) or diagnostics (e.g., output diversity measures) to distinguish “consistency via invariance” from “consistency via collapse,” leaving the claimed mechanism largely speculative.

### Minor

- **Treatment and impact of non‑equivalent inputs is not analyzed.**  
  The human study finds that 8% of rephrases and 14% of restyled images are judged not semantically equivalent (155), but the main paper does not show how many of these appear in the test split, whether they were filtered for analysis, or how removing them would affect model rankings or consistency statistics. A simple sensitivity analysis (e.g., recomputing scores on the subset judged equivalent) would strengthen confidence that core conclusions are not driven by these noisy cases.

- **The semantic‑similarity threshold for Con is adopted from an STS benchmark without task‑specific validation.**  
  The 0.7 similarity cutoff is justified by reference to the STS benchmark (Cer et al., 2017, 165), but there is no calibration study on MM‑R³ outputs themselves (e.g., human‑rated pairs at multiple thresholds). This may be acceptable as a first approximation, but it weakens fine‑grained claims drawn from Con vs. \(S_C\), given that where 0.7 falls on the “same meaning / different meaning” continuum for these outputs is unknown.

- **Sampling vs All comparisons are partially confounded by decoding settings.**  
  The Sampling vs All design is conceptually sound (Sec. 4.2, 194), and Sec. 4.3.5 systematically varies temperature for open models (278–279). However, core tables report single settings per model (e.g., BLIP‑2 and LLaVA 1.5M at temperature 0, others apparently at their defaults), and for closed models the effective sampling stochasticity is opaque. Thus, interpreting cross‑model differences in All‑Con/\(S_C\) as reflecting only inherent sensitivity to perturbations (rather than a mix of inherent behavior and decoding randomness) is somewhat overstated.

- **Some claims about “fundamental requirement for reliability/robustness” overreach what is measured.**  
  The Abstract and Introduction describe consistency as a “fundamental prerequisite (necessary but not sufficient condition) for robustness and trust” (23, 35, 44). While consistency is undoubtedly relevant, the particular unconditioned metrics here and the limited notion of semantic equivalence mean MM‑R³ measures a specific consistency proxy, not robustness or trustworthiness directly. The paper partially acknowledges this (e.g., noting that humans can be consistent but wrong, 35), but the high‑level positioning could be toned down to focus on internal behavioral stability rather than broad trust claims.

### Trivial

- Occasional minor terminology inconsistencies (e.g., “Image Styling” vs “Image Restyling,” “Entropy” vs “temperature” in Sec. 4.3.5) could be cleaned up for clarity but do not affect substance.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Analyze consistency conditional on correctness vs. incorrectness in the main text (the Appendix A.5 reference, 194, is easy to miss), e.g., reporting Con/\(S_C\) on subsets where at least one variant is correct vs. where all are wrong, to clarify when consistency is beneficial.

- Provide a small human evaluation for the adapter’s impact, judging whether adapted outputs are more (a) correct and (b) semantically stable across perturbations, to verify that numerical consistency gains correspond to perceived robustness.

- Include a baseline where BLIP‑2 and LLaVA 1.5M are fine‑tuned on the same MM‑R³‑style data without the adapter, to show whether the adapter architecture itself adds value beyond exposure to more supervision.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.

- **Criticism that the paper ignores consistency‑conditioned‑on‑correctness analysis.**  
  The harsh review claimed such analysis was missing entirely, but the paper explicitly states “Additional analysis in terms of consistency for correct vs. incorrect answers is given in Appx A.5.” (194). While one can argue that this belongs in the main text, it is inaccurate to say the issue is wholly unaddressed.

- **Claims that the adapter training targets are undefined (e.g., unclear what labels are used).**  
  Sec. 5.2 clearly states that training data are generated with the MM‑R³ pipeline and optimized with CrossEntropyLoss (316–318), which reasonably implies supervision from the underlying dataset annotations. The real gap is the absence of an explicit cross‑variant consistency loss, not an absence of any loss specification.

- **Overly strong suggestion that differing mask types in context reasoning must yield different “correct” distributions and hence invalidate the task.**  
  The task is specifically framed as abductive (“What kind of object is in the masked region?”, 149) and the paper acknowledges multiple plausible answers. While this makes correctness and consistency more nuanced, it does not by itself invalidate the task; it mainly reinforces concerns already captured under metric design and interpretation.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper’s own contributions; the issues raised—semantic equivalence of perturbations, correctness‑insensitive consistency metrics, and disentangling entropy reduction from genuine invariance—are natural critiques of this kind of benchmark and do not add fundamentally new conceptual directions beyond what the paper itself and standard evaluation wisdom already suggest.

## Suggestions

- Tighten the notion of semantic equivalence: explicitly filter or down‑weight perturbations judged non‑equivalent by humans, and report sensitivity analyses showing how consistency metrics and rankings change when those are removed.

- Complement Con/\(S_C\) with correctness‑aware variants (e.g., consistency among correct outputs; or a metric that rewards consistency only when \(S_{GT}\) exceeds a threshold) and prioritize these in the narrative when making robustness/trust claims.

- For the adapter, add ablations that (i) train the same adapter only on original (unperturbed) examples; (ii) vary loss formulations to include explicit cross‑variant consistency terms; and (iii) measure diversity of outputs (e.g., type/token ratios, entropy) before and after adaptation to rule out trivial mode collapse.

- Reduce reliance on brittle Acc for open‑ended tasks by either (a) shifting emphasis to calibrated \(S_{GT}\) thresholds validated via human judgments, or (b) adding task‑specific human evaluation to confirm that reported “accuracy” gains reflect real improvements.

- More clearly separate what MM‑R³ measures (semantic stability under controlled perturbations) from broader notions of robustness, calibration, or trustworthiness, and frame claims accordingly.

**Overall evaluation:**  
Originality is solid (multimodal consistency under three tailored perturbations plus a concrete adapter). The research question—how consistent are MLLMs across semantic perturbations, and can we improve that—is important and underexplored. Claims about the existence and pervasiveness of inconsistency are well supported by experiments; claims linking raw consistency metrics directly to robustness and trust are overextended. Experiments are broad and competently executed but weakened by limited validation of semantic equivalence and brittle accuracy metrics for open‑ended tasks. Writing is clear and structured. The work offers real value as a benchmark and as evidence that consistency and accuracy diverge, but the conceptual issues, especially around what exactly the consistency metrics reward and how the adapter works, are substantial enough that I would not recommend acceptance in its current form.
