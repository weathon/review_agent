## Summary
ARIA-MIDI presents a scalable, multi-stage pipeline for collecting, filtering, and transcribing piano recordings from YouTube into a MIDI dataset of over 1 million files (~100k hours). The core technical contributions are a novel distillation-based audio classifier trained via MVSep-sourced pseudo-labels and an LLM-guided crawling and metadata extraction workflow. The dataset is orders of magnitude larger than existing AMT-derived piano datasets (GiantMIDI, ATEPP, PiJAMA), and the pipeline is thoroughly evaluated on audio segmentation, classification, and metadata extraction. The missing verification of transcription quality is a notable gap, partially offset by the dataset's scale, the rigorous audio-level evaluation, and the practical utility of per-file quality scores.

## Strengths
- **Scalable distillation-based audio classifier:** Training a lightweight CNN on pseudo-labels generated from MVSep source separation avoids expensive source-separation inference at scale. Table 4 and Table 5 demonstrate that the proposed classifier removes 98.83% of non-piano overlap (vs. 91.10% for the AudioSet-based ablation) with an 8-fold improvement in false-positive identification (Section 1.1), while inference of 100k hours took only 20 A100 hours (Footnote 4).
- **Orders-of-magnitude dataset scale with structured filtering:** Aria-MIDI provides 1,186,253 files (~100k hours) compared to GiantMIDI's 10,855 files (Table 1). The cascaded pipeline—LLM-scored crawling → audio pruning → transcription → metadata—filters out non-piano audio and artifacts *before* the expensive AMT stage, making the pipeline cost-effective and scalable.
- **Rigorous comparison to existing datasets using the trained classifier:** Figure 2 and Table 5 systematically analyze GiantMIDI, ATEPP, and PiJAMA files below the quality threshold, showing that a large fraction of prior datasets contains non-piano audio or artifacts. This provides valuable evidence for the necessity of stricter filtering and positions Aria-MIDI's quality controls in context.
- **Practical release of per-file classifier scores and compositional metadata:** Releasing classifier scores ($T$) alongside MIDI files lets downstream users set custom quality thresholds (e.g., Table 5 shows $T \geq 0.9$ retains only high-quality recordings). The compositional metadata (Table 6: 99.3% accuracy on composer) supports deduplicated subsets (~800k files, Section 4).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None

### Major
- **No evaluation of MIDI transcription quality:** The paper's core product is a dataset of MIDI files, yet there is zero assessment of the actual transcription output using standard AMT metrics (note-wise F1, onset F1, polyphonic error rate, hallucinated/missed notes). Section 3 thoroughly evaluates the audio classifier (Tables 4–5, Figure 2) and LLM metadata extraction (Table 6), but the AMT stage (Section 2.3) receives only a brief description with no human-annotated ground truth evaluation of its output. The paper acknowledges Aria-AMT's robustness to diverse timbres (Section 2.3, referencing Appendix A.3) but does not validate this claim for the corpus being released. For a dataset paper targeting symbolic music modeling, users cannot assess whether the MIDI output faithfully captures the musical content of the source recordings without this evidence. This omission leaves the claim that the dataset is "clean" and suitable for generative/symbolic tasks partially unsupported.

### Minor
- **Metadata accuracy validated against YouTube text rather than authoritative sources:** Table 6 reports ~99% accuracy for composer, performer, and opus extraction, but Section 3 ("Metadata extraction") states that evaluation was done by "cross-referenc[ing] the metadata labels assigned by the language model with the corresponding YouTube titles and descriptions." This measures the LLM's text-parsing fidelity, not musicological correctness. YouTube metadata frequently contains misattributed composers, cover/arrangement ambiguities, and clickbait titles. Because compositional deduplication in Section 4 relies entirely on these LLM-extracted tags (matching on composer + opus + piece), the deduplication pipeline's effectiveness is not independently guaranteed.
- **Under-specified MIDI post-processing details:** Section 2.3 describes the AMT model used and reports inference speed (131× real-time), but omits details on key MIDI post-processing choices that significantly affect downstream symbolic modeling: tick resolution, quantization thresholds, sustain pedal (CC64) handling, note merging/splitting rules, and tempo/time-signature extraction. These implementation choices can substantially alter how well the MIDI files work for various MIR or generative tasks (Section 1.1 notes intended uses in generative music and MIR).

### Trivial
- **Section 3 evaluation label:** The paper describes its segmentation analysis under "Audio Segmentation" (Section 3) but the subsection evaluates both segmentation and per-file classification. Relabeling as "Audio Pruning & Segmentation" would more accurately reflect the scope. (Minor presentation issue.)

## Nice-to-Haves
- **AMT failure mode analysis by genre/recording quality:** Quantifying rates of common transcription errors (hallucinated notes from pedal resonance, missed staccato, timing drift) across different recording conditions would help users understand which subsets of the dataset are best suited for specific tasks.
- **Ablation on $\lambda$ and $T$ thresholds' impact on dataset composition:** Table 5 shows how $T$ affects per-file classification, but an analysis of how more aggressive filtering alters composer/genre distributions or removes historically valuable but lower-fidelity recordings would aid dataset curation decisions.
- **Piano-roll visualizations:** Side-by-side visualizations of source audio, separated piano stem, and transcribed MIDI for both high-quality and edge-case examples would help illustrate what "clean" means in practice.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution:
- *"Structural: Misaligned Metadata Accuracy Protocol Undermines Deduplication"* — Valid concern but the paper's evaluation protocol (YouTube-based) is transparently described and achieves 99%+ accuracy. The deduplication strategy is reasonable given the constraints; this is a minor, not structural, limitation.
- *"Evidential: Unvalidated Pseudo-Labeling Fidelity"* — The paper uses a diverse pseudo-labeled corpus (Table 2: GiantMIDI, Score-4, Jazz Trio, etc.), applies augmentations to mitigate systematic artifacts (Section 2.2.1), and validates against a strong ablation. The approach is standard in distillation and not unreasonable.
- *"LLAMA-guided crawl discovery biases toward popular classical nodes"* — The paper explicitly addresses this in Section 2.1, noting genre drift as crawl progresses and that "piano recordings representing a diverse set of musical styles and genres were crawled." The bias is acknowledged.
- *"Missing reproducibility details for AMT output formatting"* — This was moved to Minor rather than removed; the paper does provide the Aria-AMT GitHub link (Footnote 5) and describes the model, but post-processing specifics are genuinely sparse.
- *Weaknesses about "unfair comparison" with other methods* — These were removed as the asymmetric evaluation favors the baseline comparison (the ablation performs worse on non-piano detection).

## Novel Insights
The most interesting aspect of this paper is not the dataset's raw scale but the methodological demonstration that distillation-based audio pre-screening can produce cleaner data at much lower computational cost than the brute-force source-separation approaches used by prior pipelines. By training a lightweight CNN to approximate MVSep's separation decisions, the authors achieve an 8× improvement in non-piano false-positive detection while reducing inference from an estimated 5,000 A100 hours to 20 hours. This suggests a generalizable pattern for dataset curation in audio and multimodal domains: using heavier models for offline labeling and distilling to efficient classifiers for corpus-scale filtering. Additionally, the paper's systematic evaluation of prior datasets through its own classifier (Figure 2) is a useful contribution, providing quantitative evidence of quality issues in widely-used MIDI corpora that many researchers have used without scrutiny.

## Suggestions
1. Add a human-annotated evaluation of AMT transcription quality on a random stratified subset (even 50–100 files) reporting note-wise F1, onset F1, and polyphonic error rate. This is the single most impactful addition to validate the dataset's symbolic quality.
2. Clarify MIDI post-processing in Section 2.3 or Appendix: specify tick resolution, quantization, pedal handling, and tempo extraction so users can understand how the MIDI files were produced.
3. For deduplication claims, validate a stratified sample of LLM-extracted metadata against an authoritative source (e.g., IMSLP) to demonstrate that composer/opus attribution is not only correctly parsed from YouTube text but also musicologically accurate.
4. Consider adding a confusion matrix or error analysis for the audio classifier's false negatives to characterize which types of legitimate solo-piano recordings are most likely discarded.

## Score and Decision
For calibration, I compared against several anchor papers:
- **High-scoring accepted dataset papers:** J2Jyp1SZ0n (MMSearch, 6,6,8,6, Accept Poster), kQCHCkNk7s (AstroCompress, 6,8,5,6, Accept Poster), and Pz9zFea4MQ (Robust-Ego3D, 8,6,6,6, Accept Poster) were dataset/benchmark papers with strong pipelines and comprehensive evaluation. MMSearch had a small dataset (300 instances) but strong analysis and was accepted.
- **Low-scoring rejected dataset papers:** aOPTDchLBz (ivrit.ai, 3,1,3,3, Reject) was a large-scale speech dataset criticized specifically for missing transcription quality evaluation (no WER on human-annotated data) — closely analogous to the missing AMT evaluation here. 5F0WDt9CjA (PercePiano, 3,3,6,3,6,3, Withdrawn) had insufficient evaluation detail.
- **Borderline papers:** 63r2sTjkCv (KinDEL, 6,5,5,6, Reject) was a dataset paper with borderline 5s and missing analyses.

This paper is significantly stronger than ivrit.ai (rejected, missing WER) because its audio pipeline is rigorously evaluated with well-designed experiments, ablations, and comparisons to prior work. The scale is impressive and the pipeline engineering sound. However, the complete absence of AMT transcription quality metrics — the paper's own primary data product — is a meaningful gap compared to MMSearch and AstroCompress, which evaluated their data benchmarks comprehensively on their end targets. The paper sits between the strong accepted anchors (which had complete end-to-end evaluation) and the rejected anchors (which had similar missing-evaluation issues). I place it at **6** — the overall quality of the audio evaluations, the dataset's practical utility with flexible quality scores, and the genuine contribution to the MIR community outweigh the missing AMT validation, which can be addressed in future work and does not invalidate the dataset's core utility.
