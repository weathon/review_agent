## Summary

This paper introduces **Co³Gesture**, a bilateral cooperative diffusion framework for generating concurrent co-speech 3D gestures for two-person conversations, along with **GES-Inter**, a large-scale dataset of over 7M frames with whole-body mesh, facial, phoneme, and text annotations. The method achieves strong quantitative results on the new benchmark and outperforms adapted single-speaker and text-to-motion baselines.

## Strengths

- **GES-Inter fills a clear gap in concurrent gesture data.** Table 1 shows GES-Inter is the only large-scale dataset providing concurrent two-speaker gestures with whole-body SMPL-X/FLAME mesh, facial, phoneme, and text annotations, distinguishing it from prior single-speaker datasets (BEAT2, SHOW) and the smaller mocap-based TWH16.2.
- **Strong quantitative margins on the new benchmark.** Table 2 reports that Co³Gesture achieves an FGD of 0.769, outperforming the best competitor InterGen (1.012) by a large margin, alongside superior BC (0.692) and Diversity (72.824).
- **Bilateral cooperative diffusion is empirically justified.** Table 4 shows that removing the bilateral branches and generating two speakers holistically causes FGD to degrade dramatically from 0.769 to 1.669, supporting the key insight that asymmetric body dynamics benefit from separate cooperative generation branches.
- **Dual audio conditioning is validated.** Table 4 shows that removing mixed audio (FGD → 1.227) or audio separation (FGD → 1.180) both degrade performance, supporting the design choice to condition each branch on both separated speaker audio and mixed conversation signal.
- **Public release of data and code.** The abstract and project page note that the dataset and source code are publicly available.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **TIM is described as modeling gesture–gesture interaction, but its formulation does not use partner gestures.** The Abstract and Section 3.3 claim that the Temporal Interaction Module (TIM) "can effectively model the temporal association representation between two speakers' gesture sequences" and "between each current speaker movements and conversational counterparts." However, Eqs. 1–2 reveal that TIM cross-attends a speaker's own audio with that same speaker's motion embedding to produce $\mathbf{f}_{x_a,C_a}$, then blends it with $\mathbf{f}_{x_a,C_{mix}}$—a feature still belonging to the *same* speaker conditioned on mixed audio. The temporal correlation matrix $\mathbf{M}$ is computed between two variants of the *same* speaker's embedding. At no point does the partner's actual gesture sequence enter TIM. Because the paper's central technical claim is that TIM explicitly captures temporal dependencies between the two speakers' gestures, this disconnect between the claimed mechanism and the mathematical formulation undermines the description of the module's function and weakens confidence in the technical exposition. The mutual attention mechanism *does* exchange information between branches, but that is a separate component; TIM itself does not perform gesture–gesture interaction as claimed.
- **Foot-contact ablation is experimentally invalid.** Section 4.2 and Table 5 report that adding $\mathcal{L}_{foot}$ yields a large improvement (FGD drops from 1.082 to 0.769). However, the paper explicitly states that the model generates *only* upper-body joints and that "we complete the lower body joints as $T$ pose in forward kinematic function during calculate loss." Computing foot-contact loss on immobile, manually fixed feet is physically meaningless: the loss cannot propagate meaningful gradients to the upper-body parameters being optimized, and for seated talk-show speakers the very premise of foot-contact constraints is questionable. The fact that removing this loss causes a 0.313 FGD degradation—among the largest swings in any ablation—is suspicious and renders this ablation uninterpretable.

### Minor
- **Quantitative evaluation rests on unvalidated pseudo-ground-truth.** Sections 3.1–4.1 report metrics computed against a test set generated by off-the-shelf estimators (PyMAF-X, pyannote-audio, WhisperX). The paper provides no quantitative validation of pose-estimation accuracy, speaker-separation quality (e.g., DER/SDR), or alignment precision. While pseudo-labeling is common in this field, the absence of any anchoring validation (e.g., MPJPE on a small mocap subset, manual quality audit) means the metrics may partially reflect estimator bias rather than true gesture quality.
- **User study is underpowered and compares against ill-suited baselines.** With 15 volunteers and 2 samples per method (Section 4.3), the study is small and lacks reported statistical tests. Moreover, several compared baselines (TalkSHOW, ProbTalk, DiffSHEG, EMAGE) are single-speaker methods never designed for two-speaker output; their very low scores (~2.0) demonstrate only that naive adaptation fails, not that Co³Gesture achieves high absolute perceptual quality.
- **Novelty claim is slightly overstated.** The Abstract and Section 4.2 claim this is the "first to explore coherent concurrent co-speech gesture generation with conversational human audio," yet TWH16.2 (Lee et al., 2019) previously explored concurrent gestures (acknowledged in Table 1 and Section 2). The contribution is better framed as the first *large-scale, mesh-based, whole-body* concurrent dataset and the first modern deep-learning framework for this task, which is still meaningful but should be qualified.

### Trivial
- Confidence intervals are reported for Diversity but omitted for FGD and BC in Table 2.

## Novel Insights

The paper reveals a tension between architectural narration and mathematical specification that is worth surfacing: the TIM module is *described* as capturing gesture–gesture temporal association, yet its actual implementation uses mixed audio as an interaction proxy and never processes the partner's motion. This suggests that much of the empirical benefit of "interaction modeling" may come from (a) mixed-audio conditioning and (b) the mutual attention layers, rather than from the TIM mechanism as written. A corrected or clarified TIM formulation—or an ablation that truly isolates cross-gesture signal—would strengthen the technical contribution considerably.

## Suggestions

- Revise the TIM description to accurately reflect its mechanism: it blends a speaker's motion features conditioned on separated audio versus mixed audio, using mixed audio as an interaction proxy. If the intent is genuine gesture–gesture interaction, reformulate TIM to incorporate the partner's motion embedding and add an ablation isolating this cross-gesture signal.
- Remove or repair the foot-contact ablation. If the loss is indeed computed on fixed T-pose legs, this ablation is uninterpretable and should be omitted. If the implementation differs from the text, clarify how gradients reach the upper body or how the loss is meaningful for seated speakers.
- Add even a small validation of pseudo-ground-truth quality (e.g., MPJPE on a small mocap subset, or a manual audit of separation/alignment accuracy) to strengthen confidence in the benchmark metrics.
