## Summary

This paper proposes Steady Thought (ST), a thought-level preference optimization framework that addresses the "under-thinking" problem in Large Reasoning Models—where models excessively switch away from promising reasoning trajectories. ST operates in three stages: (1) entropy-based thought segmentation to identify reasoning episodes, (2) thought completion via logit suppression of switch-triggering tokens to generate coherent "commit" trajectories, and (3) Steady Thought Preference Optimization (STPO), which constructs fine-grained preference pairs at the thought level using length-normalized SimPO-style objectives. Experiments across three model sizes show accuracy improvements of 1.9–5.3% and token reductions of 17.3–39.3%.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated and timely problem.** The under-thinking phenomenon (models finding correct reasoning early but abandoning it) is a real and documented issue in LRMs. The formalization as a preference optimization problem at the thought level is principled and provides a constructive approach rather than simply suppressing switching behavior.

- **Consistent empirical improvements across model sizes and OOD transfer.** ST delivers accuracy gains while reducing output length across 1.5B, 8B, and 14B models. The positive results on LiveCode (OOD, code domain) despite training only on math data suggest genuine learned behavior rather than memorization (Table 1).

- **Thoughtful ablation design.** Table 4 compares SFT, DPO, and STPO on the same base model, showing that STPO outperforms both. The entropy threshold analysis (Table 3) demonstrates awareness of segmentation sensitivity. Table 2 provides interpretable metrics for "invalid switching" reduction.

- **Modular and extensible framework.** The three-stage pipeline (segmentation → completion → optimization) is cleanly decomposed, making each component independently analyzable and improvable.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Circularity in evaluation of "under-thinking mitigation."** The paper's central claim is that ST mitigates under-thinking by enabling "more rational switching and deeper exploration." However, the metrics used to validate this (Table 2's "proportion of correct thoughts" and Figure 2's "final thought proportion") are all computed using the same entropy-based segmentation pipeline that underpins the training data construction. The "correct thought" label is determined by forcing completions via logit suppression of switch tokens and checking if the resulting answer is correct—this is the same mechanism used to generate STPO's training signal. After training with STPO, the model produces shorter outputs with fewer identified switches and lower PCT, but this is expected *by construction* when one trains on data generated by suppressing switch tokens under a length-normalized objective. The paper does not provide any external validation (e.g., human annotation of thought boundaries, independent verifier checking whether a thought would lead to a correct answer, or even a different segmentation method) to show that the qualitative phenomenon of under-thinking is genuinely reduced. Without this, the evaluation shows that STPO produces outputs that score well on STPO-aligned metrics, not that real reasoning behavior has changed in the claimed way.

- **The Thought Completion stage uses the same mechanism (logit suppression of switch tokens) that the paper critiques in NOWAIT.** Section 1 explicitly argues that NOWAIT and SEAL "apply suppression globally, potentially limiting the model's flexibility to explore alternative reasoning thoughts when necessary." Yet in Section 3.2, ST generates its "commit trajectories" by suppressing the logits of "wait," "alternatively," etc.—the exact same mechanism as NOWAIT, just applied during data generation rather than inference. The paper does not adequately address why this suppression is acceptable for constructing preference data (where it serves as the ground truth for what counts as a "promising" thought) but not acceptable as an inference-time intervention. If the forced completions are systematically distorted by suppressing switch tokens, then the preference signal is biased toward the very behavior (suppressed switching) the paper argues against using globally. This is a conceptual tension that undermines the claim that ST teaches "when" to switch, since the training data only shows the model "commit" trajectories generated under total suppression.

- **Missing critical baselines for isolating the contribution of thought-level structure.** The paper positions STPO as a thought-level preference method, but does not compare against SimPO applied at the full-response level on the same training data (Omni-MATH). Table 4 compares SFT, DPO, and STPO, but SFT uses a different input format (full x rather than (Q, T_i)), and the DPO comparison appears to be standard response-level DPO affected by length bias—the very issue SimPO was designed to address. A fair isolation of the thought-level contribution would require: (1) response-level SimPO on the same Omni-MATH data, and ideally (2) thought-level DPO (without length normalization) to disentangle the effect of thought-level conditioning from the effect of SimPO's length normalization. Without these, it is unclear whether the gains come from the thought-level data construction, the SimPO-style length normalization, or simply from high-quality preference data on mathematics tasks.

### Minor

- **Under-specified implementation details.** The exact trigger word list (beyond "wait" and "alternatively"), the strength/scale of logit suppression, entropy computation details (greedy vs. sampling, per-token vs. window averaging), and preference data statistics (how many pairs per model, what fraction of completions succeed, how many are discarded) are not provided, making reproduction difficult.

- **NOWAIT baseline appears misconfigured on Qwen3-8B.** On Qwen3-8B, NOWAIT drops overall accuracy from 80.23% to 59.03% while *increasing* tokens by 84.6%, which contradicts its design goal and suggests poor hyperparameter tuning. This makes the relative comparison on this model less reliable.

- **Small test set size for AIME 2024 (30 problems).** Even with 8 runs, accuracy differences of a few percentage points on 30 problems may not be statistically meaningful. No confidence intervals are reported for any benchmark.

- **Self-generated preference data limits improvement ceiling.** The model generates its own "commit" trajectories, which means the preference data quality is bounded by the model's current reasoning ability. The paper does not analyze what fraction of thoughts yield successful completions, how this varies by problem difficulty, or whether low-quality completions introduce systematic noise.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Report preference data statistics: number of pairs generated per model, success rate of completions per difficulty level.
- Include response-level SimPO as a baseline using the same training data to isolate the thought-level contribution.
- Analyze failure cases where ST may cause over-commitment to incorrect early thoughts.
- Test on non-math/code reasoning tasks (e.g., logical deduction, multi-hop QA) to further substantiate the generalization claim.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation across the reviews is the tension at the heart of this paper: ST's Thought Completion stage generates "ground truth" commit trajectories using the exact logit-suppression mechanism that the paper argues against using globally at inference time. This means the preference data teaches the model to prefer trajectories that look like "force-completed" outputs—shorter, more linear reasoning chains—without any external signal that these are genuinely better reasoning paths. If the model simply learns to suppress switching behavior (as NOWAIT does at inference), then STPO is a roundabout training-time route to achieve what NOWAIT achieves at inference time, with the additional benefit of length normalization from SimPO. The paper's core contribution would then be the data construction pipeline rather than the "thought-level preference" conceptual framework. This is a contribution, but less novel than the claimed "teaching models when to switch versus when to commit."

## Suggestions

1. **Add response-level SimPO as a baseline** on the Omni-MATH data to determine whether the thought-level structure actually adds value beyond SimPO's length normalization.
2. **Conduct a small human annotation study** to validate thought boundaries (even 50–100 examples) and show that entropy-based segmentation aligns with human-judged reasoning transitions.
3. **Analyze the success rate of forced completions** by difficulty level and quantify how many preference pairs survive filtering—this determines whether the method is feasible for harder problems where the model cannot complete even promising thoughts.
4. **Acknowledge and discuss the conceptual tension** between using logit suppression for data generation while critiquing its use at inference time, and provide analysis on whether completions generated under suppression are distributionally similar to natural "commit" trajectories.
5. **Report confidence intervals** or at minimum standard deviations across runs for the main accuracy results, especially for the small AIME 2024 benchmark.
