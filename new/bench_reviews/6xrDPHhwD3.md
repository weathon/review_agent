## Summary

MFC-MIL proposes a plug-and-play framework for Whole Slide Image (WSI) classification comprising three modules: (1) a Causal Memory Intervention Module (CMIM) that replaces clustering-based distribution estimation (as in CaMIL) with a learnable memory bank to approximate front-door causal adjustment; (2) a Multiscale Spatial Representation Module (MSRM) combining PPEG positional encoding with multi-scale large-kernel convolutions to simulate multi-resolution feature extraction; and (3) a Frequency-domain Structural Representation Module (FSRM) using the Hilbert transform to extract phase-based features argued to be robust to staining artifacts. The framework is evaluated on Camelyon16 and TCGA-NSCLC against five MIL baselines.

---

## Strengths

- **Plug-and-play cross-backbone validation**: The framework is applied as an add-on to five distinct MIL backbones (ABMIL, DSMIL, TransMIL, CLAM-SB/MB, DTFD-MIL), and ACC/F1 improvements are consistent across most, providing more evidence than single-backbone demonstrations.

- **Novel Hilbert-transform application**: Using the imaginary part of the analytic signal (phase information) as a pathology feature representation is a creative and relatively unexplored idea in this domain. Table 4 provides concrete comparison against FFT, DCT, and DWT, showing meaningful AUC and F1 advantages.

- **Practical improvement over CaMIL's clustering**: Replacing the computationally expensive offline clustering step required by CaMIL with an end-to-end learnable memory bank is a legitimate engineering advance. The memory module enables a fully differentiable approximation of the front-door adjustment.

- **5-fold CV with reported standard deviations**: Reporting mean ± std across 5-fold cross-validation is methodologically better than single-run results and allows inspection of variance.

- **Ablation studies are reasonably thorough**: Sections 4.5.1–4.5.3 ablate each of the three modules, test memory slot sensitivity (k), joint dimension (D_j), and compare four frequency transforms.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None that individually invalidate the entire paper, but the combination of Major weaknesses below is serious.

---

### Major

1. **No comparison with CaMIL — the most direct competitor is absent from Table 1 and Table 2.** The paper explicitly positions itself against CaMIL (Chen et al., AAAI 2024) in Section 1 and 2.2, arguing for efficiency and interpretability improvements. Yet CaMIL never appears in the quantitative comparison tables. Without this comparison, the central claim of "improving over existing causal MIL methods" is completely unsubstantiated. This is not a missing related work issue — it is a missing baseline from a paper the authors explicitly benchmark against in prose.

2. **No experiment validates the central robustness/causal claims.** The paper's motivation is rooted in reducing spurious correlations from staining artifacts and confounders (Abstract, Sec. 1, Sec. 3.3). The FSRM is specifically motivated by stain invariance. Yet experiments in Sec. 4 use only standard in-distribution benchmarks with no stain-shift protocol, cross-domain generalization, synthetic bias test, or correlation analysis between predictions and known confounders (e.g., stain intensity). Without such an evaluation, the claims about causal deconfounding and stain robustness are purely asserted and cannot be verified.

3. **AUC — the most important threshold-independent metric in pathology — degrades in several cases.** Table 1 shows CLAM-SB AUC drops from 96.99 → 96.11 (Camelyon16), CLAM-MB drops from 97.65 → 97.29 (Camelyon16). Table 2 shows IBMIL has better AUC than MFC-MIL (96.31 vs. 95.41). The paper acknowledges this in Sec. 4.4, attributing it to altered boundary handling, but this explanation is vague and actually reveals a potential calibration failure of the causal intervention. If deconfounding genuinely improved the model, AUC degradation is hard to justify — it suggests the intervention shifts the distribution in ways that distort probability ranking.

4. **The front-door causal identification assumptions are not argued for this setting.** Eq. 4–5 import front-door notation and assume: (i) M intercepts all causal paths from X to Y, (ii) no unblocked backdoor from X to M, (iii) all backdoors from M to Y are blocked by X. None of these assumptions are argued for the WSI MIL setting, nor is there any empirical verification (e.g., correlation analysis). Furthermore, Sec. 3.1 is internally inconsistent: it first says "features generated by the aggregator θ_a are treated as mediators," then MSRM/FSRM outputs are described as the mediators — these are materially different objects. The FSRM also operates on learned feature vectors, not raw image pixels, making the claim of stain-invariance more indirect than suggested.

---

### Minor

5. **Table 3 ablation is confusing and potentially erroneous.** (a) The first row (CMIM alone, no MSRM/FSRM) shows ACC=84.50, AUC=94.88, F1=80.90 — identical to the TransMIL baseline in Table 1. This is plausible (CMIM without a proper mediator degenerates to the baseline) but unexplained. (b) Two distinct rows have all three checkmarks (✓✓✓) with different numbers (ACC 89.46/97.61 vs. 90.85/97.68) — suggesting two configurations, but the distinction is not described. This makes the ablation conclusions in Sec. 4.5 unreliable as stated.

6. **FSRM hurts precision and specificity.** Table 3 shows that adding FSRM to CMIM+MSRM drops precision from 91.61→89.25 and specificity from 94.25→92.75. The paper notes this in Sec. 4.5.3 but interprets it as improved sensitivity. This trade-off should be more carefully analyzed rather than presented as unambiguously beneficial, particularly given that false positive rates matter clinically.

7. **Outdated feature extractor.** Using ResNet18 pretrained with SimCLR (from the DSMIL framework) is weak by current standards. Modern self-supervised pathology encoders (CONCH, UNI, PLIP, etc.) routinely raise MIL baselines. The performance improvements may partially compensate for poor features rather than demonstrating a general methodological advance.

8. **The TCGA-NSCLC experiment uses a random 7:1:2 split for a method claiming generalization improvements.** A random split from a single institution primarily measures within-dataset interpolation, not generalization. Cross-institutional or cross-staining evaluation would more appropriately test the paper's claims.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Visualize what memory slots learn**: t-SNE or representative patch visualizations per memory slot would help validate whether the memory bank captures meaningful distributional structure rather than acting as generic prototypes.
- **Stain-augmented or cross-stain robustness test**: Even a simple experiment training on one staining protocol and testing on a stain-augmented version would validate the FSRM stain-invariance claim.
- **Computational overhead comparison**: The paper claims to simplify training compared to CaMIL and IBMIL; a table of FLOPs or wall-clock times would validate this.
- **Statistical significance tests**: Given that some improvements have propagated uncertainties that overlap zero (e.g., ABMIL ΔSpe=+1.25±9.15; DSMIL ΔSpe=−0.75±3.31), p-values would clarify which improvements are reliable.
- **Multi-class tasks**: Evaluating on multi-class subtyping would test whether the framework generalizes beyond binary classification.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most genuinely novel element is applying the Hilbert transform to WSI feature vectors as a frequency-domain representation intended to suppress staining confounders. While the stain-invariance claim is asserted rather than validated, the idea that phase information from feature-space signal analysis could capture structural pathology patterns independently of amplitude (which encodes color/stain) is interesting and worth pursuing. The memory-bank approach to approximating the front-door adjustment distribution is also a practical and trainable substitute for CaMIL's clustering, though the theoretical justification remains incomplete. These are incremental but non-trivial additions to the causal MIL literature.

## Suggestions

1. **Add CaMIL as a direct baseline** — this is non-negotiable given the paper's positioning. Report performance on the same 5-fold CV protocol.
2. **Design at least one confounder-robustness experiment**: Stain augmentation (e.g., random H&E augmentation or Macenko normalization ablation), subgroup analysis by scanner/center, or a synthetic bias protocol (correlate stain intensity with labels during training, evaluate on the decorrelated test set).
3. **Fix Table 3**: Explain the two ✓✓✓ rows and the first-row baseline equivalence.
4. **Temper causal language** to match what the method actually does: replacing clustering-based mediator estimation with an end-to-end memory module. The front-door framework is a motivating formalism, but if the identification conditions are not argued, state it as an approximation.
5. **Report AUC as primary metric** in the abstract and conclusion; do not claim "significant improvement" based solely on ACC/F1 when AUC is flat or negative.
