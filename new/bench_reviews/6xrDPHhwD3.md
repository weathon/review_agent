## Summary

The paper proposes MFC-MIL, a plug-and-play framework for Multiple Instance Learning in Whole Slide Image classification that combines three modules: a Causal Memory Intervention Module (CMIM) claiming to implement front-door causal adjustment via a learnable memory bank, a Multiscale Spatial Representation Module (MSRM) using large-kernel convolutions to fuse features at different scales, and a Frequency-domain Structural Representation Module (FSRM) applying the Hilbert transform to feature vectors. Experiments on Camelyon16 and TCGA-NSCLC show accuracy and F1 improvements across several MIL backbones.

## Strengths

- **Consistent empirical improvements across multiple MIL backbones.** Table 1 shows MFC improves ACC and F1 for all five MIL methods (ABMIL, DSMIL, TransMIL, CLAM-SB, CLAM-MB, DTFD) on both datasets, with particularly large gains for DTFD-MIL on TCGA-NSCLC (+9.92% ACC, +10.36% AUC). This demonstrates practical utility as a plug-in module.

- **Novel architectural combination.** The combination of learnable memory-based intervention, multiscale spatial convolutions, and Hilbert-transform-based frequency processing in the MIL context is creative. The ablations (Table 3) show each module contributes incrementally.

- **Thorough ablation on hyperparameters.** The exploration of memory slot count k (Figure 3), joint dimension D_j (Table 4), and frequency transform choices provides useful practical guidance.

- **The Hilbert transform comparison is informative.** The ablation against FFT, DCT, and DWT (Table 4) demonstrates the Hilbert variant produces the best overall performance, offering empirical justification for this design choice.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The front-door causal claim is not properly substantiated.** The paper's central novelty is that CMIM implements a front-door causal intervention to remove unobserved confounding. However: (1) The front-door criterion (Pearl) requires that M intercepts all directed paths from X to Y, no unblocked back-door path from X to M, and all back-door paths from M to Y blocked by X. None of these conditions are established or argued for. (2) The mapping from Eq. (5) to the implemented network operations (memory attention + NWGM) is qualitative, with no formal argument that the computed quantity approximates P(Y|do(X)). (3) The memory module is described as estimating P(X=ẑ), but it consists of k learnable parameters trained via gradient descent on the task loss—this is a discriminative codebook, not an explicit density model. As stated in Section 3.1: "a set of trainable parameters with length k is initialized as memory and combined with attention-weighted inputs to select relevant memory elements." There is no constraint ensuring the memory approximates P(X) in any distributional sense needed for front-door identification. The method is better described as a memory-augmented attention mechanism, but the paper frames it as causal intervention throughout the title, abstract, and narrative. This overclaim undermines the paper's core claimed contribution.

- **No empirical validation of the deconfounding claim.** The paper motivates its approach by the need to handle spurious correlations and staining/color biases (Section 1, Figure 1a). Yet all experiments use standard in-distribution train/test splits on Camelyon16 and TCGA-NSCLC with no distribution-shift or bias-stress tests—no cross-center evaluation, no synthetic stain manipulation, no color-label correlation inversion. The paper itself acknowledges that AUC improvements are inconsistent and sometimes negative (Section 4.4: CLAM-SB AUC drops from 96.99→96.11, CLAM-MB from 97.65→97.29 on Camelyon16). An AUC drop is precisely the opposite of what one would expect from genuine deconfounding. Without targeted experiments, the claim that MFC mitigates spurious correlations is unsupported.

- **Missing direct comparison with CaMIL.** CaMIL (Chen et al., 2024) is the most directly comparable method—both use front-door causal intervention for MIL—but it is absent from experimental comparison despite being discussed as a key competitor in Section 2.2 (where the paper claims MFC is simpler and more efficient). Without this comparison, the claimed advantage over clustering-based mediator estimation is unsubstantiated.

- **Inconsistent mediator definition.** The paper alternates between incompatible definitions of the mediator M: Section 3.1 states "the features generated by the aggregator θ_a are treated as the mediators"; Figure 1(e) suggests MSRM+FSRM produce M; and the implementation states "our MFC estimates the mediator using patch-level features and applies it to intervene in the aggregated bag-level prediction vector." For the front-door formula (Eq. 5) to be meaningful, M must have a clearly defined causal role, which requires specifying what M is and its conditional independences. This inconsistency makes the causal framework ill-specified.

### Minor

- **MSRM's "multiscale" label is semantically misleading.** The paper motivates MSRM by the need to integrate "low-magnification tissue structures and high-magnification cellular structures" (Section 1), but all patches are extracted at 20× magnification (Section 4.1). MSRM's "multiscale" is purely in kernel size space (2D convolutions with kernels 3/5/7 and 1D dilated convolutions), not across physical magnification levels. The method is empirically useful as a multi-receptive-field module, but the biological motivation of "low vs. high magnification" is not reflected in the experimental setup.

- **Large propagated uncertainties overlap with claimed improvements.** For example, DSMIL on Camelyon16 shows ACC improvement of +5.27(±5.27), and TransMIL shows +6.35(±2.98) on specificity: 83.50(±10.25)→92.75(±5.77). While means improve, the variances are substantial, and no paired statistical significance tests are reported to confirm these differences are meaningful across folds.

- **The "interpretability" claim is unsupported.** The abstract and conclusion claim the framework enhances "interpretability," but no qualitative results—attention heatmaps, memory slot visualizations, or feature maps—are provided to demonstrate what CMIM or FSRM actually learns or suppresses.

### Trivial

- The Hilbert transform description in Section 3.3 is textbook-level (Eq. 7-8) and could be condensed.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Comparison against a simple non-causal memory/prototype baseline (e.g., a learned codebook without the front-door formulation) would isolate whether the "causal" structure is what drives performance gains vs. simply adding more parameters and representational capacity.

## Novel Insights

The gap between the paper's causal narrative and its actual implementation reveals a broader pattern in the MIL-for-pathology literature: methods that import causal inference vocabulary (front-door, back-door, deconfounding) often implement standard attention or prototype mechanisms without establishing the structural conditions required for causal identification. The empirical gains here are real, but they likely stem from the representational benefits of memory-augmented attention and multi-scale/frequency feature enrichment—not from causal deconfounding per se. This distinction matters for the community: if architectural innovations are packaged as causal contributions without causal validation, it risks diluting the meaning of "causal" in this research area.

## Suggestions

- Reframe the contribution honestly: describe CMIM as a memory-augmented attention mechanism inspired by front-door causal reasoning, with the causal interpretation remaining a motivating perspective rather than a proven property. This would still be a valid contribution without the overclaim.

- Add CaMIL as a baseline comparison on both datasets, and ideally include a simple non-causal memory baseline to isolate the contribution of the front-door formulation.

- Report paired significance tests across the 5-fold cross-validation to confirm that improvements are statistically meaningful given the high variances.

- Add at least one distribution-shift or cross-center experiment (even an informal one) to provide initial evidence for the deconfounding claim.

- Add memory slot or attention visualizations to substantiate the "interpretability" claim.
