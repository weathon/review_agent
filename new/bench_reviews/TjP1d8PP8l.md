## Summary
The paper proposes DGAP, a framework that trains a small sentence-transformer discriminator on limited demonstrations to provide step-wise action scores, which then guide LLM planners via threshold-based in-context replanning. The method is evaluated on ScienceWorld and VirtualHome across GPT-4, Llama3-70B, and InternVL2-8B, showing consistent improvements over SwiftSage and other baselines. The paper also draws qualitative connections to critic-regularized RL optimization.

## Strengths

- **Step-level discriminative guidance is practically useful.** Unlike outcome-only feedback methods (Reflexion) or trajectory-level search (ToT), DGAP provides per-action scores via a small discriminator (RoBERTa-based, Section 3.1, Eq. 1), enabling mid-trajectory correction before environment execution. This is concretely realized through the threshold-based replanning loop in Section 3.2, where actions scoring below τ trigger regeneration.

- **Consistent empirical gains across two benchmarks and multiple backbones.** DGAP outperforms SwiftSage on ScienceWorld (Table 1: D-GPT4 85.91 vs. S-GPT4 84.68 overall; D-Llama3 82.96 vs. S-Llama3 76.43). On VirtualHome (Table 2), DGAP-GPT4 achieves the highest success rate across all three settings (In-Distribution 88.00, NovelScenes 62.67, NovelTasks 72.17), with results holding across both proprietary and open-weight models plus a VLM variant.

- **Computationally efficient architecture suitable for API-based LLMs.** Offloading step-wise evaluation to a small pre-trained sentence transformer avoids expensive LLM self-evaluation or environment rollouts during planning. No LLM parameter updates are required, making it applicable to closed-source models. This design choice is practically motivated and well-suited to the stated deployment scenario.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **No ablation isolating the discriminator's learned contribution from the prompting mechanism itself.** The central claim is that a *learned* discriminator score function provides value beyond existing in-context feedback mechanisms. However, there is no comparison against a zero-shot LLM-as-a-judge step evaluator, exact string matching to demonstrations, or a simple rule-based step heuristic. The observed gains could stem primarily from the prompt structure (exposing explicit action-score pairs and forcing replanning below a threshold) rather than from the quality of the trained $D_\phi$. Without this ablation, the necessity of training the discriminator over simpler alternatives remains unverified.

- **Evaluation metrics and reporting are inconsistently rigorous across benchmarks.** Table 1 (ScienceWorld) reports point estimates without any variance, confidence intervals, or multiple-run averages, while Table 2 (VirtualHome) includes standard deviations. For ScienceWorld, the overall improvement of D-GPT4 over S-GPT4 is ~1.2 percentage points (85.91 vs. 84.68) — a marginal gap that cannot be assessed as statistically significant without error bars given the known variance of LLM prompting in interactive environments. This asymmetry in reporting standards weakens confidence in the ScienceWorld conclusion.

### Minor

- **Theoretical analysis (Section 3.3) addresses proxy reward optimization, not task success.** The paper formalizes DGAP as a KL-regularized optimization (Eq. 3-4) and derives Lemma 3.1 / Corollary 3.2 showing that the resulting policy improves over $\pi^{\text{llm}}$ in expected return under $R_\phi$. However, $R_\phi = D_\phi(s,a)$ measures step-wise semantic alignment with demonstration actions, not environment reward or task completion. Corollary 3.2 proves improvement *with respect to the discriminator's own scoring function*, which is a standard result in regularized RL and does not establish that higher discriminator scores correlate with higher environment success rates. The section is titled "Qualitative Analysis," which partially tempers this concern, but the paper still uses the theoretical results to claim the method "obtains a stronger policy than the LLM planner" without clarifying that this strictly applies to the proxy reward.

- **Self-referential efficiency visualization.** Figure 5 plots discriminator scores on the Y-axis against time steps to claim "higher scores in fewer steps." Since DGAP is explicitly optimized to maximize discriminator scores, it will by construction achieve higher scores than any non-optimized baseline. This figure is circular and does not demonstrate environmental efficiency (e.g., fewer environment steps to task completion, lower computational cost per task, or higher success rate at fixed step budget). A plot of actual environment reward or steps-to-success would be substantially more informative.

- **Discriminator training data augmentation relies on BC priors for anchor scoring.** Section 3.1 notes that "in unseen scenarios without ground-truth, we mildly treat the first candidate generated by the LM as 10" and subsequent candidates are scored by cosine similarity to that first candidate. While expert data (score 10) and random data (score 0) provide polar anchors, a significant portion of the dataset is constructed by self-similarity to BC outputs. This means $D_\phi$ learns to distinguish actions relative to a behavior-cloned policy's distribution rather than purely to oracle expert behavior. The paper does not analyze whether this introduces systematic bias, particularly in OOD states where BC priors and optimal actions diverge.

### Trivial

- **Threshold $\tau$ is manually set without sensitivity analysis.** The paper states "we adopt a threshold of 5 for ScienceWorld and 6 for VirtualHome, based on their respective training data distributions" (Section 3.2). No analysis is provided showing how performance varies with $\tau$, which affects the trade-off between replanning frequency (and thus API cost) and action correction.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Score-to-success correlation analysis.** A straightforward diagnostic would be to correlate $D_\phi$'s output with actual environment success across tasks. If high discriminator scores frequently occur in failed trajectories, the guidance signal is fundamentally misaligned with the true objective.

- **Error breakdown by failure mode.** Categorizing failures into semantic mismatch, environment constraint violation, and planning horizon exhaustion would reveal whether the discriminator successfully filters invalid actions or merely prefers syntactically plausible but functionally wrong ones.

The weakness is real (listed as Minor above) but the critic's framing as "invalidating the central theoretical contribution" overstates the case since the theory is positioned as analysis, not the paper's core claim.

- *"The discriminator's training targets are circularly constructed from a suboptimal imitation policy, directly contradicting the stated goal of expert alignment."* **Remove the "directly contradicts" framing.** The paper uses expert data (score 10) as the strongest training signal alongside BC-augmented data. The concern that BC-augmented data introduces bias is valid (listed as Minor) but the claim that this "directly contradicts expert alignment" misreads the data construction — expert data still anchors the regression targets at the top end.

- *"Overclaims novelty by positioning 'LLM as optimizer' as a new contribution; this paradigm is now well-established."* **Removed.** The paper does not claim "LLM as optimizer" is novel; it cites Yang et al. ## Novel Insights
The paper's core insight — that a lightweight, pre-trained discriminator trained on limited demonstrations can provide step-level feedback signals effective enough to guide even API-closed LLMs through in-context prompting — is practical and addresses a real deployment constraint. However, the fundamental observation that step-level process guidance can outperform trajectory-level reflection has been established in prior process reward literature (e.g., process reward models for reasoning). DGAP's differentiator is the specific implementation: using a sentence transformer for action similarity scoring combined with threshold-based replanning. The novel aspect worth emphasizing is not the idea itself but the engineering choice to use a small discriminator as a proxy evaluator rather than relying on expensive LLM-as-a-judge or environment rollouts, achieving improved performance while remaining applicable to closed-source models. The main risk to the paper's positioning is that, without ablations against simpler step evaluators, it is unclear whether the learned discriminator provides genuine advantage over zero-shot alternatives.

## Suggestions

1. **Add an ablation comparing the trained discriminator against a zero-shot LLM-as-a-judge step evaluator.** This is the most critical missing experiment — it would establish whether training the small discriminator provides additive value over simply prompting the LLM to score its own actions. If the trained discriminator performs comparably or better at a fraction of the cost, the case for DGAP is significantly strengthened.

2. **Report variance (standard deviation or confidence intervals) for ScienceWorld results (Table 1).** Since Table 2 already includes these, adding them to Table 1 would demonstrate that the marginal ~1.2% improvement for GPT-4 is statistically meaningful and would bring the evaluation standards into alignment across benchmarks.

3. **Replace or supplement Figure 5 with an environment-centric efficiency plot.** Plot cumulative environment steps-to-success or success-rate-at-fixed-step-budget instead of discriminator scores. This would substantiate claims about planning efficiency in a way that is not self-referential.

4. **Include a brief sensitivity analysis for the replanning threshold $\tau$.** Even a limited sweep (e.g., $\tau \in \{3, 5, 7\}$ on a subset of ScienceWorld tasks) would demonstrate that the method's gains are robust to this hyperparameter rather than being the result of careful tuning.
