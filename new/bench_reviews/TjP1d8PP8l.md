## Summary

DGAP (Discriminator-Guided Action Optimization) proposes to combine offline demonstration knowledge with LLM planning by training a lightweight RoBERTa-based discriminator (scorer) that measures alignment between LLM-generated actions and expert actions at each step. The scores are fed back into an LLM prompt to guide iterative re-planning when scores fall below a threshold, eliminating the need for environment-level outcome feedback. The authors frame this as critic-regularized optimization, claim formal policy improvement guarantees, and show performance gains on ScienceWorld and VirtualHome over strong baselines including SwiftSage and Tree Planner.

---

## Strengths

- **Practical and implementable step-level guidance mechanism.** The core idea—converting expert demonstration knowledge into a learned step-level score and integrating those scores into LLM prompts—is concrete, well-motivated, and works without parameter updates to the backbone LLM. This makes it compatible with API-only deployment, a real practical constraint.

- **Meaningful empirical improvements on VirtualHome.** DGAP-GPT4 achieves 88.00 SR in-distribution (vs. 82.33 for ProgPrompt), and substantially outperforms all baselines on NovelTasks/NovelScenes SR (62.67 and 72.17, vs. 41.67 and 52.33 for the next best). These are genuine, non-trivial gains.

- **Two benchmarks, two LLM backbones.** The evaluation is more thorough than most comparable papers. Results are reported with three replications and standard deviations on VirtualHome, and the method is tested with both GPT-4 and Llama3-70B.

- **Data efficiency.** The paper uses approximately half the demonstrations compared to baselines like SwiftSage, combined with augmentation via beam search and similarity-based labeling, making the method potentially practical in low-data regimes.

- **Long-horizon gains in ScienceWorld.** The discrimination between Short/Medium/Long tasks (Table 1) is useful and reveals that DGAP benefits longer tasks substantially (Long: 84.52 vs. 83.00 for GPT-4; 78.47 vs. 62.31 for Llama3), which is exactly the setting where step-level guidance should matter most.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None identified. The core empirical contribution is real and the paper is a complete submission.*

### Major

- **The efficiency claim is not quantitatively supported.** The abstract, conclusion, and §5.1(iii) all claim DGAP achieves "better efficiency" and "reduces the necessary steps and queries." However, there is no efficiency table—no comparison of LLM call counts, replanning rounds, token usage, or wall-clock cost. Fig. 5 shows score-vs-step trajectory curves qualitatively and cannot be read as a comparative efficiency metric. Since DGAP *adds* a discriminator inference pass and triggers replanning loops whenever a score falls below threshold τ, it is entirely possible it incurs higher computational cost than, say, SwiftSage. This makes the efficiency claim an unsupported assertion, not an empirical result. The paper should either produce a quantified comparison or remove "better efficiency" from its claims.

- **Theory-practice gap in the formal guarantee.** The paper asserts in the abstract and §3.3 that DGAP is "provable to achieve a stronger policy than the LLM planner." Lemma 3.1 and Corollary 3.2 prove improvement for the *analytical* optimizer of a KL-regularized objective over cumulative returns $R_\phi(s,a)$ (Eq. 4). The paper itself acknowledges: *"such an objective is slightly different from DGAP where the LLM planner is prompted to generate actions that maximize the single-step return rather than the cumulative return."* The justification—*"a successful multi-step plan requires single-step optimality in each planning step"*—is a strong structural assumption that is generally false in sequential decision-making: locally-best actions need not maximize task success. The theory section, therefore, provides loose intuition rather than a proof that the implemented algorithm achieves the stated guarantee. The paper should be honest about what the theory does and does not establish.

- **Discriminator training labels are heuristic and partly self-referential.** The paper claims the discriminator measures "alignment with the underlying expert/optimal action" (§3.1), but the actual label signal is: (a) score 10 for oracle positives, (b) score 0 for least-semantically-similar random negatives, and (c) for the large offline dataset $\mathcal{B}_o$, the first candidate from the fine-tuned LM is *assumed* to be score 10, and remaining candidates are scored by cosine similarity to that candidate. The text states: *"we mildly treat the first candidate generated by the LM as 10 for the distribution of fine-tuned small models aligns more closely with the training data domain."* This creates a bootstrapping loop where LM-generated actions become ground truth by assumption. The discriminator is primarily learning semantic proximity to the fine-tuned LM's outputs, not to the true optimal action. This is a genuine structural issue that partially undermines the paper's core interpretive claim about what the discriminator measures, though it may still have empirical utility.

### Minor

- **Modest and mixed gains in ScienceWorld for GPT-4.** DGAP-GPT4 achieves 85.91 vs. SwiftSage-GPT4's 84.68—a 1.23 point improvement. Furthermore, DGAP underperforms SwiftSage on Short tasks (90.84 vs. 92.22) and on several individual tasks (2-2, 2-3, 5-1, 5-2, 6-3, 8-2). The paper presents this as "outperforms SwiftSage in most tasks," which is technically accurate but overstated as a characterization of the overall picture. The Llama3 gains are more substantial (82.96 vs. 76.43).

- **No ablation isolating key components.** It is unclear whether gains come from: (a) the discriminator scores themselves, (b) the enriched action-score-pair prompt context, or (c) simple rejection sampling / repeated replanning. A baseline that uses the discriminator for filtering only (without feeding score history into the prompt) would clarify the contribution of the "LLM-as-optimizer" mechanism.

- **Replanning threshold τ is unjustified and not analyzed.** The thresholds τ=5 (ScienceWorld) and τ=6 (VirtualHome) are stated to be "based on their respective training data distributions" with no further justification, sensitivity analysis, or ablation. Since this parameter directly controls both performance and cost, its omission weakens the reproducibility and interpretability of the results.

- **VLM experiment (InternVL2-8B) is poorly integrated.** This experiment modifies the action step length and generation stopping criterion simultaneously: *"the generation now stops when the next action is [walk]."* This changes both the perception mechanism and the planning granularity, making results difficult to interpret relative to the other DGAP runs. It should be presented as a separate exploratory experiment, not part of the main comparison table.

### Trivial

- §2 formalizes the BC objective as $\pi^{bc} = \max_\pi \mathbb{E}_\mathcal{B}[\pi(a_t^i|l_i,h_t)]$ — this is missing the log-likelihood and is a loose formalization, but it does not affect any downstream claims.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Discriminator-only ablation** (train only on expert vs. random data, no offline augmentation) to establish whether the complex three-part data pipeline is needed.
- **Calibration analysis** of discriminator scores (score vs. actual task success correlation) to validate the score as a meaningful proxy for action quality.
- **Data efficiency curve**: vary the number of demonstrations and report performance, since data efficiency is a core advertised property.
- **Failure case analysis**: concrete step-by-step examples where the discriminator misguides or correctly redirects the LLM would strengthen qualitative understanding.
- Compare DGAP against using $\pi^{bc}$ directly as the planner, to clarify how much the discriminator+LLM pipeline adds over the fine-tuned small model that provides augmented training data.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely insightful observation across the reviews is the bootstrapping issue in discriminator training: by assigning score 10 to the first candidate from the fine-tuned LM in unseen scenarios (§3.1, $\mathcal{B}_o$), the system uses its own prior to generate pseudo-ground-truth labels, then trains a discriminator on those labels, then uses that discriminator to steer the *same* or a stronger LLM. This circularity is not merely a labeling heuristic—it means the discriminator is learning to imitate the fine-tuned LM's distribution rather than measuring true optimality. Yet the empirical results suggest the system still works, presumably because the fine-tuned LM already captures useful domain knowledge, and semantic similarity-based ranking is a reasonable coarse approximation. This tension—between the system's claimed semantic meaning and its actual supervision—is under-theorized in the paper and represents a gap that future work in step-level process supervision should address.

## Suggestions

1. **Produce an efficiency table** with number of LLM calls, replanning rounds per episode, and/or token counts per episode for DGAP vs. SwiftSage vs. Reflexion — this either validates or corrects the efficiency claim.
2. **Soften the theoretical claim** to "provides intuition connecting DGAP to critic-regularized RL" rather than "provable policy improvement," since the formal proof applies to the idealized optimizer of Eq. 4, not the actual threshold-replanning procedure.
3. **Add a component ablation**: (i) DGAP without score-history in the prompt (pure rejection sampling), (ii) DGAP without replanning (scores in prompt only), (iii) full DGAP.
4. **Provide a sensitivity analysis** over τ (e.g., τ ∈ {3, 5, 7} for ScienceWorld) to show robustness of the threshold choice.
5. **Clarify the VLM experiment** as a standalone exploratory result, not part of the main comparison table, and either remove or motivate it more clearly.
