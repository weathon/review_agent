## Summary

The paper introduces DGAP (Discriminator-Guided Action Optimization), a framework for improving LLM-based embodied planning by training a step-level discriminator on limited demonstrations to score LLM-generated actions. The discriminator provides per-step alignment scores that are fed back to the LLM planner via in-context prompts (action-score pairs), enabling closed-loop replanning when scores fall below a threshold. The paper evaluates DGAP on ScienceWorld and VirtualHome benchmarks with multiple LLM backbones (GPT-4, Llama3-70B, InternVL2-8B), claiming both empirical improvements and a theoretical connection to critic-regularized optimization with a "provable" policy improvement guarantee.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated core idea:** Training a step-level discriminator to provide dense intermediate feedback addresses a genuine limitation of trajectory-level outcome-feedback methods (Reflexion, ToT) and demonstration-hungry imitation learning approaches. The motivation for combining LLM reasoning with demonstration-derived grounding is clearly articulated (Section 1, Fig. 1).

- **Compatibility with API-based LLMs:** Unlike RLHF-style methods requiring parameter updates, DGAP operates entirely through in-context prompting, making it applicable to black-box models like GPT-4 (Section 3.3, connection to RLHF paragraph). This is a practical advantage.

- **Multi-benchmark, multi-backbone evaluation:** The paper tests DGAP across ScienceWorld and VirtualHome with GPT-4, Llama3-70B, and InternVL2-8B, providing evidence beyond a single model-domain pair. VirtualHome includes three generalization settings (In-Distribution, NovelTasks, NovelScenes), testing different robustness aspects (Table 2).

- **Substantial improvements with Llama3-70B:** While GPT-4 improvements are marginal (discussed below), D-Llama3 shows a meaningful 6.5-point overall gain over S-Llama3 in ScienceWorld (82.96 vs. 76.43, Table 1), and DGAP-Llama3 outperforms all baselines in VirtualHome NovelTasks (Table 2). This suggests the discriminator guidance is more impactful for less capable base models.

- **Data efficiency:** DGAP uses approximately half the demonstrations of competing methods (10–30 trajectories per task, Section 5.1), which is a practical advantage over demonstration-intensive approaches.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Theoretical "provable" guarantee does not apply to the implemented algorithm.** Contribution (iii) states DGAP "is provable to achieve a stronger policy than the LLM planner," and Corollary 3.2 proves Q^{π*_θ}(s,a) ≥ Q^{π^llm}(s,a) for the KL-constrained cumulative-return objective (Eq. 3–4). However, the actual DGAP algorithm: (a) does not enforce the KL constraint—β is a free parameter with no operational role in the prompting mechanism; (b) does not compute the partition function Z(s) or the exponential reweighting of Eq. 5–6; (c) maximizes single-step returns rather than cumulative returns as assumed in the proof (acknowledged on line 147: "the LLM planner is prompted to generate actions that maximize the single-step return rather than the cumulative return"); and (d) has no convergence guarantee to any fixed point. While Section 3.3 is labeled "qualitative analysis," the introduction and abstract present the guarantee as a factual contribution. The paper should either restrict the theoretical claim to the idealized objective (explicitly noting the gap to practice) or demonstrate that the implemented algorithm satisfies the conditions required by the proof. As it stands, claiming a "provable" improvement for a heuristic prompting procedure based on solving a different optimization problem is misleading.

- **Discriminator training data relies on fabricated scores with weak justification.** The majority of the discriminator's training data (B_o) is generated by: (1) taking the first beam-search candidate from a fine-tuned LM and assigning it score 10, then (2) scoring subsequent candidates by cosine similarity to this first candidate × 10 (Section 3.1). The justification—"the distribution of fine-tuned small models aligns more closely with the training data domain"—is not a valid reason to assign a perfect score of 10 to beam-search output from a behavior-cloned model. These are not verified expert actions. Since B_o constitutes a "significant proportion of the overall dataset," this systematic bias in the training signal could substantially affect discriminator calibration. No validation is provided to confirm that discriminator scores correlate with actual action quality (e.g., a score of 7 predicting better performance than a score of 4). Without this, there is no evidence the discriminator measures what it claims to measure—"alignment between LLM actions and the underlying expert" (Eq. 1).

### Minor

- **Marginal empirical improvement with the strongest backbone (GPT-4).** D-GPT4 vs. S-GPT4 shows only 85.91 vs. 84.68 overall in ScienceWorld (~1.2 points), and DGAP actually loses on short tasks (90.84 vs. 92.22, Table 1). The improvement is more convincing with Llama3-70B (~6.5 points). The paper's explanation for poor short-task performance—"limited pairs of short task sequences being less effective"—is speculative and untested (Section 5.1). This limits confidence in the method's benefit for already-capable LLMs.

- **Unclear demonstration budget comparison with SwiftSage.** The paper states DGAP uses "around half number of demonstrations as ToT, SFT, and SwiftSage" (Section 5.1), but does not clarify whether SwiftSage was re-run with the same reduced budget or used its original larger dataset. If SwiftSage had more demonstrations, the comparison conflates data efficiency with method quality, making it hard to isolate DGAP's contribution. If both used the same budget, this should be stated explicitly.

- **Single-step vs. cumulative return disconnect insufficiently addressed.** The theoretical framework relies on cumulative returns R_φ(s_t,a_t) = Σ r_φ(s_i,a_i) (Eq. 4), but the algorithm maximizes single-step returns. The paper dismisses this as "slightly different" and argues that "a successful multi-step plan requires single-step optimality in each planning step" (line 147). This argument is non-trivial and unvalidated—greedy per-step optimization is known to be suboptimal in general sequential decision-making. The paper provides no analysis or evidence that single-step score maximization leads to policy improvement in the cumulative-return sense.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Discriminator calibration analysis:** A scatter plot or correlation analysis between discriminator scores and actual action quality (e.g., downstream task reward, or cosine similarity to expert action) would directly validate whether the discriminator provides reliable guidance.

- **Replanning statistics:** Average number of replanning rounds per step and fraction of actions initially falling below threshold would clarify whether the closed-loop replanning loop is doing meaningful work or just adding cost.

- **Ablation on the "LLM as optimizer" mechanism:** Comparing against a simpler baseline where actions below threshold are simply rejected and resampled (without score-conditioned prompting) would isolate the value of the meta-prompt optimization strategy.

## Novel Insights

The observation that discriminator-guided replanning provides substantially larger gains for weaker LLM backbones (Llama3-70B: +6.5 pts) than for stronger ones (GPT-4: +1.2 pts) suggests that the discriminator's value may primarily be in compensating for domain-specific knowledge gaps rather than providing fundamentally new planning capabilities. This raises the question of whether such discriminators become less useful as base LLMs improve—a trend that would be important to understand for the long-term viability of this approach.

## Suggestions

- Revisit contribution (iii) to clearly state that the theoretical guarantee applies to the idealized KL-constrained objective, not to the implemented prompting algorithm. Add an explicit discussion of the theory-practice gap (KL non-enforcement, single-step vs. cumulative returns, no convergence guarantee).
- Validate discriminator calibration by correlating discriminator scores with actual task outcomes on a held-out validation set. This is the single most impactful addition that would strengthen confidence in the method.
- Report replanning statistics (average replanning rounds, fraction of below-threshold actions) to characterize the operational behavior of the closed-loop component.
