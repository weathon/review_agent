## Summary
This paper proposes DGAP, a framework for embodied LLM planning that learns a discriminator from a small number of demonstrations and uses its step-wise scores to guide prompting and replanning. The idea is practically appealing for API-based LLMs because it avoids parameter updates, and the empirical study on ScienceWorld and VirtualHome shows nontrivial gains, especially on VirtualHome. However, the paper overstates what its discriminator and theory actually establish, and key mechanism-validating evidence is missing.

## Strengths
- **Well-motivated problem and practically useful framing.** The paper targets a genuine weakness of embodied LLM agents: sparse trajectory-level feedback and costly trial-and-error refinement. Using a lightweight offline scorer plus prompt-time replanning is a practical design for API-based models.
- **Interesting core idea.** Converting limited demonstrations into a reusable step-level scoring signal, rather than only using them as direct in-context exemplars or for pure behavior cloning, is a meaningful contribution.
- **Broad empirical coverage.** The paper evaluates on two established embodied benchmarks, ScienceWorld and VirtualHome, and uses multiple backbones (GPT-4, Llama3-70B, plus a VLM setting on VirtualHome). The VirtualHome results are fairly strong, with DGAP-GPT4 improving over ProgPrompt/Inner Monologue and achieving the best SR in all three settings shown.
- **Evidence of some real benefit over strong LLM planners.** In ScienceWorld, DGAP improves overall results over SwiftSage for both GPT-4 (84.68→85.91) and Llama3 (76.43→82.96). In VirtualHome, DGAP also improves SR substantially over the baselines presented, especially on NovelScenes and NovelTasks.
- **The paper is upfront about at least one limitation.** It explicitly notes weaker performance on short tasks, which is consistent with Table 1 where DGAP does not outperform SwiftSage on the short-task aggregate.

## Weaknesses

###: Fatal
None.

### Major:
- **The central interpretation of the discriminator is overstated.** The paper repeatedly claims the discriminator “assesses the alignment between LLM-generated action and the underlying optimal one at every step,” but the actual supervision in §3.1 does not support that strong reading. Beyond expert actions scored 10 and easy random negatives scored 0, much of the signal comes from pseudo-labels derived from a fine-tuned BC-style model and sentence-embedding similarity. Most notably, the paper says: *“In unseen scenarios without ground-truth, we mildly treat the first candidate generated by the LM as 10 … The scores for the subsequent candidates are determined by multiplying their cosine similarity to the first candidate by 10.”* That makes the learned score much closer to a heuristic “demonstration/BC-likeness” score than a validated estimate of true action quality or optimality in the environment. This matters because the main conceptual claim of DGAP rests on the discriminator being meaningful guidance rather than just a semantic regularizer.
- **The theoretical guarantee does not cleanly apply to the implemented algorithm.** The paper’s strongest theoretical language is too broad relative to what is actually done. In §3.3, the formalism is a KL-regularized optimization over cumulative return, but the practical algorithm is prompt-based action generation with historical action-score pairs and thresholded replanning. The paper itself concedes the mismatch: *“the LLM planner is prompted to generate actions that maximize the single-step return rather than the cumulative return.”* The follow-up justification—*“a successful multi-step plan requires single-step optimality in each planning step”*—is not generally valid for long-horizon embodied planning, where myopic good-looking actions need not imply globally good plans. As a result, the claim in the abstract/introduction that DGAP is “provable to achieve a stronger policy than the LLM planner” is stronger than the paper really establishes for the implemented method.
- **Key mechanism validation is missing.** The paper argues that step-wise scores improve planning quality before execution and reduce unnecessary interaction, but the experiments mostly report end-task success metrics. There is no direct evaluation showing that discriminator scores correlate with actual step quality, task progress, executability, or eventual return on held-out tasks. Figure 5 visualizes score trajectories, but those are DGAP’s own internal scores, not an external validation of the scorer. Since the method’s novelty hinges on the usefulness of these scores, this missing analysis is important.
- **The ScienceWorld empirical case is weaker than the headline suggests.** The overall GPT-4 gain over SwiftSage is modest (84.68→85.91), and DGAP loses or ties on multiple individual tasks. The paper also claims efficiency improvements, but Table 1 reports success rates rather than direct efficiency statistics such as environment steps, replanning rounds, or total LLM calls. Figure 5 is suggestive but not sufficient to substantiate “better efficiency” as a broad empirical conclusion.

### Minor
- **Important design choices lack ablation or sensitivity analysis.** The thresholded replanning rule is central to the algorithm, yet the thresholds are simply set to 5 for ScienceWorld and 6 for VirtualHome “based on their respective training data distributions,” without any sensitivity study. Likewise, the paper does not ablate the data mixture used to train the discriminator (expert / random / offline pseudo-labeled data), the number of replanning attempts, or the number of demonstrations, despite emphasizing limited-demo effectiveness.
- **The discriminator itself is not evaluated as a model.** Given how central it is, the paper should report at least some standalone metrics or diagnostics: regression error, ranking accuracy between better/worse actions, correlation with step success, or generalization under the NovelTasks/NovelScenes settings.
- **ScienceWorld results lack uncertainty estimates.** VirtualHome reports means and standard deviations over repeated runs, but ScienceWorld presents single scores only. Since some improvements are small, uncertainty reporting would help assess robustness.
- **The paper’s discussion of comparative results is occasionally too strong.** For example, in VirtualHome the paper says DGAP “consistently outperforms” competitors, but Table 2 shows Tree Planner is stronger on EXEC in NovelScenes and NovelTasks. The right conclusion is more nuanced: DGAP is better on SR, not uniformly better on every metric.

### Trivial
- **Some technical expressions are imprecise.** For example, the BC objective in §2 is written in a nonstandard way, and Eq. (3) is awkwardly specified since the optimization is over \(\pi_\theta\) while actions are written as sampled from \(\pi^{llm}\). These do not by themselves invalidate the paper, but they add to the theory-method disconnect.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Quantify total inference cost: average LLM calls, replanning rounds, and wall-clock time relative to baselines.
- Add a direct ablation separating the value of the discriminator from the value of closed-loop replanning.
- Analyze failure modes on short tasks, where DGAP is not clearly beneficial.
- Study how performance changes with the number of demonstrations, since “limited demonstrations” is a central claim.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.

- **“LLMs without suffering from OOD problems” is not credible.** This is indeed over-enthusiastic motivation in §1, but it is rhetorical framing rather than a substantive flaw that changes the paper’s validity.
- **Complaint that using fewer demonstrations than the baseline makes ScienceWorld unfair.** The paper states: *“We used around half number of demonstrations as ToT, SFT, and SwiftSage.”* This asymmetry favors the baseline, not the proposed method; under the review rules, this should not be treated as a weakness against the paper. If anything, it makes the comparison more conservative for DGAP.
- **Pure style/formatting complaints and missing-related-work requests.** These are excluded by policy and are not central to the paper’s merit.

## Novel Insights
The paper is more convincing as a **prompt-time heuristic regularization framework** than as a principled learned critic for embodied planning. Empirically, it appears to help by biasing LLMs toward demonstration-like, high-scoring actions and rejecting clearly poor candidates before execution. That is still a useful contribution. But the paper would be stronger if it reframed the method more modestly—as a learned action prior or step-level preference heuristic—rather than claiming validated optimal-action alignment and a policy-improvement theorem for the actual deployed algorithm.

## Suggestions
- Recast the discriminator claims more carefully: call it a learned heuristic score for action desirability/alignment with demonstration-like behavior unless you can externally validate stronger semantics.
- Add a direct validation that higher discriminator scores predict better environment outcomes at the step level.
- Separate the effects of (i) the discriminator, (ii) thresholded rejection/replanning, and (iii) demonstration budget with ablations.
- Report direct efficiency metrics: average environment steps, number of replans, total LLM queries, and inference cost.
- Tone down the theory claim unless you can more tightly connect the prompt-based thresholded algorithm to the KL-regularized objective.
- Include threshold sensitivity and discriminator generalization analysis, especially for NovelTasks/NovelScenes.
- Provide uncertainty estimates for ScienceWorld.
