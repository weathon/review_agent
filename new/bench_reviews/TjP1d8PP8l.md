## Summary

The paper proposes DGAP (Discriminator-Guided Action Optimization), a framework for improving LLM-based embodied planning by training a discriminator on a limited set of demonstrations (augmented with LM-generated data) to provide step-wise scores for LLM actions. Actions scoring below a threshold trigger replanning, and action-score pairs are fed back as process-supervised prompts. The paper connects DGAP to a KL-regularized RL objective and evaluates on ScienceWorld and VirtualHome benchmarks, showing improvements over baselines like Reflexion and SwiftSage.

## Strengths

1. **Principled idea of step-wise discriminator feedback for LLM planners.** The core motivation — that outcome-level feedback is too sparse for long-horizon embodied tasks, and that a learned step-level scorer can bridge LLM commonsense with domain-specific grounding — is well-motivated and timely. This addresses a genuine gap in the embodied planning literature, where most feedback mechanisms operate at the trajectory level.

2. **Theoretical connection to KL-regularized optimization.** The formalization in §3.3 connecting DGAP to a critic-regularized objective (Eqs. 3–6) and the derivation of Lemma 3.1 / Corollary 3.2 provide conceptual grounding for why step-wise scoring should improve planning, even though implementation deviates from the analyzed objective (see weaknesses). The RLHF analogy in the comparison paragraph is informative.

3. **Practical applicability to API-based LLMs.** Unlike RLHF or fine-tuning methods, DGAP operates entirely through in-context prompting with a lightweight discriminator, making it compatible with closed-source models like GPT-4. This is a meaningful practical advantage.

4. **Strong empirical performance in key settings.** The consistent improvements over SwiftSage on longer-sequence ScienceWorld tasks (e.g., Overall: 85.91 vs. 84.68 for GPT-4, 82.96 vs. 76.43 for Llama3) and across VirtualHome settings (e.g., NovelTasks SR: 72.17 vs. 52.33) demonstrate genuine gains, particularly on complex, multi-step tasks where step-wise guidance matters most.

5. **Diverse data augmentation strategy.** The three-tier training data construction (expert/random/offline with similarity-based scoring) is a creative approach to generating intermediate score labels from scarce demonstrations, even though it has limitations (noted below).

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The theory-practice gap is significant and under-acknowledged.** Section 3.3 presents Corollary 3.2 as proving the updated policy "improves over the LLM planner" (i.e., $Q^{\pi_\theta^*}(s,a) \geq Q^{\pi^{\text{llm}}}(s,a)$). However, three disconnects undermine this claim: (a) The analyzed objective (Eq. 4) maximizes **cumulative** return $R_\phi$, but the discriminator is trained on and provides **single-step** scores. The paper itself concedes this gap in a single sentence ("DGAP… prompted to generate actions that maximize the single-step return rather than the cumulative return") but then proceeds as if the result applies. (b) The actual algorithm does not sample from the KL-regularized distribution $\pi^{\text{llm}}(a|s)\exp(R_\phi/\beta)/Z(s)$; it uses threshold-based replanning with in-context prompting. There is no mechanism to enforce the KL constraint. (c) The corollary assumes the discriminator provides accurate rewards, which is not validated (see point 2). The paper's introduction states DGAP "is provable to achieve a stronger policy than the LLM planner" — this overclaims what the theory actually establishes.

2. **The discriminator's training signal is partly self-referential, undermining the "expert alignment" framing.** The offline data $\mathcal{B}_o$ — which constitutes the bulk of the training set — uses scores derived from cosine similarity between BC-model-generated candidates and oracle actions (or the BC model's own first candidate in unseen scenarios). For unseen scenarios, "we mildly treat the first candidate generated by the LM as 10" (§3.1). This means the discriminator is partially trained to recognize whether an action resembles what the BC model would produce, not necessarily what an expert would do. The paper frames $D_\phi$ as measuring "alignment between LLM actions and the underlying expert one" (Eq. 1), but for a substantial portion of training data, the "underlying expert" is the BC model itself. No analysis is provided showing that discriminator scores actually correlate with oracle quality or environment rewards on held-out tasks.

3. **No ablations isolating the mechanism.** DGAP combines three components: discriminator scoring, process-supervised prompting (showing past action-score pairs), and threshold-based replanning. The paper does not provide any ablation testing: (a) whether replanning alone (without score-guided prompting) achieves similar gains; (b) whether random or heuristic scores (instead of learned discriminator scores) provide similar improvements; (c) whether simple best-of-N rejection sampling with the discriminator achieves comparable results. Without such ablations, it is impossible to attribute the improvements specifically to the learned discriminator rather than to the replanning loop or the additional structure in the prompts.

### Minor

4. **"Few-shot" framing is misleading about total supervision cost.** The paper repeatedly emphasizes "a small number of demonstrations" and "limited demonstrations" (Abstract, §1). While the expert portion $\mathcal{B}_e$ is small (10–30 trajectories per task), the discriminator also trains on random negatives $\mathcal{B}_r$ and a large volume of offline LM-generated data $\mathcal{B}_o$, which the paper describes as constituting "a significant proportion of the overall dataset." The total data budget for the discriminator is therefore substantially larger than the "few-shot" framing suggests.

5. **Threshold $\tau$ selection is unvalidated.** The replanning threshold $\tau$ is set to 5 for ScienceWorld and 6 for VirtualHome, "based on their respective training data distributions" (§3.2), but no sensitivity analysis, automatic tuning procedure, or justification for these specific values is provided. Since this threshold directly controls when replanning triggers, the method's generalization to new domains depends on a manually tuned hyperparameter.

6. **The heuristic scoring for unseen scenarios is a strong assumption.** Assigning score 10 to the first beam-search candidate from the BC model in scenarios without ground truth assumes model confidence correlates with optimality. This is a common assumption in self-training, but no justification or error analysis is provided for how often this assumption is violated and how it affects downstream discriminator quality.

7. **Missing efficiency quantification.** The paper claims "better efficiency" (Abstract, §5) but only provides qualitative evidence (Fig. 5 trajectory plots) and no table of average steps-to-completion or LLM API calls per task across methods. While fewer environment interaction steps is suggested, the replanning loop likely increases LLM queries, and this trade-off is not quantified.

### Trivial

- No standard deviations are reported for ScienceWorld results (Table 1), while VirtualHome results (Table 2) include them.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation studies** separating the contributions of discriminator scoring, structured prompting, and replanning. A "replan-only" baseline and a "random-score" baseline would clarify what drives improvements.
- **Discriminator calibration analysis**: a scatter plot of discriminator score vs. environment reward or task success rate, showing whether higher scores actually predict better outcomes.
- **Comparison with best-of-N sampling** using the discriminator as a filter, which would be a simpler baseline for the same discriminator investment.
- **Sensitivity analysis** on $\tau$ and the number of demonstrations.
- **Cross-benchmark generalization** of the discriminator (e.g., train on ScienceWorld, test on VirtualHome).

## Novel Insights

The paper's most insightful contribution is the empirical observation that step-wise discriminative guidance is particularly beneficial for longer-horizon tasks (where compounding errors are most damaging) while offering less advantage on short tasks. This aligns with the theoretical intuition from compounding error analysis in imitation learning (Ross et al., 2011), but the paper does not explicitly connect this empirical pattern to that theory. The finding that the Llama3 variant sometimes outperforms GPT-4 on specific task categories (circuit tasks) is an interesting observation about the complementarity of different LLM backbone capabilities in embodied settings, though it remains under-analyzed.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a discriminator validation experiment**: Report the correlation between discriminator scores and environment rewards on held-out trajectories. This is the single most important missing piece for establishing that the discriminator functions as a valid critic.

2. **Run targeted ablations**: At minimum, compare (a) DGAP vs. replanning with random/heuristic scores, and (b) DGAP vs. discriminator-only rejection sampling (no replanning). This would isolate whether learned scores matter and whether the closed-loop replanning is necessary.

3. **Temper the theoretical claims**: Either restrict Corollary 3.2 to explicitly state its assumptions (cumulative return, accurate critic) and acknowledge the single-step approximation in practice, or remove "provable" claims from the abstract and introduction. The conceptual connection to KL-regularized optimization is valuable without overstating it.

4. **Report step-count and API-call statistics**: Quantify the efficiency claim with numbers, not just trajectory plots.
