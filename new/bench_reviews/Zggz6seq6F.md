## Summary

This paper proposes FIOVA, a video captioning benchmark featuring 3,002 videos (avg. 33.6s) with five independent human annotations per video, and introduces FIOVA-DQ, an event-based metric with human-derived event weights. The authors evaluate six small open-source LVLMs and analyze model behavior across disagreement-based subgroups, concluding that current LVLMs omit information and adopt overly uniform strategies on complex videos.

## Strengths

- **Substantial multi-annotator data collection.** FIOVA contains 15,010 human-written captions, which is a genuine improvement over single-annotator benchmarks. The long-form annotations (avg. 63 words) and longer videos address a real limitation in prior work (Table 1, Section 2.1).
- **Conceptually interesting disagreement-based analysis.** Partitioning videos into subgroups (A–H) by annotator coefficient of variation and comparing human vs. model consistency trends is a sound idea that could yield insight (Section 2.2, Figure 7).
- **Transparent disclosure of LLM usage.** The paper openly states that GPT-3.5-turbo is used for caption quality scoring, groundtruth synthesis, and event extraction (Sections 2.2, 2.3, 3.2).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The core “human” baseline is synthesized by GPT-3.5-turbo, and the entire evaluation pipeline is LLM-mediated.** The paper’s central claim is to evaluate whether LVLMs “can describe videos as comprehensively as humans” and to establish “a robust baseline that represents human understanding comprehensively for the first time.” Yet the groundtruth against which all models are scored is generated by GPT-3.5-turbo from the five human annotations (Section 2.3), the disagreement groups (A–H) are defined using GPT-3.5-assigned quality scores (Section 2.2), and FIOVA-DQ relies on GPT-3.5-extracted events (Section 3.2). Because the reference and grouping are both LLM-mediated, the evaluation does not directly compare LVLMs to human understanding; it compares smaller LVLMs to GPT-3.5’s distillation of human captions. This circularity seriously undermines the paper’s thesis.
- **FIOVA-DQ is unvalidated and underspecified.** The metric is presented as capturing “human cognitive weights,” but the paper never explains how annotators assigned importance scores to events (Section 3.2: “assigned a weight based on its average importance across the five annotators”). There is no correlation study showing that these weights predict human judgments of caption quality, nor is the weighting protocol reproducible from the main text. Without such validation, FIOVA-DQ is an unverified composite of LLM outputs and opaque human data.
- **Model selection is too narrow to support broad conclusions.** The evaluation includes only six small open-source models (7B–8B parameters, released in 2023–2024). The abstract and conclusions make general claims about “current LVLMS” and “state-of-the-art LVLMS,” but no strong closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini) or larger open models are tested (Section 3.1). The evidence cannot support sweeping statements about the state of the field.
- **Missing inter-human baseline against the synthesized groundtruth.** The synthesized groundtruth aggregates five human perspectives, making it unnaturally comprehensive compared to any individual caption. The paper never reports metric scores (BLEU, AutoDQ, FIOVA-DQ) for individual human annotators against this aggregated reference. Without this baseline, claims that models “omit information” relative to humans are unsupported—individual humans might also score poorly on recall against this super-human reference.

### Minor
- **Overstated novelty in Table 1.** The caption claims FIOVA is “the only dataset that provides multiple annotations for each video.” This is inaccurate for well-known manual captioning datasets such as MSR-VTT and MSVD, which include multiple captions per video from distinct annotators.
- **Subgroup analysis on the most complex videos lacks statistical support.** Group H contains only nine videos (Section 4.3). While the paper primarily discusses trends across all eight groups, conclusions about the most challenging content are drawn from a tiny sample without confidence intervals or significance tests.
- **Uncontrolled evaluation setup.** Models are evaluated with different temperatures (0, 0.1, 0.2, 1.0) and all receive only 8 frames for ~33-second videos (Section 3.1). The paper neither justifies these choices nor ablates their impact, leaving open whether performance differences stem from capability or evaluation setup.

### Trivial
- Figure 2 uses two-letter theme abbreviations without a legend in the main text; the legend is deferred to Table A1.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Evaluate stronger closed-source or large open models (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro) to substantiate broad claims about the field.
- Conduct a human validation study correlating FIOVA-DQ with human judgments of caption quality on a held-out subset.
- Report metric scores for individual human annotators against the synthesized groundtruth to establish a true human baseline.
- Replace LLM synthesis with human-curated groundtruth (e.g., human editors consolidating annotations) to remove circularity from the human-machine comparison.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:
- **Floor-effect confound in Group H.** The harsh critic claimed the paper ignores that model consistency on hard videos reflects shared failure. However, Section 4.3 explicitly acknowledges that “this consistency in models could be due to the similar strategies or shared limitations they employ when describing complex scenarios, leading to more uniform outputs,” and notes the marked decline in Recall for Group H. The paper does address this confound.
- **Missing formal definition of FIOVA-DQ in the main text.** The critic noted the metric is illustrated but not formally defined. Since the parser strips appendices and the paper may include formal definitions there, this criticism is deferred.
- **Typos, grammar, and formatting artifacts.** These are parser errors, not author errors.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The multi-annotator, long-form design and the disagreement-based subgroup analysis are the paper’s main conceptual contributions. However, the insight that model consistency increases on hard videos while human consistency decreases is confounded by the LLM-mediated grouping and the small sample size in Group H, limiting its reliability.

## Suggestions

- Have human editors (not GPT-3.5) create a subset of consolidated groundtruth captions to validate whether the LLM synthesis introduces systematic bias.
- Add an inter-human baseline: compute BLEU/AutoDQ/FIOVA-DQ for each of the five annotators against the synthesized groundtruth to contextualize model scores.
- Clarify in the main text exactly how annotators provided event importance scores for FIOVA-DQ, or validate the weights with a post-hoc human ranking study.

**Reasoning:** FIOVA sits in the same quality band as Prometheus and Wolf (4.5–4.75). The raw data collection is a real contribution, but the benchmark architecture and analysis suffer from fundamental issues: the “human” baseline is LLM-synthesized, the new metric is unvalidated, the model selection is too narrow for general claims, and the missing inter-human baseline makes key conclusions unsupported. These issues are more severe than those in DreamBench++ (6.0) and Vinoground (5.75), and comparable to the LLM-reliance concerns in Prometheus (4.5) and Wolf (4.75). The paper needs substantial redesign—human-curated groundtruth, human-validated metrics, and stronger model coverage—before it can support its core thesis.
