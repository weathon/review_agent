## Summary

HARDMATH introduces a benchmark dataset of 1,466 graduate-level applied mathematics problems requiring asymptotic approximation techniques (primarily the Method of Dominant Balance), with algorithmically generated problems and solutions verified against numerical ground truths. The paper demonstrates that even strong LLMs (o1-mini: 62.3%, GPT-4: 43.8% with 5-shot CoT) perform substantially worse on these problems than on established benchmarks like MATH and GSM8K, and provides fine-grained error analyses highlighting how LLMs fail on multi-step approximation reasoning.

## Strengths

- **Targets a genuine and important gap in LLM benchmarks**: The paper correctly identifies that existing math benchmarks (MATH, GSM8K, GHOSTS) focus on exact or formal reasoning, leaving approximation/asympotic reasoning — a core scientific skill — underrepresented. This is a well-motivated and novel contribution (Section 1, Section 2).

- **Algorithmic generation with numerical verification is a strong methodological contribution**: The pipeline generating problems via SymPy, verifying solutions against numerical ground truths within a 10% error threshold, and rejecting invalid problems (Figure 2, Section 3.2) enables arbitrary scalability while maintaining quality. This is a unique advantage over manually curated datasets like GHOSTS (190 problems) or ARB (34 problems) (Table 1).

- **Strong discriminative power**: Even o1-mini (which achieves 90.0% on MATH-500) reaches only 62.3% overall, and GPT-4 drops from 92.0% on GSM8K to 43.8% — a large, consistent gap demonstrating the benchmark is genuinely challenging (Table 2, Section 4.3).

- **Fine-grained error analysis provides actionable insights**: Figure 4 shows that 5-shot CoT shifts GPT-4's error distribution on Roots from "incorrect dominant balance" (66.1%) to "missing dominant balance cases" (50.8%), indicating partial understanding rather than complete failure — a meaningful diagnostic insight (Section 4.4).

- **Public dataset and generation code**: The GitHub repository supports reproducibility and community extension, and the generation framework theoretically enables arbitrary dataset scaling.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Evaluation metric ambiguity undermines interpretability of headline results**: Table 2 reports "Evaluation Accuracy (percentage)" but Section 4.1 describes a "combined protocol of automatic final answer assessment and procedural LLM-based grading" applied to Roots, ODEs, and Integrals. The paper never specifies whether the reported "accuracy" incorporates partial credit weights from the LLM grader, how the two scoring protocols are combined, or how to reconcile the "correct/partial/incorrect" breakdown in Figure 3 with the single accuracy numbers. Figure 3 shows substantial "partial" components, meaning Table 2 numbers could be significantly inflated relative to strict binary accuracy. Since MATH and GSM8K use strict equivalence scoring, cross-benchmark comparisons become ambiguous. This does not invalidate the paper's core finding (models clearly struggle), but it makes the quantitative results hard to interpret and compare.

- **Scope narrower than framing claims**: The title and abstract frame HARDMATH as covering "challenging problems in applied mathematics," but all problems use the Method of Dominant Balance across a limited set of equation forms (polynomials εx^n₁ ± x^n₂ ± 1, integrals of ∫1/(ε + P(x))dx, Laplace integrals, third-order ODEs). Section 3.1 explicitly states: "one key commonality between all HARDMATH problems is the use of the Method of Dominant Balance." While coherent as a focused contribution, the broad framing overstates coverage. Reframing as "asymptotic approximation reasoning" rather than "applied mathematics" would be more accurate.

### Minor

- **Cross-benchmark comparisons use asymmetric prompting conditions**: GPT-4's MATH result uses 0-shot CoT (72.2%), GSM8K uses 5-shot CoT (92.0%), and HARDMATH uses 5-shot CoT (43.8%). Different prompting conditions across benchmarks make direct numerical comparison inexact. However, the performance gap is large enough that the qualitative conclusion remains sound.

- **LLM-based procedural grading alignment is asserted but not quantified**: Section 4.1 states that "LLM-based grading is closely aligned with human grading" based on manual verification of a subset, but reports no quantitative agreement metric (e.g., Cohen's kappa). For a benchmark paper, this gap reduces confidence in the evaluation pipeline's reliability.

- **Template-based generation raises diversity questions**: Problems are generated by sampling coefficients and exponents into fixed templates (Section 3.2, Figure 2). The paper provides no analysis of inter-problem diversity — whether solutions within a type differ meaningfully beyond coefficients. While different problem types (Roots vs. ODEs vs. Integrals) involve genuinely different reasoning paths, within-type diversity is undocumented.

- **Limited open-source model baselines**: Only Llama3-8b and CodeLlama-13b represent open-source models, with no math-specialized variants (e.g., MathStrainer, Qwen2-Math). While this is a dataset paper and model coverage will naturally expand, stronger open-source baselines would strengthen discriminative claims.

## Novel Insights

The paper reveals a specific failure mode hierarchy in LLM mathematical reasoning: when faced with asymptotic approximation problems, models first fail at identifying the correct dominant balance (the most fundamental step), and with increased prompting, they shift to missing balance cases (an intermediate understanding) rather than achieving full correctness. This progression from "wrong setup" → "incomplete coverage" → "correct" is more nuanced than simple right/wrong evaluation and suggests that LLMs can learn the approximation schema but struggle with the combinatorial completeness required across multiple regimes. This insight about partial success in mathematical reasoning is a genuine diagnostic contribution beyond what accuracy metrics alone reveal.
