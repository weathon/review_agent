## Summary

This paper proposes Curve Line Fitting (CLF), a neural network architecture that replaces MLP's linear transformations + activation functions with piecewise quadratic Bézier curve regression. Each input dimension is independently fitted by a segmented quadratic curve parameterized by control points, and the output is a sum of per-dimension predictions. A multi-layer extension introduces parent–child relationships between dimensions to model interactions. The paper claims that removing activation functions makes the model "fully interpretable" and demonstrates it on synthetic distributions and MNIST.

## Strengths

- **Clean and complete parametric description**: Equations (1)–(3) provide a self-contained specification of the model. The midpoint-based C¹ continuity construction for piecewise quadratics is elegant, and the ToQuadraticList conversion shows that each segment reduces to a simple ax²+bx+c form, making forward passes efficient.

- **Per-dimension visualization with noise identification**: Figure 3 demonstrates that a single-layer CLF can recover individual functional forms (e.g., x₁→0.01x₁³, x₂→3sin⁵(x₂)) and correctly identifies a noise dimension as a flat curve. This is a genuine interpretability advantage over unstructured MLPs for additive decompositions.

- **Training stability and local optimization**: Table 4 shows CLF deviations of 0.07%–0.21% vs. MLP deviations of 1.46%–5.78%, with no CLF retraining needed. Equation (2) shows that only 2–3 parameters per dimension are updated per sample, providing locality and computational efficiency for the forward pass (one quadratic evaluation per dimension).

- **Honest reporting of limitations**: The paper clearly acknowledges generalizability issues on MNIST (Table 5: CLF 2-L test accuracy 94.97% vs. MLP 784-480-10 at 97.92%) and shows degradation from incorrect dimension grouping in Table 3.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The single-layer CLF is a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) with quadratic spline basis functions, but the paper never acknowledges this extensive literature.** The model ŷ = Σ fᵢ(xᵢ) with piecewise quadratic fᵢ is a GAM with quadratic spline components (Hastie & Tibshirani, 1990). The multi-layer extension with pairwise interactions corresponds to GA²M models (Lou et al., 2013) or Neural Additive Models (Agarwal et al., 2021). The paper cites none of these and instead claims novelty ("no existing network architecture has been based solely on this approach," line 23). This is not a minor citation gap — it means the most relevant baselines (GAMs, NAMs, spline regression) are entirely absent from experiments, making it impossible to assess whether CLF offers any advantage over the established methods that solve the same problem under the same additive assumption.

- **The "fully interpretable" claim is overclaimed for the multi-layer architecture.** The multi-layer CLF produces conList ∈ R^{N·seg^layer·(seg+2)}, meaning the number of curves grows combinatorially with depth. With seg=10 and 2 layers, each child dimension has 100 separate curves. The paper shows interpretability only for 2–3 dimensional synthetic examples where ground truth is known (Figures 3–4). No user study, interpretability metric, or realistic demonstration shows that humans can extract non-trivial insights from this combinatorial explosion of curves. The "fully interpretable" claim (Abstract, lines 15, 37, 265) holds for the simple additive case but is unsubstantiated for the multi-layer case that is needed to capture interactions.

- **Experimental evaluation lacks meaningful baselines and real-world demonstration of advantage.** All experiments except MNIST use synthetic distributions that exactly match CLF's architectural assumptions (additive decompositions or hand-specified multiplicative pairs). The MLP baselines on MNIST are deliberately tiny (784-10 achieves only 92.37%) and the only fair MLP comparison (784-480-10 at 97.92%) substantially outperforms CLF (95.67%). There are no comparisons with GAMs, NAMs, decision trees, or symbolic regression on any benchmark. The taxonomy task is 2D with 3 classes. Without demonstrating advantage on standard interpretable-ML benchmarks or real-world tasks, claims of practical utility are unsubstantiated.

### Minor

- **CLF+ is introduced in Table 5 but never defined.** The paper reports results for "CLF+" in the MNIST experiment but provides no methodological description of how it differs from standard CLF or why it improves generalization. This is a gap for one of the few real-world experiments.

- **The "no backward function" framing overstates the contribution.** Equation (2) computes Pᵢ ← Pᵢ + (y − ŷ) · ∂ŷ/∂Pᵢ, which is standard gradient descent. The gradient is trivially computed by hand only because the computational graph is so shallow (single layer per dimension). This is not a fundamental property distinct from backpropagation — it is simply what backpropagation computes for a shallow model.

- **The grouping heuristic Relation(i,j) = Cov(l_{:,i}, ŷ_{:,j}) lacks theoretical motivation.** The direction of parent–child relationships is ambiguous (Cov is symmetric), which critically affects performance as shown in Table 3. The paper does not address when this heuristic succeeds or fails, and all multi-layer experiments use hand-specified correct/incorrect groupings rather than testing discovered groupings.

- **The Floater (1992) citation for "approximate almost any shape" is misleading.** That result concerns high-degree Bézier curves, while CLF uses only quadratic segments. No theoretical characterization of the approximation capacity of piecewise quadratics with the specific midpoint continuity is provided.

### Trivial
None.

## Novel Insights

The paper implicitly demonstrates an interesting trade-off between parametric locality and interpretability: by enforcing that each input dimension is modeled by an independent set of piecewise quadratics with local updates, the model gains per-dimension visualizability but loses the ability to learn distributed representations that enable generalization in high-dimensional settings. The MNIST results make this explicit — CLF can memorize (99.97% train) but cannot generalize (94.97% test) as well as a modest MLP (97.92% test). This highlights that the "fully interpretable" property comes at a cost to generalization that the paper only partially acknowledges.

## Suggestions

- Add explicit comparison with at least one GAM baseline (e.g., pyGAM or Explainable Boosting Machine) on the synthetic datasets and on a standard tabular benchmark.
- Define CLF+ explicitly in the method section, even if briefly, to make the MNIST results evaluable.
- Soften the "fully interpretable" claim to "per-dimension interpretable for additive models" and acknowledge that multi-layer interpretability remains an open challenge.
- Test the Relation(i,j) grouping heuristic on a problem where true interactions are not known a priori, rather than only with hand-specified groupings.
