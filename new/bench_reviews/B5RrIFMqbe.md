## Summary

The paper introduces **FORMALALIGN**, a framework that jointly trains a language model on autoformalization sequence generation (cross-entropy loss) and informal–formal representational alignment (contrastive loss) to produce an automated alignment evaluator. It reports experiments on four synthetic benchmarks—FormL4-Basic, FormL4-Random, MiniF2F-Valid, and MiniF2F-Test—where each positive pair is augmented with 21 synthetic negatives generated by deterministic syntactic perturbations. The authors claim that FORMALALIGN outperforms GPT-4 in Alignment-Selection (AS) and precision, and that it can significantly reduce manual verification effort.

---

## Strengths

- **Dual-loss training is rigorously ablated.** Table 5 shows that the combined cross-entropy + contrastive loss outperforms each ablated configuration on all four datasets (e.g., 66.39% AS vs. 52.45% CE-only and 36.07% CL-only on MiniF2F-Valid), demonstrating that the two components are complementary.
- **Inference-time score decomposition is validated.** Table 6 confirms that the combined score $\mathcal{V}_{\text{align}}$ (Eq. 5) is necessary: using only certainty or only similarity collapses performance on MiniF2F-Valid (53.69% and 20.49% respectively), while the combined score reaches 66.39%.
- **Strong precision improvements over GPT-4 on synthetic data.** Per Table 3, FORMALALIGN achieves 93.65% precision on FormL4-Basic versus GPT-4’s 42.68%, and 68.58% versus 59.85% on MiniF2F-Valid.
- **Cross-architecture generalization.** Table 4 shows consistent AS gains when the same training recipe is applied to Phi2-2.7B, LLaMA2-7B, DeepSeekMath-7B, and Mistral-7B.
- **Large efficiency gain over manual verification.** Section 4.4 notes that FORMALALIGN evaluates 80 items in under 2 minutes at 65.00% correctness, compared with roughly 3 hours for human experts at 79.58%.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None.* The underlying methodology is not fundamentally flawed, but the experimental reporting and validation strategy contain serious gaps.

### Major

- **Internal numerical contradictions undermine the central superiority claim.** The abstract and Section 4.3 report GPT-4 AS scores that differ from Table 3. For example, the abstract cites GPT-4 MiniF2F-Valid AS as **64.34%** and FormL4-Basic AS as **88.91%**, whereas Table 3 lists **67.24%** and **90.23%** for those same settings. Section 4.3 repeats the 64.34% figure and further claims GPT-4 scores **68.31%** on MiniF2F-Test, while Table 3 shows **70.82%**. Because the abstract’s headline claim—that FORMALALIGN outperforms GPT-4—relies on the lower GPT-4 figures, the claim is unsupported by the paper’s own table. Per Table 3, GPT-4 actually surpasses FORMALALIGN on AS for three of the four datasets (FormL4-Random: 91.85 vs. 85.85; MiniF2F-Valid: 67.24 vs. 66.39; MiniF2F-Test: 70.82 vs. 64.61). These discrepancies severely damage the paper’s credibility and make the main empirical argument unreliable.
- **Evaluation rests entirely on synthetic perturbations with no validation on real autoformalization outputs.** All negative examples are generated by deterministic token-level mutations (constant swaps, exponent changes, random pairing, etc.; Table 2). Real autoformalization systems produce qualitatively different errors—e.g., misinterpreted quantifiers, omitted hypotheses, or mistranslated relations—yet the paper never evaluates on outputs from any actual autoformalization model. Consequently, the results provide no direct evidence that FORMALALIGN detects the kinds of misalignments that arise in practice, weakening the claim of practical utility.

### Minor

- **The Alignment-Selection metric is decoupled from the realistic detection task.** AS measures the ability to pick the single ground-truth sequence from a pool of 22 candidates (1 positive + 21 synthetic negatives). In practice, an alignment evaluator would more often face threshold-based detection (Precision/Recall) on individual candidates. While the paper does report Precision/Recall, the abstract spotlights AS, which can be artificially inflated by relative ranking among largely dissimilar synthetic negatives.
- **Detection threshold lacks justification.** The threshold $\theta = 0.7$ for Precision/Recall is introduced without derivation, ablation, or ROC-style sensitivity analysis.
- **Human–system accuracy gap is left unexamined.** Section 4.4 reports human accuracy of 79.58% versus FORMALALIGN’s 65.00%—a ~15-point gap—but presents this as a success without discussing the reliability implications of a system that is wrong more than one-third of the time relative to experts.
- **Unjustified averaging of mismatched scales.** The combined score $\mathcal{V}_{\text{align}}$ (Eq. 5) averages $\mathcal{V}_{\text{cer}}$ (in $[0,1]$) and $\mathcal{V}_{\text{sim}}$ (in $[-1,1]$). Table 6 shows $\mathcal{V}_{\text{sim}}$ alone performs near chance on MiniF2F, yet the paper does not explain why a simple unweighted average is appropriate or whether the combined score is dominated by the certainty term.

### Trivial

- **Misleading bold formatting in Table 3.** FORMALALIGN’s MiniF2F-Valid AS (66.39%) is bolded as best despite GPT-4 scoring 67.24% in the same table, reflecting the numerical inconsistency described above.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Evaluate FORMALALIGN on outputs from existing autoformalization models (e.g., GPT-4, Mistral fine-tuned for autoformalization, or published seq2seq models) to validate generalization to realistic errors.
- Expand the misalignment taxonomy beyond syntactic token-level perturbations to semantic error types (e.g., incorrect quantifier scope, omitted preconditions).
- Report statistical variance across multiple runs or bootstrap confidence intervals for the reported point estimates.
