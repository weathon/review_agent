## Summary
The paper proposes **ProtEx**, a retrieval-augmented framework for protein function prediction that conditions Transformer predictions on retrieved exemplar sequences. The method combines homology-based retrieval (BLAST) with a multi-sequence pretraining objective and fine-tuning strategy, achieving state-of-the-art performance across EC number, GO term, and Pfam family prediction tasks. The core contribution is demonstrating that conditioning on retrieved positive and negative exemplars improves both accuracy and robustness, particularly for rare classes and sequences with low training-set similarity. The paper is well-structured, empirically thorough, and includes thoughtful ablations and analysis.

## Strengths
- **Comprehensive and well-controlled empirical evaluation:** ProtEx is evaluated across multiple challenging splits (random/clustered EC/GO, NEW-392, Price-149, PDB, Pfam clustered) with rigorous baseline selection that includes BLAST. Tables 2–5 consistently show state-of-the-art performance, e.g., 0.958 F1 on Clustered EC (Table 2), 0.862 at 50% identity on PDB (Table 4), and 92.6% family accuracy on Pfam (Table 5), surpassing both homology-based and neural baselines.
- **Strong ablation confirming the retrieval conditioning hypothesis:** Removing exemplars causes severe degradation across all tasks (e.g., Pfam accuracy drops from 92.6% to 76.3% in Table 5), providing clear evidence that the improvement comes from exemplar conditioning rather than model capacity alone. This directly supports the paper's central claim (§1, §3).
- **Thoughtful experimental design addressing distribution shift:** The analysis of geometric/uniform sampling in §4.5 and Figure 6 demonstrates rigorous attention to aligning training and inference exemplar distributions. Uniform sampling achieves the highest family accuracy (91.85%) on Pfam, correctly mitigating the <25% similarity constraint between train and test splits.
- **Effective handling of rare classes:** Figure 5 shows ProtEx maintaining ~0.90 accuracy even for Pfam families with only 1 training example, while ProtENN, ProtTNN, and Top pick HMM degrade significantly. This is a meaningful practical contribution for annotating tail distributions in biological databases.

## Weaknesses

### Major

### Minor
- **Retrieval ceiling limits the scope of "generalization" claims.** The paper's candidate label set $\hat{\mathcal{L}}_x$ is defined as the union of labels from BLAST retrievals (§3.1, line 195: "We determine the set of candidate labels, $\hat{\mathcal{L}}_x$, as the union of the labels corresponding to sequences in $\mathcal{N}_x$"). ProtEx cannot predict any label the retriever fails to surface, meaning gains are primarily about false-positive suppression and fine-grained discrimination within the retrieved candidate set, rather than expanding predictive coverage to truly out-of-distribution labels that lack retrieved homologs. The paper partially addresses this in §5 by framing retrieval as a design choice open to future improvement, but the introduction's language about "generalization to unseen classes" and "out-of-distribution function discovery" (§1, line 21-22) suggests a broader capability than what the pipeline structurally supports.

- **Unseen-label results do not clearly demonstrate in-context learning advantage.** Table 6 shows ProtEx achieving higher Micro-F1 on unseen labels (0.970 EC) than on seen labels (0.960 EC), with BLAST showing a similar trend (0.964 > 0.953). If the model's strength were specifically in meta-learned in-context reasoning to new labels, a measurable gap favoring ProtEx over BLAST on unseen labels would be expected, or at least a non-inversion. The result is more consistent with the retrieval step dominating the metric and the micro-averaged F1 being insensitive to the removed label subset. The paper interprets this as evidence that ProtEx "performs comparably or better than BLAST even on classes it has not seen during training" (§4.5, line 454), but the setup withholds fine-tuning labels rather than retrieval access, conflating two different notions of "unseen."

- **Pretraining ablation does not fully isolate the proposed objective's contribution.** Table 7 shows sequence pair w/ score (0.958), sequence pair without score (0.956), single sequence (0.952), and no pretraining (0.912). The primary gain is from pretraining itself (+0.040 over no pretraining), while the proposed similarity-score prediction target adds only +0.006 over pair MLM without scores. The paper claims this target is critical for teaching the model to "implicitly compare sequences" (§3.2, line 247), but the ablation does not include pair MLM without Levenshtein score prediction to isolate whether the specific similarity target is necessary or mere pair exposure is sufficient. The marginal additional gain weakens the centrality of this design choice.

### Trivial
- **Qualitative computational cost discussion.** Section 5 acknowledges that running a 220M parameter Transformer $|\hat{\mathcal{L}}_x|$ times per query creates a throughput bottleneck for proteome-scale annotation, but provides only qualitative justification ("improvements in accuracy and robustness can justify an increase in computational cost," line 480). Wall-clock time, throughput (sequences/sec), or GPU-hour comparisons against single-pass baselines would strengthen the practical framing, though this is not a fatal omission for a methods paper where accuracy is the primary objective.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Oracle retrieval experiment:** Running ProtEx with a perfect retriever (using test-set homologs) versus a retrieval-only baseline that simply transfers BLAST scores would quantify the neural conditioner's contribution independent of retrieval quality.
- **Failure case analysis:** Examples where ProtEx corrects a BLAST false positive versus where ProtEx fails despite correct exemplar retrieval would demonstrate whether the model learns functional boundaries or mimics retrieval scores.
- **Candidate label distribution statistics:** A histogram of $|\hat{\mathcal{L}}_x|$ across test sets for EC vs. GO would contextualize the computational burden and probability of label ceiling effects in highly multi-labeled regimes.
- **Mechanistic interpretability:** Attention weight visualizations or gradient-based saliency maps showing whether the model attends to conserved functional residues across query/exemplar pairs would strengthen the biological plausibility of the "comparison" mechanism.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:
- **"Structural contradiction invalidates generalization narrative":** The harsh critic frames the retrieval dependency as invalidating the entire paper's core claim. However, the paper explicitly scopes its contribution to improving classification within a retrieved candidate set (§5, line 482: "we focused on training and inference procedures that can effectively condition predictions on retrieved exemplars, using a general-purpose Transformer architecture and standard methods for retrieval"). The paper does not claim to generate labels without any homolog retrieval. The criticism is partially valid as noted above (overstated introduction language), but inflating it to "invalidates the narrative" misreads the paper's actual scope.
- **PR curve zoom accusation:** The critic claims Figure 4's precision-recall curves "are zoomed into the [0.8, 1.0] range, which obscures absolute performance differences." However, precision-recall curves inherently focus on the high-precision regime for classification tasks, and this is standard visualization practice for imbalanced or high-accuracy settings. Not a meaningful weakness.
- **"Paradoxical" unseen-label interpretation:** While the unseen-label inversion is worth noting (as included above), calling it "paradoxical" and claiming it "indicates a fundamental misalignment between the evaluation protocol and the claimed in-context learning mechanism" overstates the case. The result is consistent with BLAST's inherent strength and micro-averaged F1's insensitivity to small label subsets, and the paper's claim about adaptation is narrower than the critic implies.
- **"Conceptual mismatch" with NLP terminology:** The critic argues ProtEx improperly borrows "meta-training" and "in-context learning" terminology from NLP because it's "a fine-tuned binary classifier, not a generative LM." This is a definitional argument. The paper uses these terms analogously (conditioning on exemplars to adapt to new tasks without fine-tuning), which is a valid conceptual parallel in the ML literature. Not a substantive weakness.

## Novel Insights
The paper's central insight—that retrieval-augmented conditioning can effectively combine the precision of homology search with the nuanced discrimination of neural models—is well-executed but not fundamentally novel to the broader ML community (retrieval-augmented learning has been extensively explored in NLP and vision). The true novelty lies in the specific adaptation to protein function prediction: the multi-sequence pretraining objective that teaches cross-sequence similarity awareness and the careful handling of train-test distribution shift via exemplar sampling. These are incremental contributions to computational biology rather than paradigm-shifting ones, but they are empirically validated across a comprehensive benchmark suite.

## Suggestions
1. Tone down the introduction's "generalization to unseen classes" and "out-of-distribution function discovery" language to accurately reflect that the candidate labels are retrieval-limited. Reframe claims around "improved discrimination within homolog-retrieved candidates" to avoid reviewer confusion.
2. Add a controlled ablation comparing sequence-pair MLM with vs. without the Levenshtein similarity target to isolate the contribution of the specific pretraining objective.
3. Include quantitative inference cost metrics (wall-clock throughput, GPU hours) in an appendix to help practitioners evaluate the trade-off between accuracy gains and computational overhead for proteome-scale annotation.
4. Briefly analyze why unseen-label performance does not drop relative to seen labels (e.g., per-label F1 variance, class distribution statistics) to clarify whether this supports or weakens the in-context learning interpretation.
