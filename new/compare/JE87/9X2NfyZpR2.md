# Review

## Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to Long-Term Action Anticipation (LTA) using only video transcripts for supervision, bypassing the need for costly frame-level annotations. The proposed model, TbLTA (Transcript-based LTA), employs an encoder-decoder architecture that utilizes dense pseudo-labels generated by a temporal alignment module to guide action segmentation and anticipation predictions. The transcripts are leveraged not only for weak supervision but also to enrich video features through cross-modal attention, providing a more holistic understanding of the video content. Experiments on benchmarks such as Breakfast, 50Salads, and EGTEA demonstrate that TbLTA achieves competitive performance, showcasing the effectiveness of transcript-based supervision in the LTA task.

## Strengths
1. This paper presents a novel approach to Long-Term Action Anticipation (LTA) by utilizing only video transcripts for supervision, which is a significant departure from traditional methods that rely on dense frame-level annotations. This innovation addresses a critical need for more scalable and cost-effective solutions in action anticipation tasks.

2. The proposed TbLTA model employs an encoder-decoder architecture that effectively integrates temporal alignment and cross-modal attention mechanisms. This design enables the model to capture both fine-grained long-range temporal relations and global contextual relationships, which is essential for accurate action anticipation.

3. The authors have conducted comprehensive experiments on multiple benchmarks, including Breakfast, 50Salads, and EGTEA, providing a robust evaluation of their approach. The results demonstrate that TbLTA achieves competitive performance, outperforming some existing methods in certain scenarios, which validates the effectiveness of transcript-based supervision.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational efficiency and scalability of the proposed method. Given that the approach relies solely on video transcripts, it would be beneficial to discuss potential limitations in scenarios with complex or lengthy video content. An assessment of the model's scalability to larger datasets or real-world applications would strengthen the paper.

2. While the paper presents results on three benchmarks, it would be valuable to see how the method generalizes to other datasets or more diverse action anticipation scenarios. Additionally, a comparison with a broader range of state-of-the-art methods, particularly those that also use weak supervision or unsupervised approaches, would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method's advantages and limitations.

3. The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed approach. For instance, the authors could address potential challenges in scenarios with high temporal complexity or where transcripts may not fully capture the nuances of the actions being anticipated. A critical analysis of these limitations would provide a more balanced perspective and guide future research directions.

## Questions
1. How does the computational efficiency of TbLTA compare to fully supervised methods that use dense frame-level annotations? Are there specific scenarios where the use of video transcripts significantly reduces computational costs, and how does this impact the model's practical applicability?

2. Can the authors provide more details on the scalability of their approach? How well does TbLTA perform when applied to larger datasets or more complex video content? Are there any specific limitations or considerations when scaling the method to real-world applications?

3. How does the performance of TbLTA compare to other weakly supervised or unsupervised methods in action anticipation? Have the authors considered comparing their approach to methods that use alternative forms of weak supervision, such as partial annotations or unsupervised learning techniques?
