# Review

## Summary
This paper investigates the distinguishability of reference lists generated by large language models (LLMs) from human-curated ones. The authors construct paired citation graphs using 10,000 focal papers from SciSciNet, along with ground truth references and GPT-4o-generated references. They compare two feature sets for discrimination: (i) structure-only node features (degree, closeness, eigenvector centrality, clustering, and edge count), and (ii) 3072-dimensional title/abstract embeddings. Using Random Forest (RF) classification on graph-level aggregates, they find that structure alone barely separates GPT-generated references from ground truth (accuracy ≈ 0.60), while embeddings significantly enhance separability (accuracy ≈ 0.83).

## Strengths
1. The paper presents a novel investigation into the distinguishability of LLM-generated reference lists from human-curated ones using citation graphs and semantic embeddings, addressing an important question in the field.
2. The authors conduct a thorough comparison of structure-only node features and embeddings, providing insights into the limitations of topology alone for discrimination.
3. The paper is well-written and clearly structured, with detailed methodology and robust experimental design.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper's focus on parametric knowledge limits the generalizability of the findings, as LLMs' citation practices may differ significantly when accessing external databases. Including experiments with models that have access to external databases would enhance the applicability of the conclusions.
2. The paper primarily focuses on title/abstract embeddings, which may not capture the full semantic content of the papers. Incorporating full-text embeddings in future work could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the semantic fingerprints.
3. While the paper effectively separates LLM-generated references from ground truth using embeddings, it does not adequately explain the underlying reasons for the discriminability. A deeper analysis of the semantic features driving separability would provide valuable insights into the implications of LLM-generated bibliographies.

## Questions
1. How do the findings generalize to other LLMs not included in the study, such as those with different training data or architectures?
2. What are the practical implications of the findings for automated literature-review tools and citation recommendation systems? How can the results inform the development of such systems?
3. How do the findings relate to existing work on LLM-produced bibliographies? Are there any contradictions or synergies with previous studies?
