# Expected Review Outcome:

## Summary

The paper studies lossy common information in the Gray–Wyner network and proposes a learnable three-channel codec that separates common and task-specific information for pairs of tasks. On the theory side, the authors derive bounds relating Wyner’s and Gács–Körner lossy common information through interaction information (Theorem 1), and they show how the classical Gray–Wyner optimization $T(\alpha_1,\alpha_2;D_1,D_2)$ can be rewritten as an entropy-based objective amenable to neural optimization (Theorem 2). On the practical side, they design a “Shared” Gray–Wyner-style architecture (Figure 2) with learnable entropy models and an objective (Eq. 12) parameterized by $\beta$ that trades off transmit vs. receive rate, and they evaluate it on a synthetic dataset, colored-MNIST edge cases, and two real multi-task settings (Cityscapes and COCO), comparing against Joint and Independent coding baselines and architectural ablations.


## Strengths

1. **Clear information-theoretic framing and nontrivial theory.**  
   The paper firmly grounds the problem in lossy Gray–Wyner theory and common information. Theorem 1 (pp. 4–5, Appendix A) extends Wyner’s lossless result to the lossy setting by bounding $K(X_1,X_2;D_1,D_2)$ and $C(X_1,X_2;D_1,D_2)$ via extrema of interaction information $I(X_1,X_2;\hat Z_1;\hat Z_2)$. The derivation in Appendix A is detailed and checks out at a high level: the use of interaction information identities (Eqs. 22–24) and the careful exploitation of Markov constraints (Eqs. 43–48) is technically solid and clarifies when the two lossy CI notions coincide.

2. **Principled objective bridging theory and learnable codecs.**  
   The move from Gray–Wyner’s abstract functional $T(\alpha_1,\alpha_2;D_1,D_2)$ (Eq. 9) to the concrete entropy-based objective in Theorem 2 (Eq. 10) and then to the Lagrangian loss with learned entropy models (Eq. 12) is well motivated. Under the deterministic encoder assumption, $I(X_1,X_2;Y_0)=H(Y_0)$, and conditional rate–distortion terms boil down to conditional entropies. Plugging in entropy models via Eq. 11 yields a rate term that is standard in neural compression, yet here is tied directly to a transmit/receive tradeoff parameter $\beta$. The interpretation that $\beta=1$ targets transmit rate $R_t$, $\beta=2$ targets receive rate $R_r$, and $\beta\in(1,2)$ interpolates between $C$ and $K$ (Section 3.2) is conceptually neat and well aligned with Figure 1.

3. **Thoughtful architecture design grounded in a compatibility analysis.**  
   The proposed “Shared” architecture (Figure 2) is not just an ad-hoc design. Section 3.3 and Appendix C introduce a notion of representation compatibility based on generalization error upper bounds via Rademacher complexity (Eqs. 81–92). Theorem 4 connects ResNet depth and Lipschitz properties of convolutional blocks to compatibility: deeper independent ResNets for each representation increase $C_{\mathcal S}$, suggesting that representations generated by largely shared encoders (as in Figure 2) are easier to align than fully separated encoders. While somewhat idealized, this analysis provides a rare theoretical justification for the specific architectural choice, beyond empirical ablations.

4. **Systematic experimental exploration of the transmit–receive tradeoff, including synthetic ground-truth.**  
   The synthetic experiment in Section 4.1 is carefully constructed: $X_1,X_2$ share controlled mutual information (p. 8), and theoretical rate–distortion and mutual information curves are computed via Blahut–Arimoto (Appendix D.3). Figures 3a–3d and 9 (pp. 8–9, 22–24) explicitly visualize how different $\beta$ values change the common-channel rate relative to empirical and theoretical mutual information. For instance, Figure 3a concretely shows codecs trained with $\beta=1$ having $R_0$ above empirical mutual information, while $\beta=2$ yields $R_0$ below it, and $\beta=3/2$ lies in between. This is exactly the behavior one would want from the proposed objective and makes the transmit–receive narrative more than rhetoric.

5. **Good empirical coverage across regimes, including pathological edge cases.**  
   The colored-MNIST experiments (Section 4.2, Figures 4a–4b) are a useful sanity check: they model three PMFs ranging from fully dependent digit–color (all information common) to fully independent (no common information), with a “Mixture” in between. The transmit/receive rate curves in Figure 4 align with expectations: for the Dependent PMF, the method learns to load the common channel (lower $R_t$), whereas for the Independent PMF, the common channel rate is almost zero and Independent coding wins on $R_r$. Table 6 and the channel visualizations in Figure 10 further corroborate that for Dependent PMF, almost all semantic content sits in $Y_0$, and for Independent PMF, the digit and color are separated into different private channels with an essentially empty $Y_0$.

6. **Nontrivial gains over Independent coding on realistic CV tasks.**  
   On semantic segmentation + depth (Cityscapes) and detection + keypoints (COCO), Figure 5a–b and Tables 7–8 demonstrate that the proposed method consistently beats Independent coding in terms of transmit rate at comparable task performance, while getting reasonably close to the Joint one-channel baseline. For example, in Cityscapes (Table 7), at $\eta=0.01$ the Independent codec has $R_t=3.70$ BPP while the proposed has $R_t=2.987$ BPP with essentially matched mIoU and RMSE; the BD-rate improvement vs. Independent in Figure 5a is substantial. Given that Joint coding is usually the de facto design in practice, showing that a three-channel Gray–Wyner-style codec can approach Joint’s $R_t$ while also supporting distributed inference is valuable.

7. **Experimental details and architectural clarity.**  
   The paper provides detailed architectural diagrams for the encoder/decoder and entropy models (Figures 6–8) and tabulates training hyperparameters (Table 1) and per-setting rate–distortion numbers (Tables 2–8). This level of detail, plus discussion of practical issues like the interaction between the auxiliary loss (Eq. 15) and $\beta$ (Sections 3.3 and D.4), makes the work reproducible and helps others adapt the architecture.


## Weaknesses

1. **Some theoretical claims are slightly sloppy or under-discussed, particularly around interaction information bounds.**  
   In Theorem 1 (Eq. 6–7, p. 4), the inequalities are written as  
   \[
   K \le \max_{(\hat Z_1,\hat Z_2)\in\tilde{\mathcal Z}^{(r)}} I(\cdot) \le \min_{(\hat Z_1,\hat Z_2)\in\tilde{\mathcal Z}^{(r)}} I(\cdot) \le C,
   \]
   where the middle “max ≤ min” chain clearly cannot hold as written for a non-empty set unless all values are equal. From the wording in the theorem and the proof in Appendix A, it seems the second “min” should be over the *transmit* set $\tilde{\mathcal Z}^{(t)}$, not the same $\tilde{\mathcal Z}^{(r)}$ as in the first inequality. This looks like a typesetting error but at the moment it makes the statement formally nonsensical. Given that this theorem is used to conceptually justify the idea that there is a “gap” in interaction information between transmit- and receive-optimal tuples (Section 3.1), the paper should fix the statement and add a clear intuitive discussion of when the bounds are tight in realistic continuous settings (the current remark about Gaussian GK CI being zero is helpful but brief).

2. **The deterministic-encoder assumption in Theorem 2 does not quite match the training procedure with quantization relaxation.**  
   Theorem 2 derives $T(\alpha_1,\alpha_2;D_1,D_2)$ as an infimum over deterministic functions $Y_0=f_0(X_1,X_2), Y_1=f_1(X_1), Y_2=f_2(X_2)$, enabling $I(X_1,X_2;Y_0)=H(Y_0)$ and similar simplifications. In practice, the codec uses straight-through quantization (Section 4, Eq. 13) and learns stochastic entropy models $\tilde P(Y_{\{0,1,2\}} \mid \cdot)$ (Eq. 11). The paper implicitly relies on the usual neural compression argument that training with a differentiable approximation to quantization converges to an approximately optimal discrete encoder, but here the objective is nonconvex in $P_{Y_0}$ and explicitly trades off different channel entropies. A short discussion of how the relaxed training objective approximates the theoretical $T(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)$, and whether any bias is introduced in the estimated $R_0,R_1,R_2$, would strengthen the connection between theory and implementation.

3. **Architecture for real CV tasks is somewhat constrained, and baselines are limited.**  
   For the Cityscapes and COCO experiments, the task heads $g_1,g_2$ are frozen pretrained networks attached immediately to the synthesis outputs (Section 4.3, Appendix D.5). As noted by the authors themselves (p. 27), this effectively makes the codec approximate reconstructing JPEG-compressed input images rather than just extracting task-sufficient representations, leading to high bitrates (e.g., all $R_t$ in Tables 7–8 are ≥1 BPP, which is large compared to state-of-the-art learned compressive image representations). Moreover, the baselines considered are only Joint and Independent codecs with the same backbone; prior multi-task learned codecs with shared channels but no private channels are not implemented as baselines, even though they are discussed in Section 2. While the Independent and Joint comparisons establish the Gray–Wyner value within this architecture family, they do not fully situate the method against the broader “coding for machines” or multi-task compression literature.

4. **The role of the auxiliary loss and its interaction with $\beta$ is under-theorized and somewhat ad hoc.**  
   The common-channel composition in Eq. 14 is unusual: $Y_0$ retains an entry only when the quantized tensors $Y_0^{(1)}$ and $Y_0^{(2)}$ exactly match; otherwise that entry is zeroed. This is enforced by the L2 penalty in Eq. 15. The authors note that too small $\gamma$ leads to no matches; too large $\gamma$ leads to degenerate usage of the channel; and their practical fix is to fix $\gamma=1$ and “reduce $\beta$ when necessary” (p. 7). However, this is precisely the regime where one would like to interpret $\beta$ as encoding a transmit–receive tradeoff. As it stands, there is no principled guidance on how to choose $\beta$ jointly with $\gamma$, how sensitive the model performance is to these parameters on real tasks, or whether other, smoother matching mechanisms (e.g., learned attention or probabilistic consensus) would behave better. A small ablation varying $\gamma$ and examining $R_0$ and task distortion, or at least qualitative analysis beyond the short discussion in D.4, would help.

5. **Transmit–receive tradeoff is probed deeply only on synthetic data; on real tasks the “tradeoff” reduces to a single $\beta$.**  
   Section 4.1 and Figure 3 / Figure 9 do a good job of systematically sweeping $\beta\in\{1,3/2,2\}$ and visualizing its effect on $R_0$, $R_t$, and $R_r$, tying back to the theoretical picture in Figure 1. However, for the more practically interesting CV experiments (Cityscapes, COCO), all models are trained with $\beta=1$ (Appendix D.5, last paragraph), and the only tradeoff parameter explored is $\eta$ (the rate–distortion Lagrange multiplier). Thus, the empirical part does not actually show that the method can move along a transmit–receive frontier for real tasks, or whether $\beta$ can be tuned to favor distributed inference scenarios. This limits the practical impact of the theory: the reader is left to infer that the same phenomenon “should” hold, rather than observing it.

6. **Missing or limited measurement of “common information quality” beyond raw rates.**  
   The core motivation is to isolate and code the common information between tasks. In the synthetic and MNIST experiments, commonality is measured indirectly through the bitrate of $Y_0$ and visualizations of reconstructions (Figures 3a, 9d–9f, and Figure 10). However, there is no explicit estimate of mutual information between task targets and the common representation (e.g., $I(Z_1;Y_0)$, $I(Z_2;Y_0)$) or an empirical check of the approximate Wyner/GK conditions, even using variational MI estimators. Given that Section 2.1 invests a lot of space into formal definitions of $C$ and $K$, it would be informative to see whether $Y_0$ is truly “common” in the sense that $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ can be recovered from it alone to some degree, or whether most predictive power remains in the private $Y_1,Y_2$. For example, for the Cityscapes experiment, evaluating segmentation/depth using only the common channel would be a straightforward additional curve in Figure 5a.

7. **Scalability to more than two tasks and computational overhead are not empirically addressed.**  
   Section 5 briefly notes that the number of channels would grow exponentially in the number of tasks in a naive extension, and that a “more dynamic architecture might be required.” But there is no concrete plan or small-scale experiment on three tasks to validate feasibility. Moreover, the auto-regressive entropy models conditioned on $Y_0$ (Figure 7) and the dual encoders in Figure 2 introduce non-trivial complexity. The paper does not quantify training/inference time or memory overhead relative to a single Joint codec with task-specific heads. This matters for practitioners: much of the supposed benefit is in distributed inference, but if encoding is significantly heavier, that tradeoff should at least be discussed.

8. **Some exposition and notation choices are confusing or inconsistent.**  
   - In Eq. 1 (p. 3), the Markov conditions assert that each $X_i$ is a Markov blanket for the other task’s target; later Section 3.3 states that, since each encoder sees $(X_1,X_2)$, these conditions are “removed,” but this shift in assumptions could be made clearer.  
   - The notational overloading of $D_i$ and $\tilde d_i$ around Eq. 1–3 is slightly messy: $D_i$ is both a scalar distortion and an expectation of a loss; $\tilde d_i$ appears suddenly with a tilde in Eq. 1, perhaps a typo.  
   - The common information diagram in Figure 1 is helpful, but some labels (e.g., “Trace,” “Separable point”) are never rigorously defined.  
   Tightening these bits of exposition would make the paper easier to follow, especially for readers not already fluent in Gray–Wyner theory.

9. **Evaluation metrics and tradeoffs on real tasks are compressed to sums of task metrics, which can obscure per-task behavior.**  
   In Figure 5, the vertical axis is the *sum* of mIoU and inverse RMSE for Cityscapes and the sum of detection and keypoint mAP for COCO. While this is convenient for BD-rate computation, it hides whether one task is being sacrificed for the other at certain bitrate regimes. The tables (7–8) expose per-task metrics but are only briefly interpreted. For example, in Table 7, at low $\eta$ the proposed method is slightly worse than Joint in mIoU but slightly better in depth RMSE; summarizing only the sum does not reveal that tradeoff. A per-task plot, or error bars over multiple training seeds, would give a fuller picture of how the Gray–Wyner factorization impacts different tasks.


## Potentially Missing Related Work

1. **Butakov et al., “Information Bottleneck Analysis of Deep Neural Networks via Lossy Compression,” 2023.**  
   This work analyzes deep nets via lossy compression in an information bottleneck framework, closely related to the rate–distortion and common-information perspective used here. It should be cited in Section 2 (Previous work) alongside Tishby et al. (1999) and Theis/Ballé, and briefly discussed as a complementary theoretical angle on lossy information flow in deep networks.

2. **Bhat et al., “Task-Aware Information Routing from Common Representation Space in Lifelong Learning,” 2023.**  
   This paper explicitly addresses separation of common vs. task-specific information and task-aware routing in a multi-task / lifelong setting, conceptually similar to the Gray–Wyner decomposition here. It seems directly relevant and should be discussed in the representation learning and “coding for machines” context (Section 2), and perhaps compared qualitatively in Section 5 when discussing dispersive information routing.

3. **Kleinman et al., “Gács-Körner Common Information Variational Autoencoder,” 2022.**  
   Given that Gács–Körner common information is central to this paper’s theoretical discussion (Section 2.1, Eq. 4–5; Theorem 1), this variational autoencoder approach to GK common information is highly relevant. It should be cited in Section 2 and contrasted as an alternative way to operationalize and approximate GK common information, possibly in the discussion around interaction information and separability conditions (Section 3.1). 


## Questions

1. **Can you clarify and correct Theorem 1’s inequality chain?**  
   As noted, Eqs. (6)–(7) currently read as $\max_{\tilde{\mathcal Z}^{(r)}} I \le \min_{\tilde{\mathcal Z}^{(r)}} I$, which is impossible unless all values are equal. I assume the second extremum should be taken over $\tilde{\mathcal Z}^{(t)}$. Please confirm the correct statement and, in the rebuttal, restate Theorem 1 clearly with the correct sets and perhaps a short intuitive explanation.

2. **How robust are your empirical results to the choice of $\gamma$ in Eq. 15?**  
   You fix $\gamma=1$ everywhere and tune $\beta$ and $\eta$. Have you tried varying $\gamma$ (e.g., 0.1, 1, 10) on the synthetic and/or MNIST setups? Does the qualitative “common information saturation” behavior in Figures 3 and 4 persist, and do you observe degenerate cases where $Y_0$ is essentially unused or collapses?

3. **Can you report experiments with $\beta\neq 1$ on at least one realistic CV setting?**  
   Even a small sweep (e.g., $\beta\in\{1,1.5,2\}$) on a subset of Cityscapes or COCO, with curves analogous to Figure 3c–3d (transmit and receive rate vs. summed task metrics), would substantially strengthen the claim that your objective can explore the transmit–receive tradeoff beyond synthetic data. If this is computationally infeasible, please elaborate on why and whether you anticipate any pathologies when $\beta$ moves away from 1 under the fixed-$\gamma$ regime.

4. **Have you evaluated task performance using only the common channel?**  
   In several places you argue that $Y_0$ holds “common information” between tasks. A straightforward check is to run $g_1,g_2$ using only $Y_0$ (with $Y_1,Y_2$ zeroed) and plot the resulting rate–accuracy curves. Do you see reasonable performance for tasks with large mutual information (e.g., color/digit under Dependent PMF, or depth vs. segmentation in Cityscapes), and near-chance performance for Independent tasks? Showing such plots or at least numerical results would give more direct evidence of the semantic content in $Y_0$.

5. **How large is the computational overhead of your entropy models and dual encoders relative to Joint coding?**  
   For Cityscapes and COCO, some rough measurements (e.g., relative FLOPs or wall-clock encoding time per image) comparing Joint, Independent, and the proposed Shared architecture would help practitioners judge the cost of gaining Gray–Wyner flexibility. If overhead is significant, can you comment on potential ways to reduce it (e.g., lighter entropy models, partial sharing of $f_1,f_2$)?

6. **Could you comment on potential extensions beyond two tasks?**  
   You note that the number of channels grows exponentially if one naively extends Gray–Wyner. Are there particular architectures you think are promising (e.g., hierarchical common channels, routing layers), and do your Theorem 2 / Lagrangian formulations extend in a straightforward way to $N$ tasks? A short elaboration would help position this work as a foundation for multi-task codecs rather than a 2-task-only solution.

## Presentation Rating

## Contribution Rating

3: good.  
The paper combines nontrivial extensions of lossy common information theory with a well-designed learnable Gray–Wyner codec, and provides empirical evidence that such a codec can significantly reduce redundant transmission in multi-task settings. It is a meaningful and timely contribution to both information-theoretic representation learning and coding for machines, even if some practical aspects (scalability, baselines) could be further developed.

## Overall Rating

6: Marginally above the acceptance threshold. But would not mind if paper is rejected.  

The work offers a principled and technically solid bridge between classical Gray–Wyner common-information theory and modern learnable codecs, with a well-motivated architecture and a carefully analyzed synthetic setup that convincingly demonstrates the transmit–receive tradeoff. The empirical gains on real CV tasks are consistent and nontrivial, but not transformative, and some aspects (theorem statement clarity, auxiliary loss interaction, limited $\beta$ exploration in practice, and missing baselines) dampen the impact. On balance, the strengths outweigh these weaknesses, and I lean toward acceptance, contingent on clarifying Theorem 1 and improving the discussion around practical choices.
