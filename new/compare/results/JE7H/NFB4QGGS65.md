# Expected Review Outcome:

## Summary

The paper provides a geometric and lattice-theoretic interpretation of GPTQ-style post-training quantization for linear layers in large language models. It proves that, when run in reverse order, GPTQ is mathematically equivalent to Babai’s nearest plane algorithm for the closest vector problem (CVP) on the lattice generated by the (factor of the) Hessian of activations, and derives tight worst-case and relative error bounds in the no-clipping setting. Building on this insight, the authors propose practical no-clipping quantization schemes (HPTQ and SSQR) and optimized CUDA kernels, demonstrating improved perplexity–bitwidth tradeoffs and speedups on Qwen and Llama models.

## Strengths

1. **Clear, nontrivial theoretical identification (GPTQ = Babai)**  
   - The main result, formalized in **Theorem 4**, that back‑to‑front GPTQ is *exactly* Babai’s nearest plane algorithm without basis reduction, is technically nontrivial and carefully argued. The algebraic equivalence in **Appendix C** is long but systematic: they first rewrite GPTQ in terms of cumulative error (**Algorithms 5–6**), then reverse the iteration with a UDU factorization (**Algorithm 7**), and finally match it to Babai in **Algorithm 8** via a Cholesky factor.  
   - The geometric proof in **Section 4.2** and **Figure 2** is particularly illuminating: **Figure 2(a–d)** visually walks through how OBQ’s error propagation is equivalent to projecting onto Babai’s nearest hyperplane, in terms of basis and inverse-basis vectors. This gives an intuitive explanation for why the “greedy” GPTQ update actually implements a structured nearest-plane procedure.

2. **Solid geometric reformulation of quantization as CVP**  
   - **Section 4.1** shows that L2‑output quantization of a linear layer is an instance of CVP on the lattice generated by \(X \operatorname{diag}(s_i)\), with weights becoming coordinates in the lattice basis and outputs becoming target vectors; **Table 1** provides a neat dictionary between quantization variables and CVP concepts.  
   - **Theorem 1** shows that all choices of Hessian factor \(\mathcal{X}\) are equivalent up to rotation, which is a useful observation: it justifies replacing the potentially tall \(X\) with a cheaper square factor \(\mathcal{X}\) without changing the underlying geometry.

3. **Explicit error bounds for GPTQ via Babai’s guarantees**  
   - **Theorem 5** translates Babai’s classical worst-case and relative error guarantees into a *layer-wise* quantization error bound for back‑to‑front GPTQ in the **no-clipping** setting. The bound
     \[
     \|X\operatorname{diag}(s_i) z_i - X w_i\|^2 \le \tfrac{1}{4} (T^{-1}s_i)^\top D (T^{-1}s_i)
     \]
     where \(D\) is the diagonal from an LDL factorization of the permuted Hessian, gives a concrete, data-dependent guarantee rather than an asymptotic approximation factor.  
   - The derivation in **Appendix D.1** is technically sound: they tie the squared norms of unnormalized Gram–Schmidt vectors of the permuted basis \(A_{(i)}\) to the diagonal entries of \(D_{(i)}\), then express \(\mathrm{tr}(D_{(i)})\) as the quadratic form in \(s_i\). The follow‑up **Lemma 7** and **Eq. (63)** show that, under a uniform prior within Babai’s orthogonal hyper-cuboid, the *expected* squared error is exactly one-third of the worst case, which is a nice, interpretable refinement.

4. **Insightful analysis of quantization order and practical ordering heuristics**  
   - **Section 4.5** explains how the quantization/pivot order affects the bound via the trace \(\mathrm{tr}(D)\) of the Hessian’s LDL diagonal under permutation. This both rationalizes GPTQ’s empirical “act-order” heuristic and motivates the proposed “min-pivot” rule (**Algorithm 3**), which greedily chooses the smallest current diagonal in the Schur complement.  
   - The empirical check in **Table 2** (block 18 of Qwen3‑8B) is well-chosen: it shows act-order already reduces \(\mathrm{tr}(D)\) by roughly 35–50% vs naive/random orders on key attention and MLP layers, while min-pivot further tightens the trace slightly. This concretely connects the theory (LDL diagonal trace) to actual Hessian statistics in LLMs, and explains why act-order “just works”.

5. **Practically relevant no-clipping schemes with strong empirical results**  
   - The paper goes beyond theory and introduces two concrete schemes designed to exploit the Babai/GPTQ no-clipping error guarantees in realistic LLM settings:  
     - **HPTQ**: uses a single global scale and Huffman-encodes integer weights, with an entropy‑guided binary search on the scale to hit a target average bitwidth (**Algorithm 11**).  
     - **SSQR**: extends SpQR so that GPTQ quantization is *truly* no-clipping, storing overflow elements as sparse float outliers (\(\Xi\)) and dynamically scaling per group via binary search (**Algorithms 9–10**).  
   - The main Qwen3‑8B results in **Table 3** are compelling:   
     - At around 4.125 bits, HPTQ (9.81 / 13.64 perplexity on WikiText‑2/C4) is slightly better than GPTQ (10.10 / 13.92) and HRTN (9.90 / 13.80), close to BF16 baseline (9.73 / 13.55).  
     - At 3.125 bits, HPTQ (10.34 / 14.23) clearly outperforms GPTQ (12.77 / 15.61) and especially RTN (16.30 / 21.08), and avoids the catastrophic failure regime of HRTN and GPTQ at 2.125 bits.  
     - SSQR also shows a tunable tradeoff: e.g., SSQR‑5% at effective 4.725 bits achieves 10.32 / 14.22, nearly matching HPTQ.  
   - The zero-shot benchmarks in **Table 5** (Qwen3‑8B) further validate that HPTQ and high‑outlier SSQR maintain accuracy close to baseline across MMLU, PiQA, WinoGrande, SciQ, and TruthfulQA, particularly in the 3–4 bit range.

6. **Breadth of experimental coverage, including other models and baselines**  
   - The authors evaluate HPTQ across a spectrum of Qwen3 scales (**Table 4**, bitwidths 4.125, 3.125, 2.125) and show consistent, monotonic degradation as bitwidth decreases, but with 3.125‑bit often staying within a modest factor of BF16 perplexity.  
   - Similar patterns are reproduced on Llama‑3.2‑3B‑Instr, Llama‑3.1‑8B‑Instr, and Llama‑2‑7B (**Tables 11–13**), with HPTQ consistently outperforming GPTQ at equal average bitwidth and avoiding the divergence seen in GPTQ at ~2‑bit regimes.  
   - **Table 16** is particularly interesting: in the 3–4 bit regime on Llama‑2‑7B, HPTQ and SSQR (scalar/group quantization) achieve WinoGrande/PiQA accuracies comparable to vector-quantization baselines like AQLM, QuIP#, and QTIP, closing a gap where vanilla GPTQ is clearly weaker. This strongly supports the claim that lattice‑inspired no-clipping variants are competitive with sophisticated codebook methods.

7. **Efficient CUDA kernel implementation and speedups**  
   - The paper includes a nontrivial engineering contribution: a custom CUDA kernel for SSQR that handles dense low‑bit inliers and sparse FP outliers, with careful layout and batching strategies discussed in **Section E.6**.  
   - **Figure 4(c)** reports end-to-end decoding speedups on Qwen3‑8B on RTX A6000; the method consistently achieves about \(2\times\) speedup over PyTorch BF16 at batch size 1 for 128 generated tokens, across 2–4 bit inlier formats and 0–5% outlier rates.  
   - **Figure 5** further breaks down layer-wise speedups across bitwidths, outlier rates, and batches; low-batch regimes see up to \(4\times\) speedups when outlier rates are low, and the kernel still dominates BF16 even when outliers are denser. This makes the proposed representations directly actionable for deployment.

8. **Clarity of exposition and strong figures**  
   - The paper is generally very well written, especially given the technical content. The combination of intuitive figures and formal math works nicely:  
     - **Figure 1(a–h)** does an excellent job of contrasting optimal Voronoi cells, naive RTN partitions, and Babai’s rectangular cells, and then visually highlighting how basis order affects Babai’s regions. This figure is key to understanding why GPTQ’s order heuristic matters.  
     - **Figure 3** offers a crisp 2D geometric view of OBQ’s dimension selection criterion in Eq. (1), making Corollary 3 intuitive.  
     - **Figure 4(a–b)** plots perplexity versus effective bitwidth, clearly showing 3.125‑bit HPTQ as Pareto-optimal across sizes, which nicely connects the theoretical motivation (no clipping, Babai bounds) to the practical tradeoff frontier.

## Weaknesses

1. **Core guarantees rely on a no-clipping assumption that is only partly addressed empirically**  
   - The theoretical equivalence to Babai’s algorithm and the tight error bounds in **Theorem 5** apply strictly to the regime \(\mathbb{Z}_\dagger = \mathbb{Z}\), i.e., no clipping. Yet much of today’s LLM quantization practice still uses fixed finite grids (e.g., INT4) and clipping, and the original GPTQ algorithm clips aggressively.  
   - While **Section 5** explicitly tackles the no-clipping regime by designing HPTQ and SSQR, the paper does not present any *quantitative* analysis of how the Babai/GPTQ bound degrades when clipping is introduced. There is a qualitative argument that clipping can “violate” the bound, but no characterization of the magnitude of this effect under, say, bounded overflow probabilities or heavy‑tailed weight distributions.  
   - This matters because many of the empirical comparisons in **Table 3** are still with clipped baselines (standard GPTQ, RTN, HRTN), so we see that no-clipping variants work well empirically, but the theory does not tell us how far standard GPTQ is from the CVP oracle in realistic clipped configurations.

2. **Equivalence results are extremely algebra-heavy; some key steps are hard to parse**  
   - While the algebraic equivalence proof in **Appendix C** looks correct at a high level, it is quite dense and uses over‑loaded notation in ways that make it difficult to verify step‑by‑step. For instance:  
     - The introduction of LDL and “UDU” decompositions of \(H^{-1}\) with different triangular factors \(L\) and \(U\), and the back‑and‑forth between them in **Algorithms 5–7**, is conceptually straightforward but the actual chain of equalities in Eqs. (18)–(30) and (48)–(53) is long and fragile; there are many intermediate uses of identities like \(\sum_k e_k e_k^\top = I\) that are easy to misread.  
     - The claim in **Section C.2** that a simple anti-diagonal permutation and “UDU” factorization suffices to reverse GPTQ’s order essentially leverages triangularity; but the text never states explicitly the assumptions on numerical stability or conditioning, even though the practical GPTQ code often requires damping and blockwise processing.  
   - From a reviewer perspective, the core equivalence is believable and well supported by the geometric argument in **Section 4.2**, but the algebraic proof would benefit from a higher-level structural lemma or a clearly stated invariant, instead of continuous index gymnastics. This limits how easily others can extend the proof to clipped or more general settings.

3. **Limited discussion of the impact of damping and Hessian estimation noise on the theory**  
   - In **Algorithm 1**, the Hessian is \(H = P^\top (X^\top X + \lambda I) P\) with damping \(\lambda = \frac{1}{100c} \|X\|_F^2\). The equivalence and error bounds ultimately refer to this damped Hessian and its LDL or Cholesky factors.  
   - However, **Theorem 4** and **Theorem 5** largely talk in terms of \(X^\top X\) and its decompositions without clearly spelling out how the added \(\lambda I\) modifies the underlying lattice and Babai bound. In practice, \(X\) is only estimated using a finite calibration set, so \(X^\top X\) is noisy, and then perturbed further by \(\lambda I\).  
   - There is no sensitivity analysis: for instance, how does the error bound scale as a function of \(\lambda\) or the Hessian’s condition number? In many LLM layers, especially attention projections, Hessians are ill-conditioned; whether Babai’s bound remains numerically meaningful under damped, estimated Hessians is not discussed. A simple perturbation bound or sanity check on how \(D\) behaves under realistic noise would make the theoretical claims more robust.

4. **Quantization order heuristics evaluated only at the bound level, not on actual accuracy**  
   - **Section 4.5** and **Table 2** focus on the trace \(\mathrm{tr}(D)\) as a measure of how good a pivot order is, and **Appendix D.3** states that min-pivot gives “modest but consistent” accuracy gains over act-order. However, the main experimental tables (**Tables 3–4, 11–13**) all use act-order; there is no direct comparison of test perplexity or benchmark accuracy with min-pivot vs act-order for GPTQ, HPTQ, or SSQR.  
   - This leaves a bit of a gap: the theory tells us that pivot order affects the Babai/GPTQ bound, and min-pivot is carefully justified (shortest residual in Gram–Schmidt), but the reader never sees how much this *actually* translates into better performance, even on a single layer or small model. It would be much more convincing to have at least one ablation where a layer or model is quantized with act-order and min-pivot, showing both \(\mathrm{tr}(D)\) and perplexity/accuracy side-by-side.

5. **HPTQ/HRTN comparison lacks a clear separation of benefits from “better coding” vs “better quantization”**  
   - HPTQ replaces RTN with Babai/GPTQ and uses Huffman coding of unbounded integers. Its baseline, HRTN (**Algorithm 12**), uses Huffman coding but with simple RTN quantization. The results in **Table 3** show that HPTQ is consistently better than HRTN, especially at 3.125 bits, but there is no analysis disentangling the contributions of:  
     - the improved lattice-aware rounding (Babai/GPTQ vs RTN),  
     - the use of unbounded integer grids, and  
     - the use of entropy coding vs fixed-width integer formats.  
   - For example, comparing HPTQ at 3.125 bits against a hypothetical “GPTQ + fixed 3-bit grid, no Huffman” baseline would isolate the impact of Huffman. As written, the reader is left to infer that both Babai-style rounding and Huffman coding matter, but cannot quantify how much each accounts for the observed gains.

6. **Scope of “global” contribution is somewhat specialized**  
   - The theoretical results are quite specific: they address *linear* layers, with an L2 objective on the outputs, and error bounds for *back-to-front* GPTQ in the *no-clipping* case. Extending this to non-linear structures, activation quantization, KV‑cache quantization, or cross‑layer interactions is acknowledged only briefly in **Section 6**, without even a simple toy example.  
   - That is acceptable for a focused paper, but it somewhat limits the immediate impact: many recent quantization systems use layer-wise or block-wise mixed precisions, non-uniform codebooks, or training‑time adaptation, where the pure GPTQ/Babai story only partially applies. The paper could benefit from a more candid discussion of which design patterns in modern quantization pipelines fall strictly within its theoretical domain and which lie outside.

7. **Some mathematical exposition is unnecessarily opaque or contains minor slips**  
   - In **Section 4.2**, Theorem 2’s proof compresses many geometric steps into a few dense sentences, switching between 3D and 2D projections, inverse basis vectors \(n_j\), and projection planes. The algebra at the end leading to
     \[
     \Delta \zeta_{j_1} = \frac{(B^\top B)^{-1}[j_1,j_2]}{(B^\top B)^{-1}[j_2,j_2]} \Delta \zeta_{j_2}
     \]
     is correct, but relies on a chain of equalities that is not fully expanded; for such a central identity (the link between Babai projection and Eq. (2)), I would prefer a more explicit derivation.  
   - In **Corollary 3**, the expression for OBQ’s dimension selection contains a slightly confusing notation:
     \[
       j = \arg\min_{j \in J} \frac{(q_i[j] - w_i[j])^2}{(X[:,J] \setminus X[:,J])^{-1}[j,j]},
     \]
     where the denominator seems to intend \((X[:,J]^\top X[:,J])^{-1}[j,j]\). Small notational slips like this make it harder for readers to confirm the math.  

8. **Missing direct discussion of a few closely-related works on geometric and lattice-based quantization**  
   - The related work section focuses appropriately on OBC/OBQ, GPTQ, QuIP, and lattice algorithms, but omits several recent and conceptually nearby papers, especially those using geometric or lattice-based views of quantization and explicit error bounds (see next section). This is not fatal scientifically, but it weakens the positioning and makes it harder to see how the present Babai-based view compares to other geometric approaches.

Overall, the weaknesses mostly concern scope and clarity rather than fatal flaws: the core theoretical and empirical contributions are solid.

## Questions

1. **Effect of damping and Hessian estimation on the Babai/GPTQ error bounds**  
   - All practical experiments use a damped Hessian \(H = X^\top X + \lambda I\) with finite calibration data. Could the authors provide either (a) a theoretical perturbation bound showing how the error guarantee in **Theorem 5** changes when \(H\) is replaced by a perturbed version \(H + E\), or (b) empirical evidence (e.g., a histogram of diagonal entries of \(D\) under different \(\lambda\) and calibration set sizes) that the bound is numerically stable? Clarifying this would increase my confidence in treating Theorem 5 as a meaningful predictor in practical LLM settings.

2. **Quantitative effect of min-pivot vs act-order on actual model accuracy**  
   - **Table 2** shows that min-pivot reduces \(\mathrm{tr}(D)\) slightly compared to act-order, but you only mention “modest” accuracy gains qualitatively. Could you add one or two ablations (e.g., Qwen3‑1.7B or a single layer) where GPTQ/HPTQ with min-pivot vs act-order are compared directly in terms of perplexity and/or zero-shot accuracy? Even if the gains are small, quantifying them would help understand when the more expensive min-pivot is worth using.

3. **Decomposing the benefits of Huffman coding vs Babai rounding in HPTQ**  
   - For HPTQ, can you provide an additional baseline where you fix the bit budget and use GPTQ with a *fixed-width* integer grid (no Huffman), so we can see how much improvement comes from entropy coding vs. improved rounding/no-clipping? Alternatively, a small-scale synthetic experiment where you compare RTN vs Babai vs Huffman vs no Huffman on the same linear layer would help disentangle these factors.

4. **Robustness of bounds and methods under mild clipping**  
   - In practice, fully unbounded integer grids may be impractical at very low bit budgets, and mild clipping is often used. Have you explored approximate extensions of your bound where \(\mathbb{Z}_\dagger\) is bounded but wide enough that overflow probability is small (e.g., modeled via a heavy‑tailed prior on weights as in **Li et al., 2025**)? Even a heuristic bound in terms of the overflow mass might make the theory more relevant for slightly clipped regimes.

5. **Applicability beyond weight-only linear layers**  
   - In **Section 6**, you mention future work on activation and KV‑cache quantization. Could you clarify which parts of the equivalence (e.g., Theorem 4) and error analysis (Theorem 5) carry over directly if we quantize, say, the output of a residual block or the KV cache of attention? Are there any structural obstacles, such as nonlinearity or non-square Jacobians, that would break the exact CVP mapping?

Answers and, where feasible, small additional experiments or clarifications on these points could further strengthen my assessment.
