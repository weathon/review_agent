# Expected Review Outcome:

## Summary

The paper presents a unified transformer-based framework that jointly tackles expressive piano performance rendering (EPR, score→performance MIDI) and automatic piano transcription (APT, performance→score) using shared note-level content representations and a global style latent. The model is trained on both paired and unpaired symbolic data using Seq2Seq objectives plus masked reconstruction, avoiding the need for note-level alignment. On top of the joint model, the authors introduce a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that samples style embeddings conditioned only on the score, enabling automatic style selection and style-aware rendering. Experiments on ASAP and ATEPP show competitive APT/EPR performance, evidence of content–style disentanglement, style transfer, and stylistically consistent styles generated from scores alone.

## Strengths

1. **Well-motivated joint modeling of EPR and APT with disentangled content/style**

   The high-level idea of treating EPR and APT as dual tasks over a shared latent content space (Section 3.2, Figure 1) is conceptually clean and musically meaningful. The architecture with separate score and performance encoders into a common content space, plus a global style encoder and two decoders, is a coherent instantiation of this idea. Training both directions together while sharing the content space is a natural but non-trivial extension of prior work that treats the tasks separately.

2. **Alignment-free Seq2Seq formulation of EPR with competitive results**

   Most existing EPR systems require note-level alignment; here EPR is reformulated as a Seq2Seq generation problem over a structured MIDI tokenization (Section 3.1, Huang & Yang 2020), which sidesteps alignment at training time. Table 2 and Table 3 show that “Ours (Target)” and especially “Ours (PSR)” match or surpass strong alignment-based baselines (VirtuosoNet, DExter) on several objective metrics: for example, in Table 2, Ours (Target) has the lowest KL(V) and MAE(V) among all models, and in Table 3, Ours (PSR) attains the best alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%). This gives credible evidence that the alignment-free formulation does not cripple EPR quality.

3. **Diffusion-based PSR module is a practical, musically interpretable contribution**

   The PSR module (Section 3.4, Figure 1 bottom + Figure 6) addresses a real usability issue: non-expert users typically cannot specify fine-grained style vectors. Modeling \(p(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{e}_g)\) with a DDPM, conditioned on a global score embedding, is a reasonable and flexible approach. The subjective evaluation in Figure 2 and the style-era visualization in Figure 4 lend support: PSR-generated styles yield ratings close to target-style extraction and cluster by historical era similarly to real-performance style vectors, suggesting that PSR captures musically meaningful constraints linking content and style.

4. **Solid, multi-faceted empirical evaluation**

   The paper is empirically dense and uses appropriate metrics:

   - **APT:** MUSTER and ScoreSimilarity with comparisons against several strong baselines (Table 1). The model is competitive with the alignment-free end-to-end baseline Beyer & Dai (2024) and improves several error components (e.g., \(E_{\text{extra}}\), \(E_{\text{const}}\), \(E_{\text{offset}}\)).
   - **EPR (objective):** The use of variance, KL divergence, and MAE (Table 2), plus alignment/insertion/miss rates (Table 3), gives a nuanced view of dynamics and timing behavior instead of just note-level correctness.
   - **EPR (subjective):** Figure 2 shows human listening scores across dynamics, tempo, style, and overall; Ours (Target) dominates the non-human baselines by a clear margin and Ours (PSR) is close behind.
   - **Disentanglement:** Table 4 and Figure 3 evaluate how style and content embeddings support composer/performer classification and cluster visually.
   - **Additional analysis:** Diversity analysis (Appendix D.2, Table 9–11), ablations on unpaired data (Table 12–13), and KL-weight analysis (Table 14) demonstrate some care in understanding behavior.

5. **Good use of figures to connect claims to learned representations**

   - **Figure 1** provides an understandable high-level overview of the architecture and training tasks (score/performance reconstruction, APT, EPR) and shows where \(\mathbf{z}_x\), \(\mathbf{z}_y\), \(\mathbf{z}_s\), and PSR fit into the pipeline. This makes the complex multi-module setup substantially easier to grasp.
   - **Figure 3** and **Figure 4** are particularly informative: both show 2D projections of \(\mathbf{z}_s\). Figure 3 demonstrates clear clustering by composer and by performer in the style space, which aligns with the identification results in Table 4. Figure 4 compares actual-performance style embeddings vs PSR-generated ones colored by era; the similar cluster shapes and centroid positions strongly support the claim that PSR respects stylistic regularities imprinted in the style encoder.
   - **Figure 5** nicely illustrates the style transfer subject study, where the “Target” style receives higher similarity scores without sacrificing quality in two of three samples, corroborating the narrative about a “well-structured” style manifold.

6. **Reasonably clear writing and documented implementation**

   The paper is well structured, mostly precise with notation, and explicit about data (Section 4.1), training setup (4.2), and architecture (3.5, Appendix B). Table 7 quantifies model size and inference speed, which is often omitted in generative music papers. The appendices add substantial reproducibility detail, e.g., vocabularies (Tables 5–6) and the multi-task batching scheme.

## Weaknesses

1. **Disentanglement is only weakly enforced and lacks quantitative disentanglement metrics**

   While Section 3.3 claims disentanglement between \(\mathcal{Z}_c\) and \(\mathcal{Z}_s\), the actual mechanisms are modest: separate encoders, different granularity (sequence vs global vector), and a KL prior on \(\mathbf{z}_s\). There is no explicit adversarial, mutual-information, or orthogonality constraint to prevent leakage of style into \(\mathbf{z}_c\) or content into \(\mathbf{z}_s\); the “encouraged to align” content features (Figure 1, text around Eq. (1)) are not constrained by any explicit alignment loss. The main evidence is downstream classification (Table 4) and 2D visualizations (Figure 3–4), which show that \(\mathbf{z}_s\) carries more performer/composer information than \(\mathbf{z}_c\), but they do not quantify independence or factorization. For instance, no metric such as MIG, SAP, or mutual-information gap is reported, and there is no measurement of how much content information remains in \(\mathbf{z}_s\). This matters because many of the claims in Section 5.2 (“confirming successful disentanglement”) hinge on qualitative observations rather than rigorous disentanglement evaluation.

2. **Mathematical formulation of losses glosses over important implementation details and is partially inconsistent with the appendix**

   - In the main text, Eq. (6) states  
     \[
     \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{EPR}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{APT}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec},\mathcal{X}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec},\mathcal{Y}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}},
     \]
     implying equal weighting of all components. Appendix B.1, however, introduces  
     \[
     \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{APT}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{EPR}}+\lambda_{\text{rec}}(\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec},\mathcal{X}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec},\mathcal{Y}})+\lambda_{\text{KL}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}},
     \]
     with \(\lambda_{\text{rec}}=0.2\) and \(\lambda_{\text{KL}}=0.1\). This discrepancy is nontrivial because the relative scale of reconstruction vs supervised losses and KL regularization materially affects the learned representations and the degree of disentanglement. The main paper should clearly state (and justify) these weights, or at least make Eq. (6) consistent with Eq. (11).
   - Eq. (4) for unpaired performance reconstruction is  
     \[
     \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec},\mathcal{Y}}=\mathrm{CE}(g_{\mathcal{Y}}(f_{c,\mathcal{Y}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}})\oplus f_{s,\mathcal{Y}}(\mathbf{y})),\mathbf{y}),
     \]
     where content is extracted from masked \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) but style is extracted from the *unmasked* \(\mathbf{y}\). This asymmetry is plausible, yet the notation invites confusion: the same \(\mathbf{y}\) appears both in masked and unmasked form, and there is no explanation of how the mask rate interacts with the style encoder. Does \(f_{s,\mathcal{Y}}\) ever see masked inputs, or is it always fed the full sequence? If the latter, then in the unpaired setting the model effectively uses the full ground-truth \(\mathbf{y}\) to reconstruct itself, which reduces the role of \(\mathbf{z}_y\) and complicates interpretation of reconstruction as “self-supervised masking.” Clarifying this is important for understanding the actual regularization effect on content vs style spaces.
   - For the KL term (Eq. (5)), the paper defines \(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{y})\) but never specifies its parameterization (mean/variance, diagonal covariance?), whether it is amortized via the style encoder output, and whether reparameterization is used. Given that the KL is central to regularizing style and the KL-weight ablation in Table 14 is used to argue against posterior collapse, the main text would benefit from making the underlying VAE-like formulation explicit in equations (e.g., define \(\mu_s(\mathbf{y}), \sigma_s(\mathbf{y})\) and \(\mathbf{z}_s = \mu_s + \sigma_s \odot \epsilon\)).

3. **Limited and mildly mixed APT gains compared to the strongest alignment-free baseline**

   While Table 1 shows that the proposed method is competitive with Beyer & Dai (2024), the improvements are not uniformly strong. In fact, the end-to-end baseline achieves better \(E_p\) (2.73 vs 3.08), \(E_{\text{miss}}\) (8.40 vs 8.43), and especially \(E_{\text{extra}}\) (8.95 vs 7.33, where lower is better for the proposed model, so that is a plus), but the headline “comparable to SOTA” hides a nuanced tradeoff. More importantly, the reliance on a single, very recent transformer baseline for alignment-free APT and the absence of an ablation that removes EPR/PSR but keeps the same architecture make it hard to isolate how much of the APT performance is due to the joint modeling vs simply reusing an existing strong Seq2Seq design. For instance, a “APT-only” variant mirroring Beyer & Dai but with the same content encoder and training regime would help decouple architectural differences from the effect of EPR coupling and unpaired data.

4. **Scope and baselines for PSR evaluation are somewhat narrow**

   The PSR module is evaluated mainly via: (a) subjective scores in Figure 2 and Figure 5, and (b) stylistic era clustering in Figure 4. While these are useful, there is no direct comparison to alternative or simpler style-generation methods. For example:
   - A baseline that samples \(\mathbf{z}_s\) from the VAE prior \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) learned in the joint model, or a conditional Gaussian (e.g., \(\mu(\mathbf{e}_g), \Sigma\)), would show whether diffusion is actually needed.
   - There is no quantitative measure of how close PSR-generated styles are to the empirical \(\mathbf{z}_s\) distribution beyond loose visual similarity in Figure 4; e.g., Fréchet distance or KL between era-conditional Gaussians would be informative.
   In its current form, the evaluation shows that PSR is “not terrible” and sometimes stylistically plausible, but does not convincingly demonstrate that it is materially better than simpler stochastic style selection strategies.

5. **Content–style transfer experiments, while promising, are quite limited**

   The style transfer study (Section 5.2, Figure 5) is based on only three samples and a single rating protocol. The results are positive for Samples 1 and 3, but less clearly so for Sample 2 where the “Mean” interpolation seems to perform similarly to or better than the Target style. There is no systematic exploration of failure cases, genre distance (e.g., very different compositional styles), or how robust the transfer is across longer pieces. Given that cross-piece style transfer is one of the core advertised benefits of disentanglement, the current set of three qualitative examples is on the thin side for making strong claims such as “supports flexible style transfer.”

6. **Computational cost and parameter count are high for the reported gains**

   Table 7 shows that the unified model uses 188M parameters, roughly 3× the APT baseline and 36× VirtuosoNet. EPR inference time (10.17 s/sample) is significantly slower than the RNN baseline (0.35 s/sample) and not much faster than the diffusion-based DExter (14.14 s/sample). The APT improvements relative to the end-to-end transformer are modest, and EPR improvements relative to DExter/VirtuosoNet are mixed depending on the metric (e.g., Table 3 alignment is slightly below the Score baseline). The paper acknowledges this overhead but does not really argue for why such a heavy model is justified in contexts where only APT or only EPR is needed. Some discussion of parameter-efficiency (e.g., weight sharing, pruning, distillation) or a smaller model ablation would strengthen the practicality argument.

7. **Some experimental choices and comparisons could be better justified**

   - For EPR objective metrics, the authors note that DExter’s larger variance does not equate to higher quality, but this assertion is mostly based on informal listening. It would help to show in Figure 2 how listeners score DExter vs Ours on the same pieces to back this claim empirically; Figure 2(a)/(b) does include DExter, but the text could more explicitly connect the variance vs subjective-quality discrepancy.
   - The APT experiments are limited to ASAP; no out-of-domain APT evaluation (e.g., real-world noisy MIDI from ATEPP or similar) is presented, despite the claim that alignment-free training may be more robust to ornaments and expressive deviations.
   - The diversity analysis in Appendix D.2 (Tables 9–11) is commendable, but the methodology depends on note alignment between model outputs and human performances. Since the whole pitch of this work is being alignment-free, some discussion of how robust the analysis is to alignment errors would be welcome.

## Potentially Missing Related Work

1. **Hawthorne et al., “Enabling Factorized Piano Music Modeling and Generation with the MAESTRO Dataset”, 2019.**  
   This work introduces MAESTRO as a large-scale dataset coupling scores and performances and explores factorized modeling of piano music. It is directly relevant both as a potential alternative data source for joint EPR/APT and as an example of “factorized” modeling, which is conceptually close to the content/style separation in this paper. It should be discussed in Section 2.1/2.2 and possibly as a future extension dataset, with a brief comparison of representation and supervision regimes.

2. **Hawthorne et al., “Semi-Supervised Learning for Piano Transcription”, 2020.**  
   This paper studies semi-supervised learning for piano transcription with large amounts of unlabeled data. Since the present work leverages unpaired symbolic data and emphasizes gains from unpaired training (Table 12–13), this semi-supervised piano transcription literature is directly relevant and should be cited and contrasted in Section 2.2 and in the discussion of unpaired benefits in Section 5.1 / Appendix D.3.

3. **Hawthorne et al., “Learning Disentangled Representations of Pitch and Timbre for Musical Instrument Sounds Using Gaussian Mixture VAEs”, 2020.**  
   This paper explicitly tackles disentanglement of musical factors (pitch vs timbre) with VAEs. It aligns with the disentanglement goals of Section 3.3 and the use of a Gaussian prior on \(\mathbf{z}_s\). It should be discussed in Section 2.3 as an example of DRL in music, and cited when introducing the KL regularization (Eq. (5)).

4. **Hawthorne et al., “A Universal Music Translation Network”, 2018.**  
   This work performs cross-domain translation among musical modalities (instruments, styles, etc.) and is conceptually related to the “unified cross-modal translation” angle mentioned with Jung et al. (2025). It should be included in Section 2.1 or 2.3 when situating the proposed multi-domain Seq2Seq architecture and the style-transfer capabilities.

## Questions

1. **Clarification of KL parameterization and posterior collapse prevention**

   - How exactly is \(q(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{y})\) parameterized? Does the style encoder output \(\mu_s(\mathbf{y})\) and \(\log \sigma_s^2(\mathbf{y})\) for a diagonal Gaussian, with \(\mathbf{z}_s\) sampled via reparameterization?
   - In Table 14, all settings report AU = 512; can you provide more insight into why AU does not shrink with stronger KL, and whether there are signs of posterior collapse in any dimensions (e.g., dimension-wise KL histograms)?

2. **Content-style leakage and disentanglement metrics**

   - Have you attempted any explicit measurement of content leakage into \(\mathbf{z}_s\) or style leakage into \(\mathbf{z}_c\), e.g., predicting note-level features (pitch/duration) from \(\mathbf{z}_s\) alone, or predicting performer identity from \(\mathbf{z}_c\) beyond the composer/performer classifiers in Table 4?
   - Would you be able to compute a simple disentanglement metric like MIG or SAP on a subset of factors (e.g., composer vs performer vs key vs tempo) using your latent spaces?

3. **Ablations on joint training and unpaired data specifically for APT**

   - Could you provide an APT-only ablation where the EPR-related modules and PSR are removed, but the content encoder/score decoder remains identical? This would help quantify the incremental gain of joint training on APT (beyond what Beyer & Dai already showed).
   - Similarly, Table 12 shows APT improvements from unpaired data; do you have a parallel ablation for EPR metrics (Table 2/3) showing how much EPR benefits from unpaired score/performance data?

4. **Justification for diffusion in PSR**

   - Have you compared PSR to simpler baselines like sampling \(\mathbf{z}_s\) from the learned VAE prior, or a conditional Gaussian (mean + covariance) regressed from \(\mathbf{e}_g\)? If so, how do these alternatives fare in terms of style-era clustering (Figure 4) and subjective ratings (Figure 2, Figure 5)?
   - If such a comparison is not available, what is your main argument that diffusion is necessary here, as opposed to a simpler normalizing flow or parametric distribution?

5. **Alignment between the two content encoders \(f_{c,\mathcal{X}}\) and \(f_{c,\mathcal{Y}}\)**

   - Aside from sharing architectures and being indirectly supervised by their respective decoders, is there any explicit objective that forces \(\mathbf{z}_x\) and \(\mathbf{z}_y\) (for paired samples) to live in the same latent space (e.g., contrastive loss, cycle consistency, or feature matching)?
   - If not, have you observed empirically whether the two encoders produce similar distributions (e.g., by projecting both \(\mathbf{z}_x\) and \(\mathbf{z}_y\) into 2D and comparing) for aligned pairs?

6. **Generalization to out-of-domain EPR/APT**

   - You use ATEPP mainly for representation diagnostics; have you attempted to run APT and/or EPR directly on ATEPP as a test set to measure generalization of the full pipeline? If so, what performance shifts do you observe compared to ASAP?

Clarifications or additional experiments along these lines would significantly increase confidence in the disentanglement claims and in the distinct contributions of joint training and PSR.

## Overall Rating

6: marginally above the acceptance threshold. But would not mind if paper is rejected.

The main limitations are in the rigor of the disentanglement analysis, the lack of stronger baselines or ablations for PSR and joint training, and the relatively heavy model compared to the incremental empirical gains. On balance, however, the paper would likely be of interest to the representation learning and MIR communities and merits acceptance as a solid, if not spectacular, contribution.
