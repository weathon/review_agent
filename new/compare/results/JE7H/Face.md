# Expected Review Outcome:

## Summary
This paper introduces FaceLinkGen, an identity-centric attack framework designed to expose identity leakage in frequency-domain Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition (PPFR) systems. The authors argue that current PPFR evaluations, which primarily rely on pixel-level reconstruction metrics like PSNR and SSIM, are insufficient and misleading. FaceLinkGen demonstrates that identity information can be extracted and regenerated from protected templates with high fidelity, even when pixel-level reconstruction is deemed unsuccessful, thereby revealing a critical gap in the privacy guarantees of several state-of-the-art PPFR methods.

## Strengths
1.  **Novel Perspective on Privacy Evaluation:** The paper's central argument, that pixel-level reconstruction metrics are inadequate for assessing identity-level privacy in PPFR, is a crucial and timely insight. This re-framing of the privacy threat model, moving from pixel-level to identity-level leakage, is a significant conceptual contribution that challenges the prevailing evaluation paradigm in the field. The distinction between $z_I$ and $z_N$ in Section 3 provides a clear theoretical underpinning for this argument.
2.  **Empirical Validation of Vulnerability:** FaceLinkGen provides compelling empirical evidence that several recent frequency-domain PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace) are vulnerable to identity leakage. The high matching accuracy (over 98.5%) and regeneration success (over 96%) across multiple datasets, as shown in **Table 2** and **Table 4**, are striking and effectively demonstrate the severity of the identified vulnerability.
3.  **Simplicity and Generality of the Attack:** The attack's design, leveraging a straightforward distillation pipeline and off-the-shelf generative models (Arc2Face), underscores the inherent vulnerability of the protected templates rather than relying on complex, highly specialized adversarial techniques. This simplicity, as highlighted in Section 3, makes the findings more impactful, suggesting that the issue lies in the representation itself. The ability to generalize to non-frequency-domain methods like CanFG and de-identification systems like TIP-IM (Table 8) further strengthens this point.
4.  **Comprehensive Evaluation Metrics:** The use of commercial face verification APIs (Face++, Amazon) for assessing regeneration success, alongside traditional metrics and cosine similarity distributions, adds robustness to the evaluation. This multi-faceted approach, detailed in Section 5.3 and **Table 5**, helps to mitigate concerns about potential biases from using a single verification system or the same embedding model for both attack and evaluation.
5.  **Insightful Analysis of Soft Biometric Leakage:** The paper extends its analysis beyond hard identity to soft biometrics, demonstrating that attributes like age, gender, and race can also be inferred from protected templates with high accuracy, as presented in **Table 9**. This highlights a broader privacy concern often overlooked in PPFR research and provides a more holistic view of identity leakage.

## Weaknesses
1.  **Lack of Theoretical Rigor in Information-Theoretic Claims:** While the paper posits a compelling argument about $z_I$ and $z_N$ in Section 3, the formalization remains largely conceptual. The statement "$T \sim p(\cdot \mid z_I)$" (Equation 1) is a strong claim that implies complete removal of $z_N$ and perfect preservation of $z_I$. However, the paper does not provide any information-theoretic bounds or formal proofs to support this ideal mapping or to quantify the mutual information retained. Without such a rigorous analysis, the theoretical foundation for why $z_I$ *must* persist in $T$ in a learnable form, even when $z_N$ is discarded, remains an intuition rather than a proven property. This weakens the claim that the vulnerability is solely "in the representation itself."
2.  **Reliance on Specific Pre-trained Models and Commercial APIs:** The attack heavily relies on ArcFace embeddings and Arc2Face for generation, and commercial APIs (Face++, Amazon) for verification. While the authors argue this avoids using the same model for attack and verification, it introduces a dependency on external, black-box systems whose internal workings and biases are not fully transparent. The performance of FaceLinkGen is thus intrinsically tied to the capabilities and limitations of these specific models and APIs. For instance, the discrepancy in regeneration success between Face++ and Amazon API in **Table 7** (e.g., 94.6% vs 47.3% for FracFace) suggests that the choice of verification system significantly impacts the perceived success rate, raising questions about the generalizability of the "near-total identity recovery" claim.
3.  **Limited Scope of Attacked PPFR Methods:** The paper focuses exclusively on three frequency-domain PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace). While these are presented as "mainstream," the PPFR landscape is broader, encompassing cryptographic methods, differential privacy, and other transformation techniques. The conclusion that "pixel-level reconstruction metrics may not adequately capture identity-level leakage in the frequency-domain PPFR systems *we evaluated*" (Section 11) is accurate but the generalization to *all* PPFR systems, or even all transformation-based systems, is not fully justified. The brief evaluation of CanFG and TIP-IM is a step in this direction but lacks the depth of analysis applied to the core three methods.
4.  **Ambiguity in "Oracle Access" Threat Model:** The threat model in Section 2 states the adversary has "oracle access to the conversion process (the ability to query it with arbitrary inputs and observe outputs), but no knowledge of its internal architecture or parameters." However, the training of the student model (Equation 2) requires paired $(t_k, i_k)$ data, implying the attacker can generate protected templates $t_k$ from original images $i_k$. This "oracle access" is a strong assumption. While the paper attempts to address this with a "Minimal-Resource Attack" (Section 5.1) and "Assumption-Constrained Attack" (Section 6), the initial threat model's implications for data acquisition are not fully elaborated, particularly how an attacker would obtain a sufficiently diverse public dataset of *paired* original images and protected templates without significant effort or privileged access.
5.  **Insufficient Discussion of Countermeasures and Robustness:** While Section 10, "Future Directions," briefly touches upon cryptographic hardening and alternative de-identification strategies, the paper's primary focus is on demonstrating vulnerability. A more in-depth discussion of potential countermeasures, or how existing PPFR methods could be modified to resist FaceLinkGen, would significantly strengthen the paper. For example, could adversarial training or more robust information-theoretic obfuscation techniques be integrated into PPFR systems to specifically counter this type of identity-centric distillation attack? The current suggestions are rather high-level and do not directly address how to make the *current* frequency-domain methods more robust.
6.  **Visual Examples in Figure 1 and Figure 3 Lack Quantitative Context:** While **Figure 1** provides qualitative examples of regenerated faces, it lacks quantitative metrics (e.g., Face++ scores) directly beneath each example to substantiate the visual claim of "regeneration success." Similarly, **Figure 3** visually compares U-Net, StyleGAN, and FaceLinkGen attacks, but the "Face++ error rate" for StyleGAN is given as ">1e-3" while for "Our ID-Based Attack" it's "<1e-5". These are thresholds, not direct scores, and a more precise quantitative comparison (e.g., average similarity scores or FARs for these specific examples) would make the visual argument more rigorous. The visual quality of the regenerated faces in **Figure 1** is good, but without direct comparison to the original images' identity features, it's hard to fully gauge the "identity consistency" purely from visual inspection.
7.  **The "Minimal-Resource Attack" (Section 5.1) is not fully convincing:** The claim that "identity information is not meaningfully disrupted by the transformation, but rather preserved in a form that remains easily extractable" is strong. However, the minimal resource attack, while impressive, still uses a subset of CASIA-WebFace (800 images, then 256 images). This is still a non-trivial amount of data for an attacker to acquire *paired* with protected templates, especially if the PPFR system is truly black-box. The paper needs to clarify how these paired samples are obtained under the "constrained scenario" more rigorously, beyond "leaked samples, known identities, or attacker-controlled accounts." The practical feasibility of obtaining such paired data for *any* target identity remains a critical question for the real-world applicability of this "minimal-resource" attack.
8.  **Mathematical Notation for Loss Function (Equation 2):** The loss function is defined as $\mathcal{L}=1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}s\big{(}f_{s}(t_{k}),f_{t}(i_{k})\big{)}$. While $s(\cdot, \cdot)$ is stated to be cosine similarity, it's not explicitly defined mathematically. For a paper at ICLR, it would be beneficial to explicitly state the cosine similarity formula, i.e., $s(u,v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|u\| \|v\|}$, to ensure full clarity and avoid any ambiguity, especially given the critical nature of the loss function in training the student model.

## Potentially Missing Related Work
1.  **Song, Z., Wang, G., Yang, W. (2025): *Privacy-Preserving Method for Face Recognition Based on Homomorphic Encryption*** — This paper presents a method utilizing homomorphic encryption to protect facial data during recognition processes. It is directly relevant as it offers a fundamentally different approach to PPFR compared to the frequency-domain methods attacked in this paper. It should be discussed in Section 1, "Introduction and Related Works," to broaden the scope of PPFR techniques considered and to contrast the security guarantees of cryptographic methods with those of transformation-based approaches. It could also be referenced in Section 10, "Future Directions," when discussing cryptographic hardening.
2.  **Chamikara, M., Bertok, P., Liu, D. (2020): *Privacy Preserving Face Recognition Utilizing Differential Privacy*** — This work proposes a technique for controlled information release in face recognition using differential privacy. Differential privacy is a strong privacy notion that aims to provide formal guarantees against various attacks, including identity leakage. This paper should be discussed in Section 1, "Introduction and Related Works," to position the current work within the broader context of formal privacy guarantees in PPFR, and to highlight how differential privacy might offer stronger protection against identity-centric attacks compared to the evaluated methods.

## Questions
1.  Could the authors elaborate on the practical feasibility of obtaining the paired (original image, protected template) data required for training the student model, especially in the "Minimal-Resource Attack" scenario? While "leaked samples" are mentioned, how realistic is it for an attacker to acquire 256 or 800 such pairs for arbitrary target identities, particularly if the PPFR system is truly black-box and deployed by a cautious service provider?
2.  The paper highlights the discrepancy in regeneration success rates between Face++ and Amazon API in **Table 7**. Could the authors provide a more detailed analysis or hypothesis for this difference? Does it suggest that some commercial APIs are more robust to AI-generated images, or that the identity signal is weaker for certain PPFR methods when evaluated by different systems? This could impact the interpretation of "near-total identity recovery."
3.  Given the strong claims about the inherent vulnerability residing "in the representation itself," what specific modifications or design principles would the authors propose for future frequency-domain PPFR systems to make them genuinely resistant to FaceLinkGen-like attacks, beyond the high-level suggestions in Section 10? For example, how could the information quantity of $z_I$ be provably suppressed while maintaining recognition utility?
4.  For **Equation 2**, please explicitly define the cosine similarity function $s(\cdot, \cdot)$ for mathematical completeness.
5.  The paper mentions that ArcFace embeddings retain soft biometric information. Could the authors provide a more detailed explanation or reference for *why* ArcFace embeddings, designed for identity discrimination, also preserve these attributes, and how this impacts the design of privacy-preserving systems?
