# Expected Review Outcome:

## Summary

The paper introduces IncentRL, a reinforcement learning framework that augments extrinsic rewards with an internal KL-based incentive term between a predicted outcome distribution \(p(o \mid s,a)\) and a preferred distribution \(q(o \mid s)\). The incentive weight \(\beta\) is claimed to be treated as a Bayesian random variable, supposedly adapted online to balance extrinsic vs intrinsic signals without manual tuning. The authors provide simple theoretical observations about the effect of \(\beta\) and present experiments on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar-v0, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8x8, along with some plots (Figures 3 and 4, Table 3) illustrating the behavior of the Bayesian adaptation scheme.

## Strengths

1. **Clear conceptual framing and connection to cognitive/neuro inspiration**  
   The idea of aligning predicted and preferred outcome distributions via a KL term is intuitively appealing and well-motivated in the introduction and Section 3.2. The connections drawn to dopamine-based reward prediction error and the Free Energy Principle (Sections 1, 2.3, 6.3) are conceptually coherent and may be interesting to a subset of the ICLR community exploring cognitive or neuroscience-inspired RL.

2. **Simple and transparent shaping formulation**  
   The shaped reward in Section 3.2,
   \[
   r^{\text{total}}(s,a) = r^{\text{ext}}(s,a) - \beta \,\text{KL}(p(o\mid s,a)\| q(o\mid s)),
   \]
   is easy to understand and can be plugged into many standard RL algorithms. Proposition 1 and 2 in Section 4, while elementary, do at least articulate that for small \(\beta\) the optimal policy is preserved and for large \(\beta\) preferences dominate, which is conceptually clarifying.

3. **Empirical evidence that *some* KL shaping can help in sparse reward tasks**  
   Table 1 and **Figure 1** show that in the toy 2-state MDP, any \(\beta>0\) significantly improves convergence speed and reduces variance of Q‑learning compared to \(\beta=0\). In MiniGrid Doorkey 8x8, **Figure 2** indicates that with \(\beta=0.01\) the final success rate improves from \(90.5\%\) to \(98\%\), and the mean episode length drops from about 75 to 29, which is a noticeable behavioral improvement. These figures support the basic claim that a modest KL-based shaping term can help exploration in sparse-reward settings.

4. **Initial attempt to adapt \(\beta\) rather than tune it by hand**  
   Although underspecified (see weaknesses), the paper at least *attempts* to move beyond fixed coefficients by introducing a Bayesian adaptation story for \(\beta\). **Figure 3** and **Figure 4**, together with **Table 3**, show some posterior dynamics and sampled-\(\beta\) distributions that qualitatively indicate concentration of probability mass over time. This is an interesting direction in principle, since balancing intrinsic vs extrinsic terms is a well-known nuisance in intrinsic motivation methods.

## Weaknesses

I see multiple substantial issues in technical clarity, missing core details of the main claimed contribution (Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation), methodological rigor, and empirical evaluation. I list them in some detail because they materially affect the scientific value and credibility of the work.

1. **Core “Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation” contribution is essentially undefined and non-operational in the main paper**

   The abstract, introduction, and contributions list heavily emphasize “treating the incentive weight \(\beta\) as a Bayesian random variable, updated online to balance exploration and exploitation without manual tuning”. However, the main body never gives:

   - A prior \(p(\beta)\) or its parameterization.  
   - A likelihood \(p(\mathcal{D}\mid\beta)\) or any surrogate used to connect \(\beta\) with observed returns, TD errors, or KL statistics.  
   - An explicit posterior update rule, learning objective, or even a high-level algorithmic description of how \(\beta\) is sampled or updated during RL training.

   Sections 3 and 4 contain *no* mathematical specification of the Bayesian scheme, and Section 5 only says “we tracked the posterior evolution of \(\beta\) using our Bayesian adaptation scheme” and then jumps directly to **Figure 3**, **Figure 4**, and **Table 3**. There is no pseudo-code, no equation, and no definition of what “rounds” are, how data from previous rounds is summarized, or what posterior family is used. Without those, the claimed main novelty is impossible to reproduce and essentially functions as a black-box heuristic rather than a scientific contribution. This is, in my view, the single most serious weakness.

2. **Ambiguous and underspecified modeling of outcome distributions \(p(o\mid s,a)\) and \(q(o\mid s)\)**

   The method hinges on computing \(\text{KL}(p(o\mid s,a) \| q(o\mid s))\), but the paper provides almost no concrete implementation detail outside the toy MDP:

   - In the toy MDP (Section 5.2), it is implicitly assumed that \(p(\cdot\mid s,a)\) equals the known transition probabilities, and \(q\) is hand-picked. That is fine for a toy example.  
   - For **MountainCar** (Section 5.3) and **MiniGrid** (Section 5.4), the paper never specifies:
     - What constitutes an “outcome” \(o\): the next state, an extended trajectory, the goal indicator, or some latent embedding?  
     - Whether \(p(o\mid s,a)\) is obtained from a learned forward model, from empirical visitation frequencies, from the environment’s transition probabilities, or approximated by some parametric family.  
     - How \(q(o\mid s)\) is defined in continuous state spaces or high-dimensional grids beyond the informal description “assigns all probability to the goal (x ≥ 0.5)” for MountainCar and “encodes a strong internal preference (‘reach the goal’)" for MiniGrid.

   Section 4.3 mentions possible latent-space modeling (\(z=\phi(o)\), Gaussian assumptions, variational encoders), but this is purely hypothetical and not tied to the experiments. This disconnect between the theoretical setup and empirical implementation makes it very hard to understand what is actually being optimized in the non-toy tasks, which directly affects soundness and reproducibility.

3. **Mathematical formulation of the KL term in practical settings is problematic or at least opaque**

   Using \(\text{KL}(p\|q)\) where \(q\) collapses “all probability to the goal state” (Section 5.3) is mathematically problematic:

   - If \(q(o\mid s)\) is a Dirac at a single outcome \(o^\star\), \(\text{KL}(p\|q)\) is infinite whenever \(p(o^\star\mid s,a) < 1\). The paper does not explain how this is addressed (smoothing, approximate support, truncation, etc.).  
   - In the continuous MountainCar state space, writing “q(o|s) assigns all probability to the goal (x ≥ 0.5)” is not even well-defined unless you define goals over some coarse-grained or binary outcome variable, which is never spelled out.

   Without a clear specification of what the support of \(p\) and \(q\) is, and whether \(\text{KL}(p\|q)\) is numerically stabilized or approximated, the fundamental shaped reward
   \[
   r_{\text{shaped}} = r_{\text{ext}} - \beta \cdot \mathrm{KL}(p(o\mid s,a)\| q(o\mid s))
   \]
   (Equation in Section 5.1 and 5.3) is ill-posed in a strict sense. At the very least, the authors need to define the discrete or continuous outcome variable, the parametric form of \(p\) and \(q\), and the exact expression of KL used in the experiments.

4. **Theoretical analysis is very shallow and almost tautological**

   Section 4 essentially states obvious continuity and scaling properties:

   - **Proposition 1**: For sufficiently small \(\beta\), the optimal policy of the base MDP remains optimal. This relies on the unproven assumption of a positive reward gap and just gestures to “continuity of \(J_\beta(\pi)\)”. No explicit bounds on \(\epsilon\) in terms of the KL term, reward gap, or mixing properties are provided. The “sketch” glosses over key steps: you would need to bound changes in value under a uniform perturbation of the reward and argue that this preserves policy ordering, but no such bound is written.  
   - **Proposition 2**: As \(\beta \to \infty\), the optimal policy minimizes KL and ignores rewards. This is immediate from the objective being dominated by \(\beta\) and is not really a substantive proposition.

   There is no analysis of convergence rates, no characterization of how KL shaping affects exploration in a more formal sense (e.g., in terms of visit probabilities or mixing), no connection to potential-based shaping or Bayes-adaptive MDPs, and crucially no analysis at all of the *Bayesian* adaptation mechanism for \(\beta\). For a paper that advertises theoretical analysis as a contribution, this is weak and feels more like a sanity check than real theory.

5. **Bayesian adaptation figures are inconsistent and not clearly related to performance**

   - **Figure 3** shows the posterior mean of \(\beta\) dropping from around 0.11 to below 0.02 across four rounds, while the caption says the posterior “concentrates near the effective region (\(\beta \approx 0.1\))”. This is self-contradictory: the curve is clearly *moving away* from 0.1, not concentrating around it.  
   - **Table 3** confirms this, with mean \(\beta\) going from 0.1057 in Round 1 to 0.0173 in Round 4. Again, this contradicts the narrative that the posterior remains near 0.1.  
   - It is unclear in which task environment these Bayesian experiments are run, what “round” means operationally (full re-training? episodes? batches?), or how the adapted \(\beta\) affects actual performance metrics like those in Table 2 or Figure 2. The text in Section 5.4: “In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\) achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value” is anecdotal and not backed by any actual results.

   In short, Figures 3–4 and Table 3 do not convincingly support the claim that Bayesian adaptation “concentrates near task-relevant values” or “eliminates manual tuning”. They show some arbitrary dynamics of a hidden heuristic whose definition we do not have.

6. **Experimental evaluation is limited in scope, baselines, and rigor**

   - **Small-scale and few seeds**: For MountainCar and MiniGrid, results are averaged over only 3 seeds; for a noisy RL setting this is weak evidence.  
   - **No comparison to standard intrinsic motivation or shaping methods**: There are no baselines using curiosity, count-based exploration, entropy regularization, potential-based shaping, or more recent adaptive shaping methods. The only comparison is against the same architecture with \(\beta=0\). That is a very weak baseline package given the large literature on shaping and intrinsic motivation.  
   - **Inconsistent and confusing \(\beta\) ranges**:  
     - In MountainCar (Table 2), the best fixed \(\beta\) is 0.1.  
     - In MiniGrid (Figure 2), they use \(\beta=0.01\) vs 0.0, with no exploration of other values.  
     - The Bayesian adaptation experiments (Figure 3–4, Table 3) show \(\beta\) drifting to ~0.02, not 0.1.  
     The narrative jumps between “effective region ≈ 0.1” and “effective value 0.01” without reconciling these or relating them to task differences or scaling of KL magnitudes.
   - **Metrics are somewhat cherry-picked and partially disconnected**: For MountainCar, Table 2 lists “Final Mean Goal Reaches per 5000 episodes” but no learning curves over time, no steps-to-goal, and no statistical tests. For MiniGrid, Figure 2 shows learning curves for only two \(\beta\) values and does not relate them to the Bayesian adaptation story at all.

   Overall, the experimental section provides suggestive but quite weak support for the claimed advantages, especially for the central “Bayesian adaptation removes manual tuning” slogan.

7. **Presentation and notation issues that cause confusion**

   There are multiple inconsistencies and missing clarifications that impede understanding:

   - Section 3.1 describes “outcomes” rather than next states but never clearly defines their granularity or relation to \(s'\) in the MDP.  
   - The paper oscillates between using \(r_{\text{total}}\) (Section 3.2), \(r_{\text{shaped}}\) (Section 5.1), and \(r_{\text{ext}}\) / \(r^{\text{ext}}\) with minor notational mismatches. This is minor but contributes to a sense of sloppiness.  
   - In **Figure 2**, the y‑axis label “Value” combines success rate and mean episode length, which are conceptually different metrics plotted on the same axis. This is confusing and should be disentangled or dual-axis.  
   - The discussion in Section 6.1–6.2 about “KL regularization stabilizing policy updates” drifts toward generic PPO/SAC-style KL-regularized policy optimization, which is not what the method actually does (the KL is over outcome distributions, not policies), so this risks conflating concepts.

8. **Positioning with respect to closely related adaptive shaping and Bayesian RL work is incomplete**

   The related work section focuses on classical curiosity, empowerment, KL-regularized RL, FEP/active inference, and dopamine RPE, but omits several strongly related recent efforts on adaptive reward shaping and Bayesian formulations of preferences and rewards:

   - *Ding et al., 2023, “Bayesian Reparameterization of Reward-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning with Energy-based Models”* explores Bayesian reparameterization ideas around reward conditioning that resonate with treating incentives in a Bayesian manner; it should be cited and contrasted in Sections 2.1 and 4.  
   - *Lidayan et al., 2025, “BAMDP Shaping: a Unified Framework for Intrinsic Motivation and Reward Shaping”* directly casts pseudo-rewards as reward shaping in Bayes-Adaptive MDPs, which is conceptually extremely close to IncentRL’s story about adaptive preferences. This belongs in Section 2.1–2.3 and in the theoretical discussion.  
   - *Ma et al., 2024, “Highly Efficient Self-Adaptive Reward Shaping for Reinforcement Learning”* proposes automatic, success-rate-based adaptive shaping, which is a natural comparison baseline and conceptual neighbor for the “adaptive \(\beta\)” idea; this should be discussed in Section 2.1 and considered as a baseline where feasible.  
   - *Louie, 2022, “Asymmetric and Adaptive Reward Coding via Normalized Reinforcement Learning”* discusses adaptive reward coding and balancing of exploration vs exploitation; it is relevant to the paper’s core objective of adjusting the trade-off between external and internal signals.  
   - *Rothkopf & Dimitrakakis, 2011, “Preference Elicitation and Inverse Reinforcement Learning”* presents a Bayesian perspective on preferences, which is directly in line with “Bayesian adaptation of preference gaps” and should be mentioned in Section 2.3 or 4.3.

   Omitting these works weakens the paper’s positioning and makes the originality of the Bayesian aspect hard to assess.

Collectively, these weaknesses go beyond missing details; they cut into the core claim (Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation), the mathematical well-posedness of the objective, and the persuasiveness of the empirical evidence.

## Questions

Author responses that clarify or address the following points could significantly change my assessment:

1. **Precise Bayesian formulation for \(\beta\)**  
   - Please specify:
     - The prior \(p(\beta)\) used (family, parameters, support).  
     - The likelihood or surrogate objective connecting \(\beta\) to data (e.g., episodic returns, TD errors, KL magnitudes).  
     - The explicit posterior update rule, including what constitutes a “round” in Figures 3–4 and Table 3.  
   - A clear equation set and pseudo-code for the adaptation algorithm are needed. Without this, it is impossible to judge the main claimed contribution.

2. **Exact definition and implementation of \(p(o\mid s,a)\) and \(q(o\mid s)\) in each environment**  
   - For MountainCar and MiniGrid:
     - What is the outcome variable \(o\)? Is it the next state \(s'\), a goal indicator, or some latent \(z\)?  
     - How are \(p\) and \(q\) parameterized and estimated? Are they tabular, Gaussian, neural networks, or something else?  
     - How is \(\text{KL}(p\|q)\) computed numerically, and how do you avoid infinite KL when \(q\) is concentrated on goal states?

3. **Relationship between fixed \(\beta\) sweeps and Bayesian adaptation results**  
   - In MountainCar, the best \(\beta\) in Table 2 is 0.1, whereas in MiniGrid the only non-zero \(\beta\) tested is 0.01. In the Bayesian runs, Figure 3 and Table 3 show the posterior mean shrinking below 0.02. How should we reconcile these different “effective” regimes?  
   - Can you provide *actual performance curves* for the Bayesian-adapted \(\beta\) and compare them numerically against the best fixed \(\beta\) per environment?

4. **Clarification of the KL formulation and practical stability**  
   - Do you add any small constant \(\epsilon\) to probabilities to avoid infinite KL? If so, please write the exact formula (e.g., \(\tilde{p} = (p+\epsilon)/(1+K\epsilon)\)).  
   - In the continuous case (MountainCar), is the KL computed in a discrete approximation, latent space, or directly via parametric densities?

5. **Comparison to more competitive baselines**  
   - Can you include at least one standard intrinsic motivation baseline (e.g., curiosity, RND, count-based exploration, or a self-adaptive shaping method) in MountainCar and MiniGrid, and report learning curves with standard deviations over more seeds?  
   - This would help assess whether IncentRL offers any advantage over existing, relatively simple shaping strategies.

If these issues are addressed with concrete definitions, additional experiments, and clarified theory, I would be open to reconsidering the contribution, especially on the conceptual side.
