# Expected Review Outcome:

## Summary

The paper studies the relationship between adaptive optimizers (e.g., AdaGrad/Adam/Shampoo) and their normalized steepest descent (NSD) counterparts in non‑Euclidean geometries. It formalizes the role of *adaptive smoothness* and *adaptive variance* induced by structured preconditioner sets, extends the adaptive smoothness framework to nonconvex optimization via a unified analysis for a broad meta‑algorithm (Algorithm 1), and shows that adaptive smoothness/variance can yield acceleration and dimension‑free stochastic rates that are impossible under standard smoothness/variance assumptions for certain norms (notably ℓ∞/ℓ₁). The paper’s main results include nonconvex convergence rates for adaptive methods depending on adaptive smoothness, an accelerated O(T⁻²) rate for an accelerated adaptive method under adaptive smoothness, and dimension‑free stochastic NSD rates under adaptive variance, together with matching lower bounds under standard variance.

## Strengths

1. **Clear conceptual framing around two geometries and two smoothness notions.**  
   The paper nicely disentangles *standard* norm‑based smoothness/variance from *adaptive* smoothness/variance generated by a structured preconditioner set, and uses this to articulate a sharp difference between adaptive optimizers and NSD, even when the underlying geometry (preconditioner set) is the same. The connection between (2)–(3) and diagonal adaptive smoothness, starting from Adam vs. SignGD, is particularly clean and helps ground the perspective.

2. **Unified nonconvex analysis for a broad adaptive meta‑algorithm.**  
   Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 3.2 give nonconvex convergence rates for Algorithm 1 under arbitrary *well‑structured preconditioner sets* (Definition 2.1). This covers diagonal AdaGrad/Adam, full‑matrix AdaGrad, and one‑sided Shampoo/ASGO, as well as preconditioners like $\mathcal{S}_+^{d_L}\otimes I_{d_R}$. Prior work either focused on convex objectives for general preconditioners or nonconvex analysis only for diagonal preconditioners; this unified nonconvex treatment is a genuine technical advance.

3. **Nontrivial matrix‑analytic contribution enabling general preconditioners.**  
   Lemma 3.3 (expanding on Lemma C.1) is a substantial technical piece: it controls  
   \[
   S_T=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}V_t^{-1}(V_t^2-\beta V_{t-1}^2)V_t^{-1}
   \]  
   with dimension‑explicit operator‑norm bounds for general noncommutative $\mathcal H$, and sharper bounds in the commutative (e.g., diagonal) case. This is exactly what breaks the “entrywise telescoping” available for diagonal preconditioners and enables the extension from diagonal to general well‑structured sets. The proof via a new inequality relating $X-Y$ to $\log X - \log Y$ (Lemma C.1) and the use of Lieb‑type concavity are of independent interest to researchers working on structured preconditioning.

4. **Sharp structural comparison between adaptive and standard smoothness.**  
   Proposition 2.5 and Lemma 2.2 formalize that the adaptive smoothness $\Lambda_{\mathcal H}(f)$ is always between $L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)$ and $d L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)$, and that the induced norm $\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}$ is the supremum of primal norms $\|\cdot\|_H$ while $\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H,*}$ is the infimum of dual norms. **Figure 1** makes this concrete in the diagonal case: the left panel illustrates that the ℓ∞ unit ball is the intersection of ellipses corresponding to weighted ℓ₂ norms with trace‑1 diagonal $H$, while the right panel shows the ℓ₁ unit ball as the union of the corresponding dual ellipses. This geometric visualization powerfully supports the claim that Adam’s adaptivity can be interpreted as optimizing against this supremum/infimum envelope of norms.

5. **Acceleration result that cleanly separates adaptive from standard smoothness.**  
   Theorem 4.3 shows that the accelerated adaptive Algorithm 2, under adaptive smoothness $\Lambda_{\mathcal H}(f)$ and adaptive variance $\sigma_{\mathcal H}$, achieves a deterministic $\hat{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal H}(f) D^2 / T^2)$ term plus a stochastic $\hat{O}(\sigma_{\mathcal H} D / \sqrt{T})$ term on convex losses. Combined with the lower bound of Guzmán & Nemirovski (2015) ruling out better than $\Omega(1/(T\log T))$ under ℓ∞‑smoothness, this gives a crisp *formal separation*: replacing standard ℓ∞‑smoothness with diagonal adaptive smoothness is not just cosmetic, it’s necessary to get acceleration in that geometry.

6. **Adaptive variance as a natural analogue of adaptive smoothness.**  
   Definition 4.1 introduces adaptive variance in a way that mirrors adaptive smoothness: minimize (over $H \in \mathcal H$, $\mathrm{Tr}(H)\le 1$) the worst‑case variance measured in the $H^{-1}$ norm. Propositions B.10 and B.11 neatly relate this to bounded covariance assumptions and standard norm‑variance, and Proposition B.11 shows $\sigma_{\mathcal H}^2$ is sandwiched between $\sigma_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H,*}}^2$ and $d\sigma_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H,*}}^2$, in direct analogy to Proposition 2.5 for smoothness. This gives a clean and reusable definition that other works on stochastic adaptive methods can adopt.

7. **Dimension‑free stochastic NSD rates under adaptive variance, with tight lower bound under standard variance.**  
   Theorem 4.5 proves that NSD with momentum (Algorithm 3) achieves $\tilde{O}(\sigma_{\mathcal H} T^{-1/3})$ or $\tilde{O}((\Delta_0 L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f))^{1/4}\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathcal H}} T^{-1/4})$ rates (depending on regimes of $T$ and $a_0$), with **no explicit $d$ factor**, under adaptive variance and standard smoothness. In contrast, Theorem 4.6 and Theorem 4.7 show that under standard variance in $\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H,*}$ (e.g. ℓ₁ for SignGD), dimension‑dependent distortion factors are inevitable; Theorem 4.7 gives a constructed lower‑bound instance for ℓ∞/ℓ₁ where the $d^{1/2}$ dependence cannot be removed. Together these results persuasively argue that adaptive variance is the “right” stochastic analogue of adaptive smoothness if one cares about dimension‑free rates in non‑Euclidean geometries.

8. **Mathematical details are carefully tracked and largely consistent.**  
   The paper goes to substantial length to spell out auxiliary lemmas (Appendix B), particularly about the mapping $P_{\mathcal H}(M)$ and its properties (linearity in $M$ over $\mathcal H$, projection interpretation in Lemma B.4, Lipschitzness in Lemma B.5, connection to dual norms in Lemma B.8, and characterization of the steepest‑descent direction in Lemma B.9). These technical pieces are used transparently in the nonconvex and accelerated analyses and make the proofs modular and readable for someone familiar with convex analysis and matrix inequalities.

9. **Figure‑level support for geometric claims.**  
   **Figure 1** is not just decorative: it explicitly depicts the intersection/union relationship of the primal/dual unit balls for the diagonal preconditioner set, visually backing equations (4) and Lemma 2.2. This figure concretely conveys why $\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}$ behaves like ℓ∞ and $\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H,*}$ like ℓ₁, thereby linking the abstract definitions to Adam/SignGD behavior and helping readers internalize the geometric story behind the theorems.

## Weaknesses

1. **No empirical illustrations or concrete function classes showing when adaptive smoothness is much larger than standard smoothness.**  
   The paper’s entire story hinges on the distinction between $L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)$ and $\Lambda_{\mathcal H}(f)$, yet beyond Proposition 2.5’s $[1,d]$ multiplicative sandwich and some high‑level discussion, the main text does not provide concrete examples of functions where this gap is large, nor any synthetic experiments. Even a simple diagonal quadratic example illustrating regimes where adaptive smoothness is significantly greater than standard ℓ∞‑smoothness (and where the acceleration in Theorem 4.3 is meaningful) would make the assumptions far more tangible. Right now, one has to take on faith that “typical” ML losses will satisfy a useful adaptive smoothness/variance condition.

2. **The practical implications for optimizer choice remain abstract.**  
   While the theory is rich, the paper stops short of connecting it back to how a practitioner should choose between, say, Adam, one‑sided Shampoo, and NSD‑type methods like Muon or Lion. For instance:
   - The nonconvex guarantees in Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 are on $\|\nabla f(x_t)\|_{\mathcal H,*}$ (e.g. ℓ₁ for Adam) rather than the more standard ℓ₂ norm, and the comparison with NSD in the nonconvex setting (bottom of Page 6) is only qualitative (“they exploit different smoothness notions”) rather than giving a scenario where one dominates the other.
   - The acceleration result (Theorem 4.3) and dimension‑free NSD results (Theorem 4.5) require adaptive smoothness/variance; it would be helpful to at least outline how one might check or estimate these quantities in realistic settings, or to provide a few stylized models where the conditions are known to hold.
   Thus, despite the nice theoretical separations, the paper currently reads more like a pure mathematical exploration than a guide to understanding the empirical success of Adam / Shampoo versus Lion / Muon.

3. **The smoothness comparison paragraph around Proposition 2.5 is confusing and likely contains a typographical error.**  
   On Page 5, after Definition 2.4, the comparison paragraph states
   \[
   L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f) = \sup_{x,y}\frac{\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|_{H,*}}{\|x-y\|_H}
   \ge \sup_{x,y}\frac{\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|_{\mathcal H,*}}{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal H}}
   = L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f),
   \]
   which is self‑inconsistent: the leftmost and rightmost terms are the same symbol but refer to different norms (one should clearly be $L_{\|\cdot\|_H}(f)$ or similar). This appears to be a notational slip but, as written, it makes the logic in the “Comparison between two smoothness notions” paragraph hard to follow and may confuse readers trying to reconstruct the inequalities that lead into Proposition 2.5. This should be corrected and the inequalities rewritten with precise indices on the norms ($L_{\|\cdot\|_H}$ vs. $L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}$) to avoid ambiguity.

4. **Assumptions on adaptive smoothness/variance and bounded covariance are quite strong and under‑discussed.**  
   The deterministic nonconvex results (Section 3) and stochastic results often assume that there exists $H\in\mathcal H$ such that $-\!H \preceq \nabla^2 f(x)\preceq H$ for all $x$ (Definition 2.4), or a global covariance bound $\Sigma\succeq 0$ satisfying  
   \[
   \mathbb{E}[(\nabla f(x_t)-\nabla f_t(x_t))(\nabla f(x_t)-\nabla f_t(x_t))^\top]\preceq\Sigma
   \]
   (Assumption D.1), or that adaptive variance $\sigma_{\mathcal H}$ is finite. For high‑dimensional, highly nonconvex deep networks, these are nontrivial and may fail dramatically. Proposition B.10 remarks that Definition 4.1 is weaker than a uniform covariance bound, but the paper does not give realistic examples where these assumptions provably hold, nor does it critically discuss when they might be violated. Given that many of the highlighted benefits (acceleration, dimension‑free rates) are conditional on these assumptions, the paper should be more explicit about their restrictive nature and where they are likely reasonable.

5. **Essentially no discussion of how the new rates compare numerically to existing nonconvex NSD bounds.**  
   The paper makes some effort to relate its results to recent NSD analyses (e.g., citing Pethick et al. (2025) and Kovalev (2025b) around Page 6 and Section 4.3). However, the comparison is still fairly high‑level:
   - For deterministic nonconvex problems, Theorem 3.2 gives $O(\log d \sqrt{\Delta_0 \Lambda_{\mathcal H}(f)/T})$ in $\|\nabla f(x_t)\|_{\mathcal H,*}$. NSD achieves $O((\Delta_0 L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)/T)^{1/2})$ in the same norm. Because $\Lambda_{\mathcal H}(f)$ and $L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)$ differ by at most a factor $d$, it may well be that the NSD bound is numerically better in many regimes. The paper does not spell out this tradeoff clearly or provide any simple worst‑case examples where the adaptive method’s rate is strictly better or worse.
   - For stochastic NSD, Theorem 4.5’s $T^{-1/4}$ rate under adaptive variance is comparable to, but not obviously stronger than, various recent results under different smoothness/variance definitions. A more detailed “side‑by‑side” discussion of exponents and constants, with a focus on when adaptive geometry yields a concrete advantage over existing NSD analyses, would strengthen the case for the new assumptions.

6. **Some key high‑level narrative claims are only partially backed by the theorems.**  
   The introduction and conclusion emphasize that “adaptive smoothness enables acceleration” and “adaptive variance enables dimension‑free rates,” and suggest this as an explanation for the empirical superiority of adaptive methods. However:
   - The acceleration in Theorem 4.3 is for a specially constructed Nesterov‑style accelerated adaptive algorithm (Algorithm 2), not for the standard Adam/AdaGrad/Shampoo variants used in practice. It is not shown that, say, Adam + Nesterov momentum with EMA preconditioning satisfies all the requirements or achieves this rate (although one might expect it to).
   - The dimension‑free rate is for NSD with momentum under adaptive variance, not for adaptive optimizers per se, and requires careful tuning of $\alpha$ and $\eta$ based on unknown problem parameters.  
   The high‑level slogans are directionally correct, but the paper should be more nuanced in stating exactly which algorithms and assumptions the theorems cover.

7. **Minor mathematical and notational issues that could trip up readers.**  
   A few notational or typographical glitches hinder clarity in an otherwise dense mathematical exposition:
   - Definition 2.4 writes $\nabla^{d} f(x)$ instead of $\nabla^2 f(x)$ in the Hessian sandwich condition, which is presumably a typo.
   - In the pseudo‑code for Algorithm 1 (Page 5), the line describing $M_t$ mixes three variants in a single cell (“Weighted variant. | Cumulative variant, EMA variant, Weighted variant.”), which is confusing and hard to parse; it took reading Appendix D.1 to see how exactly the three variants are defined.
   - Theorem 4.3’s bound involves a term  
     \[
     \Big(\frac{D^2}{2\eta}-\frac{\eta}{2}\Big)\frac{4}{(T+1)^2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} g_t^\top V_t^{-1} g_t,
     \]  
     and the text later sets $\eta=D$ so this term’s coefficient is zero. This is fine but should be stated explicitly in the theorem remark to reassure readers that no uncontrolled positive term remains.
   While none of these is fatal, they slow down comprehension and could easily be cleaned up.

8. **No tables summarizing rates or assumptions for quick comparison.**  
   The paper contains no results tables. Given the number of regimes and assumptions (deterministic vs stochastic; standard vs adaptive smoothness/variance; NSD vs adaptive meta‑algorithms; diagonal vs general $\mathcal H$), a small table summarizing for each algorithm: the quantity controlled, the rate, and the assumptions (norm, smoothness, variance), would significantly improve accessibility and make it easier to see precisely where the claimed separations come from.

9. **Some closely related recent work on non‑Euclidean optimization and adaptive methods is not cited.**  
   The related work section (Appendix A) is strong but still omits several very directly related recent works on non‑Euclidean NSD and structured adaptive methods (see “Potentially Missing Related Work” below). Given that one of the paper’s main selling points is a unified geometric view on adaptive vs NSD methods, missing these references undercuts the claimed positioning and should be rectified.

## Potentially Missing Related Work

Below are directly related works that appear not to be cited or discussed and should be incorporated, with suggested points of integration:

1. **Naganuma et al., “Adaptive Batch Sizes Using Non-Euclidean Gradient Noise Scales for Stochastic Sign and Spectral Descent”, 2026.**  
   This work studies adaptive batch‑size strategies using non‑Euclidean gradient noise scales for stochastic sign and spectral descent, directly overlapping with this paper’s emphasis on non‑Euclidean geometries and gradient noise structure. It should be cited in Section 4.1 (adaptive variance) and Appendix A, with a discussion of how adaptive variance could interact with or formalize their heuristic noise scaling, and how the dimension‑free NSD rates compare.

2. **Khademi & Silveti‑Falls, “Adaptive Conditional Gradient Descent”, 2025.**  
   This paper proposes adaptive step‑size and geometry strategies for conditional gradient methods in non‑Euclidean settings. It is conceptually close to viewing certain optimizers as steepest descent with respect to adaptive norms. It should be discussed in Section 4.3 and the related work (Appendix A) as another instance where adaptivity plus non‑Euclidean geometry yields improved rates, and contrasted with the current focus on preconditioner‑based methods.

3. **Ozkara et al., “MADA: Meta‑Adaptive Optimizers Through Hyper‑Gradient Descent”, 2024.**  
   MADA designs meta‑adaptive optimizers by adjusting parameters through hyper‑gradients, again emphasizing adaptivity of the effective geometry. This should be mentioned in Section 3.1 (discussion of Algorithm 1’s flexibility) and Appendix A, noting that while MADA adapts at the meta‑level, the present work focuses on the induced smoothness/variance structures of preconditioners and could, in principle, be used to analyze MADA’s geometry if cast within a suitable $\mathcal H$.

4. **Crawshaw et al., “An Exploration of Non‑Euclidean Gradient Descent: Muon and its Many Variants”, 2025.**  
   This exploration of Muon and its variants explicitly formulates them as steepest descent methods under non‑Euclidean norms, very much in the same spirit as the Adam/SignGD vs NSD storytelling here. It should be cited in Section 2.1 and Appendix A when discussing Muon, Shampoo, and NSD under spectral norms, clarifying how their non‑Euclidean steepest descent perspective relates to the well‑structured preconditioner framework used in the current paper.

5. **Veprikov et al., “Preconditioned Norms: A Unified Framework for Steepest Descent, Quasi‑Newton and Adaptive Methods”, 2025.**  
   This paper also provides a unifying framework via preconditioned norms that connects steepest descent, quasi‑Newton, and adaptive methods, very much parallel to the role of $\mathcal H$‑induced norms in this submission. It should be cited near Definition 2.1 and Section 3.1, with a clear comparison: Veprikov et al. study preconditioned norms and algorithmic unification more broadly, while this paper focuses on adaptive smoothness/variance and nonconvex convergence rates.

6. **Chen, Huo & Yan, “IO‑Adam: Rethinking Memory‑Efficient Adaptive Optimizers from Gradient Computation”, 2025.**  
   IO‑Adam introduces a memory‑efficient adaptive optimizer with a modified gradient computation pipeline. Because this paper’s adaptive smoothness and $\mathcal H$ framework cover diagonal (Adam‑like) methods, IO‑Adam should be mentioned in Appendix A as a recent practical variant of Adam whose behavior might be analyzed under the same adaptive smoothness notions.

Incorporating these references would more fully situate the contribution within the rapidly growing literature on non‑Euclidean optimization, NSD‑type methods, and structured adaptive optimizers.

## Questions

1. **On the practical relevance of adaptive smoothness and variance:**  
   Can the authors provide explicit examples of loss functions (even simple synthetic ones) where $\Lambda_{\mathcal H}(f)$ is significantly larger than $L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)$, and similarly where $\sigma_{\mathcal H}$ is much larger than $\sigma_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H,*}}$? Showing such examples, and perhaps indicating whether they resemble properties of deep networks in practice, would help assess how restrictive or meaningful these adaptive conditions are.

2. **Checking and clarifying the smoothness comparison around Proposition 2.5:**  
   The paragraph “Comparison between two smoothness notions” appears to have a notational issue where $L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)$ is used on both sides of an inequality. Could the authors rewrite that paragraph with clearly distinct symbols (e.g. $L_{\|\cdot\|_H}(f)$ vs $L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)$) and confirm that the intended inequalities are  
   \[
   L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f) \le \Lambda_{\mathcal H}(f) \le d L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)
   \]  
   with the middle step coming from comparing the induced and adaptive norms?

3. **Tightness of the logarithmic dimension factors in Lemma 3.3 and nonconvex rates:**  
   For general (noncommutative) preconditioner sets, Lemma 3.3 introduces a $\log d$ factor that propagates into Theorem 3.2 and Theorem 4.3. Do the authors believe this $\log d$ is intrinsic to noncommutative $\mathcal H$, or is it potentially an artifact of the current technique? Any intuition or partial lower bounds indicating that a dimension‑free bound is impossible for some $\mathcal H$ would be enlightening.

4. **On acceleration for standard Adam / Shampoo variants:**  
   Algorithm 2 is theoretically clean but somewhat idealized. Is it possible to instantiate Algorithm 2 with the EMA or cumulative variants of $M_t$ used by Adam or Shampoo (rather than the simple additive variant) and still obtain the same $O(T^{-2})$ deterministic rate under adaptive smoothness? If not, what technical barrier arises?

5. **Parameter tuning and adaptivity in Theorem 4.5:**  
   The dimension‑free NSD rates in Theorem 4.5 rely on different choices of $\alpha$ and $\eta$ depending on the unknown quantity $a_0 = \sqrt{\Delta_0 L_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal H}}(f)}/\sigma_{\mathcal H}$. Is there a way to choose $\alpha_t$ and $\eta_t$ adaptively (e.g., via a schedule) so that a single algorithm achieves near‑optimal rates across both regimes without prior knowledge of $a_0$? Even a conjectural direction or partial result would be useful.

6. **Explicit numerical comparison with existing NSD results.**  
   Could the authors add a small table or remark quantifying how their NSD bounds (Theorems 4.5 and 4.6) compare numerically with those in Pethick et al. (2025), Kovalev (2025b), and Kovalev & Borodich (2025) for typical parameter regimes? This would help clarify how much practical improvement adaptive variance and the present proof technique provide.
