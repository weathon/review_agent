# Expected Review Outcome:

## Summary

The paper argues that current scientific LLMs (Sci-LLMs) are fundamentally limited by a “tokenization dilemma” when handling biomolecular sequences: sequence-as-language approaches lose functional motifs, while sequence-as-modality approaches suffer from semantic misalignment between biological and linguistic representations.  

Instead of forcing LLMs to decode raw sequences, the authors propose a context-driven paradigm that feeds high-level, human-readable annotations generated by classical bioinformatics tools (InterProScan, BLASTp, ProTrek) into a general LLM. Across a suite of protein and DNA reasoning tasks, and three input regimes (sequence-only, context-only, sequence+context), they empirically find that context-only inputs consistently outperform the others and that adding the sequence to the context often *harms* performance. They further analyze representation quality (Figure 2, Figure 3), temporal robustness (Figure 4), efficiency (Table 2), and wet-lab-validated novel proteins.

## Strengths

1. **Clear and provocative central claim, well supported at the high level.**  
   The paper’s main message is sharp: *for current Sci-LLMs, high-level context beats raw sequences, and sequences can even be harmful when combined with context.* Table 1 (Page 6) is particularly compelling: for every evaluated model (Intern-S1, Evolla, NatureLM, Deepseek-v3, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5, Qwen3), the Context-Only setting matches or outperforms Sequence+Context, often by a nontrivial margin (e.g., Evolla 74.02 vs. 70.53; Intern-S1 86.15 vs. 84.03; Gemini 2.5 Pro 87.19 vs. 86.98). This systematic pattern across both domain-specific and general LLMs is a strong empirical result.

2. **Carefully engineered context pipeline and informative ablation.**  
   Section 4 and Appendix A describe a reasonably principled pipeline: InterProScan and BLASTp as primary evidence, ProTrek as fallback for orphans, and a hierarchical rule-based integration. Table 3 (Page 21) is a helpful ablation: it shows GO-only (84.02) and Pfam+GO (84.60) outperform ProTrek-only (66.44), and that *unconditional* inclusion of ProTrek actually degrades performance vs. Pfam+GO, whereas the final *conditional* strategy recovers the best result (84.99). This supports the claim that not all “more context” is good and that the constructed context is not just a trivial aggregation.

3. **Representation-space analysis gives useful mechanistic insight.**  
   Figure 2 (Page 7) compares t-SNE visualizations of final embeddings produced by Evolla, Intern-S1, NatureLM, and the proposed context-driven representation (via Qwen embeddings), along with ARI scores. The context-driven approach reaches ARI 0.958, beating Evolla (0.809), Intern-S1 (0.690), and NatureLM (0.492). This is not just eye-candy: combined with Table 4 (Page 25), which shows identical ARI patterns under UniClust50 and UniClust30, the paper makes a credible point that context-based representations cluster functional families more cleanly than sequence-based LLM outputs.  

   Similarly, Figure 3 (Page 8) and Table 4 highlight that Evolla’s SaProt encoder has strong functional structure (ARI 0.945) that progressively degrades through the Q-Former alignment and decoder (down to 0.809), concretely illustrating the “semantic misalignment” horn of the dilemma.

4. **Interesting temporal robustness analysis.**  
   Figure 4 (Page 8) plots LLM-Score versus first publication year for ~100 proteins per year (1995–2024) for the context-driven method vs. Evolla vs. Intern-S1. The context method (4a) shows high overall performance and only a mild downward trend for recent proteins; Evolla (4b) degrades steeply for newer proteins; Intern-S1 (4c) is flat and low. This is a nice angle: it suggests that models heavily relying on evolutionary depth and legacy training data struggle on “frontier” proteins, while context from up-to-date tools plus a general LLM is more temporally stable.

5. **Extension beyond one task/domain, including DNA and EC numbers.**  
   Appendix D (Figure 8) shows EC number prediction where context-driven models (e.g., Gemini-2.5 Pro with context) match or surpass CLEAN, a specialized sequence-based EC predictor, especially at 1–3 digit levels. Sequence-only LLMs collapse as specificity increases. Appendix G shows similarly strong results on Mol-Instruction protein classification (Figure 10) and a KEGG DNA mutation dataset (Figure 11), with context-only inputs again dominating. These results alleviate concerns that the main finding is overfitted to one QA dataset.

6. **Wet-lab validation on unpublished sequences, plus honest limitation discussion.**  
   Section 5.6 and Appendix N describe a zero-shot classification experiment on genuinely novel Rhodopsin and PETase proteins from wet-lab experiments. Figure 5 (Page 10) shows the context-driven + Deepseek-v3 method achieving 100% / 97.3% accuracy, while Evolla (Figure 6) performs much worse on PETase. While details are sparse, this is rare and valuable evidence that the method can handle real, unseen sequences.  

   Appendix J explicitly acknowledges a serious limitation: the context pipeline is largely blind to point mutations, since tools like InterProScan and BLASTp yield identical Pfam / GO annotations for wild-type and mutant sequences (examples at Pages 34–35). This level of self-critique is appreciated.

7. **Clarity and positioning are generally good, with engaging narrative.**  
   The writing is readable and the narrative around the “tokenization dilemma” is well structured (Sections 1–3). Figure 1 (Page 2) is a simple but effective conceptual diagram contrasting sequence-as-language, sequence-as-modality, and the context-driven approach, helping non-specialist LLM researchers quickly grasp the problem framing.

## Weaknesses

1. **Evaluation target is often very close to the context source; “reasoning” vs. retrieval is under-analyzed.**  
   The core experiments (Section 5.1, Table 1) rely on QA tasks where ground truth answers are *directly excerpted* from the same Swiss-Prot entries that feed BLASTp-based GO annotations. Even though the method claims to avoid using the query protein’s own record (Page 6: “we restrict our context-driven approach to reading GO annotations from the homologous sequences, rather than from the query protein's own record”), high sequence identity means homolog annotations are often paraphrases or near-copies of the target ground truth.  

   In that setting, the task is arguably closer to paraphrase / summarization of known annotations than genuine functional inference. The paper does not quantify sequence-identity or textual overlap between the context and the ground truth for the main QA benchmark, nor does it analyze cases where the context is clearly *insufficient* and success would require nontrivial extrapolation. This is critical, because “LLMs are good at reading GO strings and restating them” is a much weaker claim than “LLMs understand proteins via high-level reasoning”.  

   Concrete suggestion: report statistics on (i) maximal BLAST identity and coverage between the query and the top homolog(s) used in context, and (ii) lexical / embedding similarity between the context text and the ground-truth answer. Also include curated subsets where homolog annotations are either absent or substantially less informative, and show whether context-only still wins there.

2. **Heavy reliance on an LLM judge (LLM-Score) with potential leakage and circularity.**  
   Evaluation on the main QA tasks uses another LLM, DeepSeek-V3, as the adjudicator (Appendix C.1). At the same time, DeepSeek-V3 is also one of the *models under comparison* in Table 1, and the base model for the context-driven pipeline in multiple experiments. This raises two issues:  
   - Potential **model-specific bias**: the judge’s scoring criteria may favor answers stylistically or semantically closer to its own pretraining distribution and reasoning style, which might align better with general LLMs than with specialized Sci-LLMs.  
   - **Leakage risk**: for the context-driven setting, DeepSeek-V3 is scoring its *own* outputs given tool-generated context, which could inflate differences relative to the other models.  

   While LLM-based evaluation is increasingly common, here it is central to the main quantitative claims (Table 1, Figure 10, Figure 11). There is no calibration with human expert evaluation on a subset, nor any cross-judge sanity check (e.g., scoring with multiple independent LLMs).  

   Concrete suggestion: (a) at minimum, evaluate all models under a *judge* that is never used as a generator in any condition (e.g., a disjoint LLM), and (b) for a subset of samples, obtain human biologist scores to verify that LLM-Score correlates meaningfully with expert judgments.

3. **“Sequence as noise” claim is stronger than what the data actually proves.**  
   The narrative repeatedly asserts that raw sequences, when added to context, “consistently act as informational noise” and are “actively detrimental” (e.g., Page 7 “The inclusion of the raw sequence… resulted in a lower score.”). Yet in Table 1 the degradation is often small and within what could easily be evaluation noise: e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro All = 86.98 (Seq+Context) vs. 87.19 (Context-only), Deepseek-v3 86.03 vs. 84.99 (here *Sequence+Context is slightly better*), NatureLM 38.86 vs. 39.50, Qwen3 85.90 vs. 84.99.  

   We never see confidence intervals, variance estimates, or per-sample statistics. Without statistical testing, asserting that the sequence is “actively detrimental” across the board is overstated. In at least one case (Deepseek-v3 All), Sequence+Context is *higher* than Context-only.  

   Concrete suggestion: report mean ± std or confidence intervals over multiple evaluation runs (e.g., with different judge random seeds), perform paired significance tests for Sequence+Context vs. Context-only per model and per task, and moderate the rhetoric where differences are minor or inconsistent.

4. **Task design and train–test contamination are not fully transparent.**  
   The manuscript states the benchmark proteins have <30% or <40% sequence identity to Evolla’s training set (Appendix B.1), but there is no analogous analysis with respect to the underlying LLMs’ *textual* training data. Since Swiss-Prot annotations and Gene Ontology definitions are widely available, it is plausible that DeepSeek-v3, Gemini, GPT-5, etc., have partially memorized many of the ground-truth answer sentences.  

   Moreover, the context itself is built from InterProScan and BLASTp outputs over Swiss-Prot. Without a careful split of UniProt entries by accession, and without ensuring that the LLMs did not see those specific Swiss-Prot records during pretraining, the experiments risk overstating generalization. Figure 4’s temporal analysis partly addresses this, but only indirectly.  

   Concrete suggestion: clarify the protocol for ensuring that the *exact* Swiss-Prot entries used in evaluation are not trivially memorized by the LLMs, or at least run a “text-masked” variant where context removes any phrases that are near-verbatim copies of the ground truth.

5. **Mathematical formulation is shallow and not tightly connected to experiments.**  
   The “Preliminaries” (Section 3) introduce functions \(f\), \(\mathcal{M}\), tokenizers \(T_{\text{seq}}\), \(T_{\text{text}}\), and encoders/aligners \(\mathcal{E}_{bio}, \mathcal{A}_{\text{align}}\), culminating in Eqs. (1)–(6). However:
   - Eq. (6) asserts \(P(a|s,q)\approx P(a|c,q)\), but no conditions are provided under which this approximation is expected to be accurate, nor is it empirically quantified (e.g., via mutual information between \(s\) and \(c\)).  
   - In practice, the experiments do not involve any optimization over \(\theta\); models are frozen APIs. The probabilistic notation is therefore mostly decorative, and the paper never connects these equations to metrics like ARI or LLM-Score.  
   - The “sequence-as-modality” formulation in Eq. (4) hides critical design choices: How are \(K\) and \(d\) fixed across models? Does \(\mathcal{A}_{\text{align}}\) concatenate or interleave tokens? These details matter for interpreting the semantic misalignment effects in Figure 3 and Appendix F.  

   While this is not a theorem-heavy paper, the math section currently gives an illusion of formality without adding much technical insight. A more concrete information-theoretic or capacity-based argument would significantly strengthen the conceptual claims.

6. **Representation analysis relies heavily on t-SNE and ARI but misses important controls.**  
   Figure 2 and Figure 3 visualize representation spaces with t-SNE and then compute ARI after clustering. Several issues remain unclear:  
   - How are clusters derived for ARI? The text mentions “performing clustering on the high-dimensional embeddings,” but not which algorithm (k-means? hierarchical?), number of clusters, or hyperparameters are used.  
   - Since t-SNE is only used for visualization, ARI presumably is computed in the original space, but that should be explicitly clarified.  
   - The same ground-truth labels (from MMseqs2 at 50% identity or UniClust50/30) are used to evaluate both sequence-based and context-based representations. However, since the context pipeline itself depends on homology, this can partially bake in those labels.  

   Without these details, it is hard to know how robust the ARI comparisons are. For instance, could the near-perfect 0.958 ARI for context be partly a reflection of the clustering used by InterPro/BLAST, not a genuinely new structure induced by the LLM?

7. **Mutation insensitivity is a major limitation for many real applications.**  
   Appendix F and J convincingly show that both the sequence-as-modality architecture (Evolla) and the proposed context-driven pipeline are largely blind to point mutations: the Q-Former alignment in Evolla smooths away fine differences (Figure 9), and the context pipeline yields identical Pfam/GO outputs for wild-type vs. mutant (Appendix J examples on Pages 34–35).  

   The paper acknowledges this but then mostly side-steps it in the main narrative. For many key problems (variant effect prediction, protein engineering, disease mutation analysis), this is not a minor caveat but a core requirement. Given that the paper’s rhetoric is about “unlocking biomolecular understanding,” this limitation should be more prominently discussed in Section 6, with a clearer statement that the proposed paradigm is unsuitable *as-is* for mutation-sensitive tasks.

8. **Some experimental design choices are under-specified or asymmetric.**  
   - In Section 5.5 and Table 2, the cost analysis compares Deepseek-v3 (sequence-only), Evolla (sequence-only), and the context-driven method (context + Deepseek-v3). However, it omits a sequence+context Evolla in batch, or an “Evolla + context” variant, which would clarify whether similar gains could be obtained by simply feeding its outputs into a general LLM.  
   - For wet-lab validation (Section 5.6, Figure 5 and 6), we only see the context-driven method and Evolla. Intern-S1 is in Appendix N (Figure 12) but NatureLM is dismissed qualitatively. A more systematic table of accuracies across all models would strengthen the case.  
   - For the KEGG DNA task (Figure 11), only GPT-4o and Qwen3-4B are tested, with no Sci-LLM baselines. It is unclear whether sequence-as-modality or DNA foundation models might behave differently.

   These gaps do not invalidate the findings but make some of the broader generalizations (“Sci-LLMs fundamentally fail at sequence interpretation”) less airtight.

## Potentially Missing Related Work

Below I list directly related papers that are not cited in the manuscript but should be considered, especially in Section 2 or in the discussion of the tokenization dilemma and sequence understanding:

1. **Sanabria et al., “Distinguishing Word Identity and Sequence Context in DNA Language Models,” 2024.**  
   This work explicitly studies tokenization and context effects in DNA language models, making it highly relevant to the “tokenization dilemma” framing. It should be discussed in Section 2.1 or 2.3 as prior evidence that tokenization choices and context composition crucially affect biological sequence modeling.

2. **Rives et al., “Biological structure and function emerge from scaling unsupervised learning to 250 million protein sequences,” 2021.**  
   Although Rao et al. (2020) and Elnaggar et al. (2021) are cited, this large-scale ESM paper is a central reference for emergent structure and function in protein LMs. It directly speaks to whether sequence-only models can, in principle, overcome tokenization issues, and should be contrasted in Section 2.1 and in the discussion of weak representation (Section 5.2).

3. **Bepler & Berger, “Learning the Protein Language: Evolution, Structure, and Function,” 2019.**  
   A foundational work on learning protein representations with explicit connections to structure and function. It should be cited in Section 2.1 when surveying protein representation learning and in discussions about alignment between learned embeddings and biological semantics.

4. **Alley et al., “Unified Rational Protein Engineering with Sequence-Based Deep Representation Learning,” 2019.**  
   Demonstrates that sequence-based deep representations can support rational protein engineering. This is directly relevant to the claim that sequence-based models are “fundamentally handicapped” and should be acknowledged as a counterpoint in Section 2.1 and Section 6.

5. **Heinzinger et al., “Modeling aspects of the language of life through transfer-learning protein sequences,” 2019.**  
   An early demonstration of transfer learning on protein sequences, relevant to the idea that LLMs can extract meaningful biological features from sequences. It should be included in Section 2.1.

6. **Rao et al., “Evaluating Protein Transfer Learning with TAPE,” 2019 (distinct from Rao 2020).**  
   Provides a standard benchmark suite for protein transfer learning and evaluates various sequence-based models. Including this in Section 2.1 would help contextualize the limitations of sequence-only approaches and offer potential future evaluation settings.

7. **Yang et al., “Machine-learning-guided directed evolution for protein engineering,” 2019.**  
   Illustrates practical success using sequence-based ML for protein engineering, again serving as an important counterbalance to the paper’s negative view of sequence-based modeling. Could be cited in Section 2.1 or 6 to nuance the discussion.

8. **Madani et al., “ProGen: Language Modeling for Protein Generation,” 2020.**  
   A key protein generation LM using sequence tokenization. Fits naturally into Section 2.1 when describing “sequence-as-language” models and should be mentioned when discussing whether such models are truly “lost in tokenization.”

9. **Strodthoff et al., “UDSMProt: Universal Deep Sequence Models for Protein Classification,” 2020.**  
   Develops deep sequence models that perform well on a wide range of protein classification tasks. This is directly related to the EC number prediction and protein classification experiments, and could be added to Section 2.1 and Appendix D’s discussion.

Incorporating these works would sharpen the positioning of the paper’s contribution relative to decades of sequence-based representation learning and would temper some of the stronger claims about the inadequacy of those approaches.

## Questions

1. **Context–ground truth overlap and reasoning depth.**  
   Could the authors quantify how much of the ground-truth text is recoverable from the provided context alone, e.g., via lexical overlap, embedding similarity, or a retrieval baseline that simply selects the closest context sentence? A concrete analysis here would clarify whether the main benchmark is mostly a restatement task or requires genuine inference.

2. **LLM-Score reliability and judge choice.**  
   How sensitive are the reported LLM-Score differences (Table 1, Figures 10–11) to the specific choice of DeepSeek-V3 as the adjudicator? Have the authors tried scoring with a different, independent LLM that is *not* used as a generator anywhere? Even a small-scale comparison would increase confidence that the conclusions are not an artifact of the judge.

3. **Statistical significance of “sequence hurts” claim.**  
   For each model in Table 1, can the authors report standard deviations or run multiple evaluations with different judge seeds, and compute paired tests comparing Sequence+Context vs. Context-only? Are the decreases for Evolla and Intern-S1 statistically significant across samples and tasks, or within noise?

4. **Robustness to weaker or noisy context.**  
   The ablation in Table 3 focuses on which context components are helpful under the *current* high-quality setup. What happens if GO annotations are restricted to higher-level terms, or if only Pfam is used for all proteins? Is there a regime in which adding sequence *helps* compensate for sparse context, perhaps for very recent proteins where homology is weak?

5. **Handling mutation-sensitive tasks.**  
   Given the strong evidence in Appendix F and J that both Evolla (post-Q-Former) and the context pipeline are mutation-insensitive, do the authors see a viable path to combining a mutation-aware encoder (e.g., Evo) with a context-driven LLM without losing fine-grained sensitivity? How might they redesign \(\mathcal{A}_{\text{align}}\) or modify the context to retain local mutation effects?

6. **Train–test data leakage and temporal splits.**  
   Can the authors provide more details on how they ensured that the Swiss-Prot entries (and their specific text fields) used for evaluation are not trivially memorized by the LLMs? For example, have they considered a time-based split where evaluation proteins and their annotations were added to Swiss-Prot *after* the LLMs’ pretraining cutoff?

7. **EC number prediction protocol details.**  
   In Appendix D, how exactly were context strings for EC prediction constructed? Were explicit EC numbers ever present in the context given to the LLMs, and if so, how is label leakage avoided? It would be useful to see an ablation where any numeric EC identifiers are stripped from the context.

Answers and additional experiments addressing these questions could substantively change my assessment, particularly regarding the strength of the causal claim that sequences are “informational noise.”

## Soundness Rating

3: good.  
The experimental methodology is generally reasonable and multi-faceted (QA, EC prediction, Mol-Instruction, DNA tasks, temporal analysis, representation metrics), and the qualitative conclusions are plausible. However, reliance on an LLM judge, lack of significance testing, and incomplete analysis of context–label overlap and potential data leakage prevent a higher soundness rating.

## Presentation Rating

3: good.  
The paper is well written, clearly organized, and uses figures and tables effectively (e.g., Figure 1 for conceptual framing, Figure 2 and 3 for representation analysis, Table 1 and Table 3 for key quantitative results). Some methodological details (clustering for ARI, precise dataset splits, judge choice implications) are underspecified, but overall clarity is above average.

## Contribution Rating

3: good.  
The conceptual reframing from “sequence decoding” to “context-driven reasoning” for Sci-LLMs is timely and thought-provoking, and the empirical evidence that context-only often outperforms sequence-based setups is valuable for the community. The work is more of a strong empirical and conceptual study than a new algorithm, and some claims are overstated, but the insights into the limitations of current tokenization and alignment strategies are important.

## Overall Rating

6: marginally above the acceptance threshold. But would not mind if paper is rejected.  

The paper makes a compelling and well-articulated case that, for current Sci-LLMs, carefully constructed high-level context is more useful than raw sequence tokens, and that the prevailing sequence-centric paradigm should be reconsidered. The breadth of experiments (proteins, EC prediction, Mol-Instruction, DNA, wet-lab sequences) and representation analyses (Figure 2, 3, 4, 8, 9, 10, 11, Table 1, 2, 3) provide substantial evidence, and the work will likely influence how researchers design scientific LLM pipelines.  

However, the evaluation setup has nontrivial weaknesses: strong dependence on an LLM judge that also serves as a generator, potentially shallow “reasoning” over near-verbatim annotations, lack of statistical analysis for small differences, and incomplete discussion of data leakage and mutation sensitivity. These issues keep the paper from a higher recommendation, but I still lean positive given its relevance and the usefulness of the empirical findings.
