# Expected Review Outcome:

## Summary

The paper proposes a training-free framework, TokenCount, for class-agnostic object counting using the Segment Anything Model (SAM) without any fine-tuning or auxiliary models. The approach has two main components: (1) a probabilistic prompt generation scheme based on a similarity-derived probability distribution over the SAM image embedding, and (2) an output token–based verification stage that uses a geometric similarity metric (TS-SS) on SAM decoder output tokens instead of encoder embeddings. Experiments on FSC-147 and CARPK report competitive performance compared to prior training-free and some supervised counting methods, with ablations on similarity metrics, iteration/prompt budgets, and token vs image-embedding spaces.

## Strengths

1. **Interesting use of SAM’s decoder tokens for counting.**  
   The core idea of interpreting SAM’s decoder output tokens as the main representation for similarity and verification is conceptually interesting and more aligned with SAM’s architecture than the typical encoder-feature similarity used in prior training-free counting work. The PCA visualizations in **Figure 5** (Page 9) give qualitative evidence that output-token space clusters positives and negatives more cleanly than image embedding space, which supports the claimed benefit of moving similarity computation to the decoder side.

2. **Clear architectural story around using foundation models “as is.”**  
   The method keeps SAM frozen and does not introduce extra encoders or decoders, which is aligned with the stated goal of a fully training-free pipeline. **Figure 2** (Page 4) does a good job at visually summarizing how the proposed probabilistic prompt generation and token-based verification sit on top of the vanilla SAM pipeline, including the iterative positive/negative refinement loop. This clarity makes it easy for practitioners to see how to bolt the method onto existing SAM implementations.

3. **Non-trivial empirical results on standard benchmarks.**  
   On FSC-147 and CARPK, the method performs competitively among SAM-only training-free approaches. In **Table 1** (Page 6), the method achieves MAE 16.25 on FSC-147, beating prior SAM-only methods such as TFOC (19.95 MAE) and C-Anything (27.97 MAE). On CARPK, it reaches MAE 4.68 vs 10.97 for TFOC and 16.97 for vanilla SAM, which is a substantial relative improvement for a purely training-free SAM-only method.

4. **Ablations that probe the core design choices.**  
   The paper does include targeted ablations:
   - **Table 3** (Page 8) compares dot product, cosine similarity, L2 distance, and TS-SS in the token space, showing TS-SS marginally but consistently best (MAE 16.25 vs 16.48/16.53).  
   - **Table 4** studies the effect of iterations and prompts per iteration, showing that more prompts and more iterations lead to better MAE/RMSE at the cost of more prompts, which indeed validates that the iterative probabilistic strategy is doing useful exploration rather than random noise.  
   - **Table 5** compares operating in output-token space vs image-embedding space under the same pipeline, again supporting the central claim that tokens are a better representation for verification.

5. **Qualitative figures that help illustrate both success and failure modes.**  
   The qualitative examples in **Figure 4** (Page 7) and **Figures 6–7** (Appendix B) show that the method often produces clean instance masks and accurate counts across varying densities and viewpoints. **Figure 8** presents failure cases in dense and highly overlapping scenes, which is an honest and useful disclosure of limitations.

## Weaknesses

1. **Overstated claims relative to the baselines, especially supervised methods.**  
   Some of the narrative over-claims the performance. For example, in the abstract and Section 4.2 it is suggested that the method “outperforms existing training-based and training-free counting methods” or is “superior” to supervised methods. Yet **Table 1** clearly shows that on FSC-147, several supervised methods are substantially better in MAE than TokenCount: LOCA (10.79), PseCo (13.05), SAFECount (14.32), BMNet+ (14.62), all beating 16.25 by a wide margin. On CARPK, BMNet+ (5.76) and SAFECount (5.33) are close, and TFCAC (training-free but with extra models) is better (4.39 vs 4.68). The paper later clarifies some of this, but the strong wording in the abstract and introduction is not fully justified by **Table 1** and **Table 2**. This matters because it can mislead readers about the actual position of the method in the performance landscape.

2. **Limited novelty and somewhat ad-hoc choice of similarity metric.**  
   The idea of using decoder output tokens instead of encoder embeddings is the main conceptual contribution. However, the TS-SS metric itself is borrowed from prior work on document similarity and is not specifically derived or tailored for this token space. Section 3.2 provides intuition that output tokens encode both “semantic (angular) and positional (magnitudinous) information” and that dot product / cosine / Euclidean are individually insufficient. But the use of Equations (2)–(3) is essentially plug-and-play; there is no formal argument that TS-SS is appropriate for the statistics of SAM tokens, nor is there any analysis of why TS-SS should outperform, say, a weighted combination of cosine and Euclidean distance learned or tuned for this space. The empirical gains in **Table 3** are small (e.g., MAE 16.25 vs 16.48 / 16.53), which suggests that while TS-SS is somewhat better, it is not a game changer and the choice feels heuristic.

3. **Mathematical formulation and notation in Section 3.1 are sloppy and sometimes ambiguous.**  
   Equation (1) and the surrounding lines contain several issues that hinder reproducibility:

   - The pipeline is described as  
     \[
     \mathbf{F}^I = f(\mathbf{l}),\quad \mathbf{M} = g(\mathbf{F}^I, e),\quad
     \mathbf{M}^R = \mathrm{resize}(\mathbf{M},(64,64,1)),\quad
     \mathbf{F}^E = \operatorname{nonzero}(\mathbf{F}^I \odot \mathbf{M}^R)
     \]
     Then  
     \(\mathbf{S} = \cos(\mathbf{F}^I, \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{F}^E))\) and
     \(P(\mathbf{l}; \mathbf{e}) = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{S})/\tau)\).

     However:
     - The operator `nonzero` is not properly defined. The text says “image embedding of mask area, and \(N\) is the number of mask pixels,” but how exactly the 3D tensor \(\mathbf{F}^I \in \mathbb{R}^{64\times64\times256}\) and the mask \(\mathbf{M}^R \in \mathbb{R}^{64\times64\times1}\) are reshaped into \(\mathbf{F}^E \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times 256}\) is left implicit.  
     - The use of `cos` between a 3D tensor \(\mathbf{F}^I\) and a vector \(\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{F}^E)\in\mathbb{R}^{256}\) should be spelled out. I assume this yields a 64×64 map of cosine similarities, but the exact broadcasting and normalization are not described.  
     - The paper states that ReLU “discards anti-correlated features,” but ReLU on cosine similarity in \([-1,1]\) is arbitrary; why not shift and rescale to a non-negative range, or use a softplus? There is no ablation on this design, and mathematically the justification is hand-wavy.  
     These notation and design gaps are small individually, but together they make the probabilistic prompting pipeline less rigorous and harder to reimplement faithfully.

4. **Probabilistic prompt generation is under-specified and heavily heuristic.**  
   While the idea of sampling prompts from a probability map derived from exemplar similarity is reasonable, several important parts of Section 3.1 are not precisely defined:

   - The multinomial sampling over the 64×64 grid is only described at a high level. How are prompts mapped back to image space? Does each sampled cell yield exactly one point (center of the cell)? Are there constraints to prevent multiple prompts in a single cell in the same iteration?  
   - The update rule for the probability distribution is only qualitatively described via **Figure 3** (Page 4): “positive → set to 0, negative → decreased.” But the exact negative scaling factor or schedule is never given, which is crucial for reproducing the exploration behavior.  
   - The early stopping criterion in Section 4.4 (IoU of mask unions > 0.95 with patience 5) is introduced but its effect is not quantified; there is no comparison to a version without early stopping, nor sensitivity analysis.  
   From a methodological standpoint, these heuristic decisions strongly impact both accuracy and speed, and the paper does not provide enough mathematical or empirical detail to justify them.

5. **Evaluation breadth and analysis are limited for an ICLR-level paper.**  
   The experiments are restricted to FSC-147 and CARPK. While these are standard benchmarks, state-of-the-art recent work in training-free counting often tests on additional datasets (e.g., crowd scenes, object counting under different scales / domains). There is no cross-dataset or robustness evaluation, no discussion of generalization to categories unseen in FSC-147, and no analysis of sensitivity to instance size, occlusion, or background clutter beyond very qualitative comments.  
   Also, run-time is given as “1.69 seconds per image on FSC-147” in Section 4.1, but there is no explicit comparison with prior methods under the same hardware. The statement in Section 4.2 that TFCAC is “more than twice as slow” is not backed by a table or experiment. For a work that advertises efficiency as a primary benefit, this lack of quantitative complexity comparison is a noticeable gap.

6. **Positioning relative to the broader training-free counting literature is incomplete.**  
   The related work section mentions TFOC, OmniCount, TFCounter, and TFCAC, but misses several closely related, contemporaneous training-free SAM-based counting frameworks that integrate spatial and semantic information (see “Potentially Missing Related Work” below). These works are conceptually very close to TokenCount, so not discussing them weakens the claim of originality and makes it harder to judge how much of the proposed design is genuinely new versus a variant of the general “use SAM + smart prompting + hybrid similarity” template. This is especially important because TokenCount’s novelty is primarily in the choice of representation (output tokens) and metric, not in introducing an entirely new problem formulation.

7. **Some inconsistencies and confusing statements in the discussion.**  
   Section 5 states that “our method, composed solely of SAM without additional models, demonstrates superior computational efficiency compared to state of the art. This limitation affects scalability for real-time applications and large datasets.” It is unclear what “this limitation” refers to; the sentence appears to contradict itself by first claiming superior efficiency and then saying this limitation affects scalability. There are similar phrasing issues elsewhere that reduce clarity and make it harder to parse the actual practical trade-offs.

8. **Figure usage is sometimes superficial relative to the claims.**  
   While **Figure 1** (Page 2) shows an “attention map for output token,” it is never quantitatively tied to the arguments about semantic/positional entanglement. The figure is essentially an illustration that attention maps light up the “400” digits, which is intuitive, but there is no further analysis (e.g., attention profile vs distance, or comparison to encoder maps). Similarly, **Figure 2**’s bottom row of iterative examples is helpful conceptually, but the exact mapping from those toy grids to the real 64×64 probability map is not described. In other words, the figures support the intuition but are not really used to substantiate more precise technical claims.

Overall, the paper has a solid central idea and some careful ablations, but the combination of oversold performance, incomplete mathematical specification, and limited positioning relative to concurrent work makes it fall short of a clear accept at ICLR.

## Potentially Missing Related Work

1. **Spanakis et al., “OCCAM: Class-Agnostic, Training-Free, Prior-Free and Multi-Class Object Counting,” 2026.**  
   - *Why related*: OCCAM is explicitly a class-agnostic, training-free counting method built around SAM2 and clustering, very close in spirit to TokenCount’s “training-free, SAM-based counting” setting.  
   - *How to integrate*: It should be discussed in Section 2.1 alongside TFOC, OmniCount, TFCounter, and TFCAC, and ideally compared empirically in **Table 1** if code is available, or at least positioned conceptually (e.g., OCCAM focuses on multi-class counting with clustering, whereas TokenCount focuses on single-class counting via output tokens).

2. **Huang et al., “A training-free framework for valid object counting by cascading spatial and semantic understanding of foundation models” (ValidCounter), 2025.**  
   - *Why related*: ValidCounter is a training-free framework explicitly designed to integrate spatial and semantic understanding from foundation models to improve object counting validity, with strong conceptual overlap with TokenCount’s attempt to combine positional and semantic information (via tokens and TS-SS).  
   - *How to integrate*: It should be added to Section 2.1 as a key recent training-free counting baseline and its design should be contrasted with the token-based approach in Section 3 (e.g., ValidCounter uses cascaded spatial/semantic checks over encoder features, while TokenCount uses decoder output tokens and a geometric metric). If performance numbers are available on FSC-147 or CARPK, they should be added to **Table 1** for a fair comparison.

(If the authors are aware of other contemporaneous training-free SAM-based counting approaches beyond those above, they should also be positioned explicitly.)

## Questions

1. **Exact probability update rule in probabilistic prompting.**  
   Can you provide a precise mathematical expression for how the probability distribution \(P(\mathbf{l};\mathbf{e})\) is updated after each iteration, both for positive and negative masks? In particular, what is the scaling factor for negatives, and is it constant across iterations or adaptive?

2. **Construction of the multinomial sampling grid and mapping to prompts.**  
   How exactly are positions in the 64×64 probability map translated into point prompts in the original image space? Are prompts generated at cell centers, or is there some offset / jitter? Is it possible to sample multiple prompts from the same cell in one iteration, and if so, is that desirable?

3. **Sensitivity to the TS-SS threshold and hyperparameters.**  
   The implementation details mention a token verification threshold of 300 and temperature \(\tau\), but there is no ablation. How sensitive is performance (MAE and RMSE) to these hyperparameters? Could you add a small study similar to **Table 3** showing MAE vs threshold and vs \(\tau\)?

4. **Complexity and runtime comparison to baselines.**  
   You report an average processing time of 1.69 seconds per FSC-147 image on an A100. Can you provide comparable numbers for at least TFOC and TFCAC under the same hardware and settings, and break down where TokenCount spends its time (SAM forward, token verification, NMS, etc.)? This would substantiate the claimed efficiency advantages.

5. **Generalization to extremely dense small-object settings.**  
   The discussion and **Figure 8** show that dense small-object scenes are a challenge. Have you experimented with varying SAM’s resolution or prompt strategies (e.g., more iterations with fewer prompts vs fewer iterations with more prompts) to mitigate this, and if so, what were the trends? It would be helpful to know whether the main bottleneck is SAM’s segmentation quality or the token-based verification.

Clarifying these points would significantly increase my confidence in both the method’s reproducibility and the validity of the empirical conclusions.
