## Weaknesses

1. **Limited methodological depth; attack is essentially standard distillation + off-the-shelf generator.**  
   Although the simplicity is partly the point, from an ICLR perspective the core technical novelty is small. Equation (2) is a direct application of cosine-similarity distillation, and Equation (3) just plugs $z_I'$ into an existing Arc2Face + SD1.5 generator. There is no new objective, architecture, or nontrivial theoretical analysis of information leakage. The main contribution is empirical and conceptual (evaluation critique) rather than algorithmic. For a top ML venue, this raises questions about whether the work should be positioned more as a system security case study than as a machine-learning contribution.

2. **Heavy reliance on a specific embedding/generator stack (ArcFace + Arc2Face + SD1.5).**  
   While Section 3 claims that any facial embedding model and compatible generator could be used, all experiments hinge on Antelopev2/ArcFace as the teacher and Arc2Face as the generator. There is no empirical evidence that the attack remains effective with different embedding backbones or different generative models, particularly in settings where no embedding-conditioned face generator is readily available. For instance, if only an embedding model is public but no high-quality embedding-conditioned generator exists, the practicality of the regeneration attack is unclear. This limits the generality of the claim that "any PPFR system leaking identity-discriminative features is vulnerable".

3. **Threat model and access assumptions could be further nuanced.**  
   The main attack (Sections 3–5) assumes oracle access to the conversion process and access to a large corpus of raw unlabeled face images whose templates can be generated. While this is realistic for a malicious provider, many PPFR proposals target cases where conversion happens client-side on trusted hardware or in secure enclaves. Section 2 argues that client-side models can be reverse engineered, but this is asserted rather than analyzed; there is no discussion of how much effort is needed or whether standard obfuscation/TEEs would materially raise the bar. The constrained attack in Section 6 alleviates this somewhat, but still presupposes access to real templates from the specific system and does not fully treat stronger deployment scenarios.

4. **Evaluation relies on proprietary, black-box verification APIs without full calibration details.**  
   The paper uses Face++ and Amazon Rekognition for verification, arguing they provide "financial-grade" thresholds. However, the actual FAR calibration and behavior of these APIs are opaque. For example, Table 2 reports Pass@1e-5 rates based on Face++ thresholds, but it is impossible for readers to independently verify that these thresholds correspond to the claimed FARs on faces outside Face++’s internal deployment domain. The Amazon-based results in Table 5 and Table 7 are only simple pass/fail with unknown operating point. While this does not invalidate the qualitative conclusion that identities are largely preserved, it undermines the quantitative weight of claims like "over 96% regeneration success at FAR $10^{-5}$".

5. **Some experimental design choices may favor the attack and lack ablations.**  
   - All linkage and regeneration tasks are evaluated with ArcFace-compatible embeddings, which match the teacher/student models and the generator. There is no evaluation of cross-model verification (e.g., embeddings from a different commercial FR backbone) to test whether the recovered identities generalize beyond the attack model’s embedding space.  
   - Section 5.1 reports "97–98.7% linkage accuracy" with as few as 256 training images but does not show how performance degrades across a broader range or different identity distributions; the hyperparameter tweaks (weight decay, batch size) are only briefly mentioned.  
   - No ablation is provided on the student architecture (only one Conv2D + Antelopev2), the loss (e.g., comparing cosine loss vs MSE in embedding space), or the impact of different template preprocessing for the constrained attack in Section 6. This makes it harder to understand which parts of the pipeline are essential.

6. **Positioning with respect to broader model inversion and embedding-based attacks is somewhat shallow.**  
   The paper discusses model inversion attacks (Section 1) and tries to contrast them with its method, but the comparison is mostly qualitative. Given the existence of recent diffusion-driven inversion attacks on face embeddings and analysis of embedding invertibility (e.g., works similar to [23,33]), a more systematic empirical comparison would be informative: for instance, applying state-of-the-art embedding inversion directly to templates and measuring whether FaceLinkGen yields clearly better regeneration or linkage. Currently it is asserted that "this is not just another MIA," but not rigorously supported.

7. **Soft-biometric leakage analysis is limited and could be more rigorous.**  
   While **Table 9** is a strong point, the methodology in Section 9 is quite lightweight: a single MLP trained for one epoch on FairFace, with no train/validation split details, architecture description, or analysis of overfitting. There is also no statistical significance analysis between the "Original Image" and PPFR methods’ performance. Given that soft-biometric inference is central to privacy/legal concerns, a more careful experimental design (e.g., confusion matrices, robustness to distribution shift) would strengthen this part.

8. **Missing or incomplete discussion of some closely related PPFR work.**  
   The related work focuses mainly on PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace, OneFace, and CanFG, but it does not discuss other recent frequency-domain PPFR approaches or perturbation schemes that also claim identity preservation / leakage resistance. In particular, relevant 2025 work on component-guided frequency perturbation (see missing related work section) is absent. This weakens the case that the conclusions generalize across the "mainstream" of frequency-domain PPFR.

9. **Paper places most blame on evaluation metrics but proposes no concrete new evaluation protocol.**  
   The paper convincingly argues that PSNR/SSIM are insufficient and that identity-centric evaluation should be added. However, beyond "use commercial-grade FR verification and soft-biometrics prediction," it does not formalize or standardize an alternative benchmark. For example, there is no proposed standardized threat model, attack family, or metric suite that PPFR methods should report against. As a result, while the critique is powerful, the constructive guidance remains somewhat high-level.

10. **Minor mathematical and notation issues.**  
   - In Equation (2), $t_k, i_k$ are said to be "derived from the same source image of identity $k$"; but the index $k$ is also used for identities in the text, and later on in Section 5.2 the experiments explicitly consider different images of the same identity. It would be clearer to distinguish an image index from an identity index, e.g., $(x_{i,j}, t_{i,j})$ for identity $i$, image $j$.  
   - Equation (1) writes $T \sim p(\cdot \mid z_I)$, but the mapping from $X$ to $T$ in the considered PPFR systems is deterministic given $X$ (or at most pseudo-random with a key). The probabilistic notation could be tightened or replaced with an explicit (possibly keyed) function $T = F(X)$ to correctly align with the actual setting.
