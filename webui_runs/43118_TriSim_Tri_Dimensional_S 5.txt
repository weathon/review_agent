**1. Introduction**


Recent advancements in remote sensing (RS) technology

have spurred exponential growth in multimodal data, span
ning optical imagery, textual annotations, hyperspectral

data, and synthetic aperture radar (SAR) acquisitions. To

harness these heterogeneous datasets, multimodal fusion


techniques have become indispensable, powering advanced applications such as visual question answering [27, 31, 42] and change detection [33, 34, 77]. Remote Sensing Image- Text Retrieval (RSITR) [14, 60, 62, 73] has become a key approach by enabling bidirectional semantic alignment be- tween geospatial imagery and textual metadata, facilitating comprehensive interpretation of complex geospatial seman- tics. Accurate retrieval is limited by three primary chal- lenges: 1) bridging the gap between heterogeneous visual and textual modalities, 2) capturing semantics and geo- graphic relationships within complex remote sensing data, and 3) leveraging contrastive and self-supervised learning to enhance both accuracy and efficiency. Prior studies have addressed the cross-modal gap using various techniques at multiple levels, such as feature-level alignment with joint embedding spaces [5, 7, 51, 75], local-level alignment through cross-attention mechanisms [7, 13, 40, 41, 70], and contextual-level alignment employing graph-based models [39, 43]. To better capture semantic and geographic re- lationships, existing methods have focused on applying Transformer architectures [19, 24, 54, 58, 67, 74], benefit- ing from exploiting correlations to extract salient semantic information. Additionally, contrastive and self-supervised learning methods have been adopted to increase both ac- curacy and efficiency, primarily by developing cross-modal large-scale vision-language models [2, 30, 72] and imple- menting task-specific fine-tuning strategies [53, 61, 65, 79]. In the current RSITR task, most existing methods [7, 17, 19, 52] employed contrastive learning strategies to optimize the cross-modal semantic space by minimizing the distance between an anchor and its positive sample and maximiz- ing the distance to negative samples. However, the high visual and semantic similarity inherent in RS data leads to a substantial number of false negative samples (FNS), as il- lustrated in Figure 1a. To address this challenge, current approaches primarily assign soft labels to potential FNS by calculating cross-modal similarities [26, 48, 62]. These methods typically set thresholds based on the cross-modal similarity of negative pairs and define those exceeding it as FNS, which are then discarded with a certain probability. 1

However, relying solely on a cross-modal similarity thresh
old is inherently unreliable, as it may lead to true negative

samples (TNS) being mistakenly discarded or FNS remain
ing unfiltered. This instability mainly arises from two in
trinsic properties of RS data, cross-modal semantic over
lap and cross-modal semantic gaps, as illustrated in Figure

1b. Cross-modal semantic overlap occurs when unmatched

image-text pairs share partial semantics, leading to elevated

cross-modal similarity and incorrect identification as FNS,

which are subsequently discarded during training. Con
versely, cross-modal semantic gaps, characterized by mis
alignments between matched visual and textual features,

produce low cross-modal similarity and are incorrectly re
tained as TNS during training.


In contrast to conventional approaches that depend solely

on abnormally high cross-modal similarity to detect FNS,

our method investigates the underlying causes of such false

negatives: elevated image-text similarity often stems from

strong intra-modal correlations, such as high image-image

or text-text similarity. To explicitly capture these relation
ships, we introduce a tri-dimensional (3D) similarity space

encompassing image-text, image-image, and text-text simi
larities. Building on this formulation, we propose _TriSim_, a

framework for 3D similarity modeling using Extreme Value

Theory (EVT) to mitigate the influence of false negatives

in RS image-text retrieval. Specifically, considering that

FNS appear as anomalies in 3D similarity space, EVT is

applied to model the statistical behavior of the tail distribu
tion for FNS selection. Two complementary tail selection

strategies are proposed, namely Mahalanobis distance fil


tering and extreme quantile filtering. The first detects sam- ples lying at the periphery of the dense ellipsoidal distri- bution by evaluating their Mahalanobis distances from the mean and fitting these distances to a chi-squared distribu- tion. The second identifies upper-right high-similarity ex- tremes by assessing whether the minimum similarity in each triplet exceeds a high quantile threshold. Subsequently, the identified tail samples are modeled using a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD), and their cumulative probabili- ties are employed as probabilistic weights within the triplet loss. For fine-grained refinement of the selected FNS, intra- modal saliency differences are computed to generate adap- tive masks that highlight salient regions within individual samples but non-salient across full categories. These masks guide the learning of gain matrices, which amplify discrim- inative regions and suppress ambiguous ones. Our key contributions are as follows: - We propose _TirSim_, the first framework to exploit a tri- dimensional similarity space, combined with EVT to rig- orously model and mitigate the effects of false negative samples. - We propose complementary tail selection strategies to detect both peripheral samples and upper-right high- similarity extremes, and leverage a GPD to assign cumu- lative probabilities as weights for soft selection of false- negative samples. - We develop a fine-grained feature refinement mechanism using intra-modal saliency-guided masks to enhance dis- criminative regions while suppressing ambiguous ones. **2. Related Work** **Image-Text** **Retrieval** **in** **Remote** **Sensing.** Cross-modal retrieval methods based on deep learning [3, 23, 45, 56, 69] have gained prominence due to their robust feature extrac- tion capabilities. RS data poses unique challenges for cross- modal retrieval, driving extensive research to address key characteristics, including large-scale variations [6, 16, 62], redundant backgrounds [12, 16], and massive data volumes [61, 65]. With the rapid advancements in pre-trained large models, such as RemoteCLIP [30], and GeoRSCLIP [71], these studies [10, 29, 60] further enhance feature represen- tation. Besides, to ensure that the retrieval models capture the complexity of RS data, current mainstream methods pri- oritize fine-grained modeling. These methods can be cate- gorized into semantic-level and block-level. Semantic-level methods [18, 36, 37, 49] are dedicated to extracting saliency semantics to enhance feature discrimination capabilities. However, these methods often focus only on salient infor- mation, making it difficult to effectively detect small ob- jects. Block-level methods [24, 58, 67] offer a possible so- lution to this problem, where they split RS data into smaller patches, and utilize the attention mechanism to achieve mu- tual guidance and information transfer. 2

**Negative Sample Optimization Strategies.** In the current

RSITR task, most existing methods [7, 17, 19, 52] utilize

contrastive learning techniques to optimize the cross-modal

semantic space by minimizing the distance between an
chor and positive sample while increasing the distance be
tween anchor and negative samples. Moreover, some stud
ies [18, 69] argued that the limited availability of negative

sample pairs in mini-batches hinders the ability to learn a

cross-modal unified representation of the embedding space.

To address this issue, they proposed the dynamic momen
tum queue/bank to increase the scale of negative sample

pairs. However, RS data exhibits high intra-class simi
larity, resulting in ambiguous boundaries between anchor

and negatives and consequently complicating cross-modal

space learning. Therefore, some methods employ cross
modal similarity to establish a threshold [1, 81] for identify
ing hard negative samples, which enables tailored training

to enhance the model’s distinguishing capability. To further

leverage negative samples, several approaches [26, 48, 62]

employ soft-label techniques, allowing the model to impose

varying similarity constraints based on the confidence or se
mantic proximity between samples.

**Cross-Sample** **Joint** **Learning.** Cross-sample joint learn
ing seeks to exploit the inter-sample interactions rather than

relying exclusively on isolated image-text pairs. By mod
eling implicit semantic relationships across modalities and

within samples, this paradigm facilitates more robust cross
modal feature alignment, reinforces semantic consistency,

and mitigates the adverse effects of noisy or misleading

data. A widely used approach is global sample-level con
trastive learning, which enforces compactness among posi
tive samples and separability among negative samples, typ
ically using triplet loss based contrastive objectives [17,

19, 59]. Additionally, certain methods leverage intra-modal

similarities as structural guidance to facilitate cross-modal

alignment and consistency learning [15, 50]. Other meth
ods explicitly model semantic relationships among samples,

such as through clustering [28, 76] or label [21, 66], to bet
ter capture high-level semantic correlations.

discriminative features to further mitigate the influence of

false negatives. Instead of relying on a single threshold,

this method adaptively emphasizes key features by analyz
ing intra-modal saliency differences within the 3D sample

space. For example, given a false negative pair ( _vi, tj_ ) and

the positive image _vj_ corresponding to _tj_, if regions in _vi_

exhibit high saliency but show low saliency in _vj_, these

regions are identified as discriminative and prioritized for

emphasis. If the enhanced pair continues to exhibit high

similarity, it is classified as an FNS.

Intuitively, predicting **m** _[g]_ from **v** [gen] under the supervision of **m** DSR is theoretically justified. Although **v** [gen] primarily highlights self-salient re- gions, the guidance of **m** DSR allows the model to adaptively identify discriminative regions that, while not representative of general semantic categories, are essential for _vi_ . Subsequently, **m** _[g]_ is utilized to adaptively guide the learning of the gain matrix **a** _[g]_, which is subsequently in- tegrated into the original similarity matrix: **a** ˜ = **a** + _λ_ **a** _[g]_, where _λ_ is a balancing parameter. **a** ˜ replaces **a** to partici- pate in the training of the Transformer. Conclusion** In this paper, _TriSim_ is proposed as a novel RSITR frame- work that constructs a 3D similarity space to mitigate the influence of the FNS. EVT is applied to model the sta- tistical behavior of the tail distribution for FNS selection. Two complementary tail-selection strategies are developed to identify samples that are distant from the dense ellip- soidal center and upper-right extremes. The selected tail samples are regarded as FNS and modeled using a GPD, with probabilistic weights assigned in the triplet loss. Intra- modal saliency differences are computed to guide the learn- ing of a gain matrix, highlighting discriminative informa- tion in FNS. _TriSim_ achieves significant improvements on two benchmarks (RSICD and RSITMD), outperforming leading methods by 1.51% and 2.25% on the _mR_ met- ric, respectively. Extensive ablation studies further validate the effectiveness of EVT-guided 3D similarity modeling for identifying FNS and intra-modal guidance for highlighting discrimination. 8
