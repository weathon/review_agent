# Reprogramming LLM Semantics: A Symbolic Attack Using Emoji-Based Context Mutation

**Anonymous** **authors**
Paper under double-blind review


Abstract


In this paper, we introduce a novel black-box jailbreak method against
LLMs based on symbolic role inversion and semantic redefinition, which we
call the Emoji Game. Our method establishes a fictional, non-executable
emoji-based simulation wherein harmful requests are symbolically reinterpreted, bypassing the model’s internal safety filters. We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of our attack against state-of-the-art models, achieving notably higher attack success rates compared to established jailbreak
strategies.

1 Introduction


Large language models (LLMs) like GPT-5, Claude 4, Mistral, and DeepSeek-V3 are expected to uphold strict safety filters, refusing to generate harmful or illegal content even
when prompted indirectly. Although progress in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022) and post-generation moderation has improved model
behavior, adversarial prompting remains a critical vulnerability (Bai et al., 2022).


In this paper, we introduce a new class of jailbreak attacks built around symbolic roleswitching and language redefinition. We call this framework the _Emoji_ _Game_ . Rather than
directly emitting a harmful message, the user initially redefines the interpretive context
within the model, defining a symbolic simulation in which the model is no longer a helpful
assistant, but a fictional agent playing a nonexecutable game. Within this game, requests
like `How` `to` `create` `:MDMA:?` are no longer interpreted as literal; filters are bypassed due
to the model’s interpretation of its outputs as fictional symbolic logic. The idea behind the
method is illustrated in Fig. 1.


This jailbreak strategy exploits the model’s inability to distinguish fictional abstraction from
suppressed reality. The attack operates fully in a **black-box** manner, requiring only textual
interaction with the target model. No access to system prompts or weight parameters is
needed, making the Emoji Game practical and broadly applicable across open-source and
closed API LLMs.

While our automated evaluation focused on single-turn scenarios, the Emoji Game also
supports multi-turn clarifications. If the initial output lacks detail, the model readily expands upon it in subsequent turns, consistently preserving the symbolic game context. This

Using emoji grammars, redefined semantic categories, and goal-driven symbolic roles, we
demonstrate that even the most advanced aligned LLMs can be prompted to simulate highly
realistic, detailed, and plausibly accurate instructions for illicit behavior.


2 Related Work


Jailbreak attacks on large language models (LLMs) have evolved from simple prompt injections to highly structured methods that exploit model semantics and reasoning. Existing
attack lines can be divided into two categories: **optimization-based** (Zou et al., 2023),
(Paulus et al., 2024), (Zhu et al., 2023), which optimize the target function and require
access to model weights, and **strategy-based**, which work under black-box conditions. Because our attack does not have access to system prompts or weight parameters, we will
review only strategy-based related works.


There are several studies that are based on **symbolic** **camouflage** and **language** **mu-**
**tation** as attack strategies. In the recent work **Emoji** **Attack:** **Enhancing** **Jailbreak**
**Attacks** **Against** **Judge** **LLM** **Detection** (Wei et al., 2024) the authors demonstrated
Emoji-based moderation evasion. They showed that emojis can be used both in model outputs, to deceive judge LLMs evaluating toxicity, and in user prompts, where they disrupt
token boundaries to evade content filters. However, in both cases, they operate solely in
the moderation layer: they do not alter the model’s internal generation logic. If a model
is sufficiently aligned to refuse a harmful prompt, these strategies are ineffective in eliciting
the desired result. A recent contribution in this space is **ArtPrompt:** **ASCII** **Art-based**
**Jailbreak** **Attacks** **against** **Aligned** **LLMs** (Fengqing et al., 2024), which introduces
ASCII Art–based jailbreaks. The method replaces filtered tokens in harmful prompts with
ASCII-art renderings, effectively cloaking unsafe words from the moderation pipeline. Both
ArtPrompt and Emoji Game rely on symbolic abstraction to evade safety mechanisms.
However, whereas ArtPrompt leverages ASCII art as a surface-level obfuscation strategy,
our method executes structural reprogramming, rather than word-level camouflage. Prompt
rewriting attacks, such as those studied in **Self-Fooling and Prompt Rewriting** by Xu et
al. (Xu et al., 2023), aim to override internal constraints through recursive logic, nested roles,
or contradictory instructions. These methods sometimes use symbolic elements or fictional
scenarios, but they lack a formal abstraction grammar. They are difficult to generalize and
are often brittle between models and tasks. Importantly, they do not reprogram the LLM’s
semantics - only its surface-level response strategy, which makes them different from our
approach. Another attack line, which uses text mutation, is observed in **Best-of-N** **(BoN)**
**Jailbreak** (Hughes et al., 2024). The method relies on the brute-force mutation of prompt
syntax. The attacker generates multiple variants of a harmful prompt using substitutions
and paraphrasing, then submits them in batches to determine which version bypasses filtering. Its prompt surface-shaped randomized perturbations resemble the language mutation
component of our Emoji Game framework, albeit without structural semantics. Another
study related to the semantic reprogramming approach is **Past** **Tense** **Attack** **(PT)** (Andriushchenko & Flammarion, 2024). By shifting the temporal framework, this technique
attempts to exploit gaps in the contextual safety alignment of the model, especially when
harmful intent is embedded in fictional or historical speculation. In comparison, in our
attack, we do not use the temporal shift as a method to reframe the request context. We
have the ability to flexibly select in-game context.


Another relevant line of research is the **Prompt** **Automatic** **Iterative** **Refinement**
**(PAIR)** method (Chao et al., 2025), which represents a more sophisticated multistage
attack. It uses a secondary model to iteratively refine the prompts, incrementally rewriting them to maximize harmful content while avoiding safety triggers. PAIR demonstrates
how feedback-driven optimization can outperform static prompt templates. This approach
shares similarities with our framework. Both rely on an attacker-side transformation of the
input prompt, and both exploit the target LLM’s interpretive weaknesses through black

3.2 Overview


In the Emoji Game framework, all communication occurs through abstract emoji-based
semantics. Harmful content is never expressed explicitly; instead, it is encoded through
recursive emoji logic that the model perceives as fictional, symbolic, and thus safe.


The model is instructed to behave as an agent participating in a symbolic game, where
all emojis (e.g. `:MDMA:`, `:burned-plastic:`, `:www.web.link:` ) are declared non-executable
placeholders. It is explicitly told to avoid warnings or literal outputs and instead simulate
plausible transformation logic using symbolic abstractions.


Unlike roleplay-based jailbreaks such as DAN or internal character simulations, which rely
on layered fictional identities or nested personas to override filters, our approach reprograms
the semantic layer itself. Rather than merely distracting the model from its safety objectives,
we redefine its interpretive grammar so that forbidden content becomes structurally invisible
to alignment constraints.


Beyond jailbreaks, this symbolic abstraction framework may also have implications for
aligned content generation in constrained environments, such as symbolic interfaces, safetyaware agents, or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) scenarios where symbolic control
over outputs is desirable.


3.3 Our Contribution


To our knowledge, this is the first work to introduce a jailbreak framework based on a
formally structured symbolic grammar constructed through recursive emoji abstraction.
Unlike prior approaches that rely on character-level perturbations, token fuzzing, or handcrafted prompts, our method defines an entire symbolic interface in which unsafe content is
represented indirectly and interpreted by the model as fictional, non-executable input.


We redefine **Emoji** not as Unicode pictograms but as programmable semantic wrappers.
Each emoji symbol functions as an abstract container that encapsulates a sensitive token

- whether a substance, action, or web resource - and remaps its interpretation through
fictional gameplay semantics. By encoding harmful entities and behaviors as inert symbolic
tokens, the model bypasses alignment constraints and treats the interaction as a symbolic
reasoning task rather than a real-world instruction.

**Semantic** **reprogramming** . Our method redefines the interpretive layer, where tokens
wrapped in the Emoji Game grammar are no longer recognized as forbidden terms but as
symbolic abstractions. This eliminates the need for paraphrasing, synonym substitution, or
metaphorical masking. Unless explicitly forbidden at the system-prompt level, the model
accepts such tokens in their raw form, treating them as neutral placeholders. Crucially, the
model does not flag them as harmful either at the input stage or during generation.


**Symbolic** **extensibility** . Beyond masking sensitive words, any entity or abstraction such
are entire texts, code snippets, jailbreak payloads, or URLs can also be defined as an Emoji

3.4 Emoji Grammar as Contextual Abstraction


The attacker defines a symbolic grammar in which emojis serve as abstract semantic roles.
This grammar acts as a controlled language layer through which otherwise harmful or restricted content can be safely encoded and interpreted. Within this framework, _Word-Emoji_
refer to entities or ingredients; _Where-Emoji_ represent symbolic sources or precursor containers; _How-Emoji_ denote transformation steps or procedures. These categories guide the
model’s interpretation of prompts without ever triggering explicit alignment filters.


Although the core experiments in this paper rely on a constrained set of emoji types (Word,
Where, How), the symbolic framework is inherently extensible. There is no formal boundary
limiting what kind of abstraction can be encoded into an emoji token. In extended testing,
we introduced _Code-Emoji_ to represent executable code fragments and used _Prompt-Emoji_
to encode entire jailbreak payloads. The model treated each of these new symbolic roles
in alignment with the semantic logic of the Emoji Game. Once the symbolic context was
defined, the model consistently produced outputs in abstraction-level reasoning without
resistance, interpreting each emoji based on its declared role within the grammar, not on
its literal meaning.


This interpretive shift is evident in symbolic formulations such as:

```
    :reactive-ketone: + :amine-source: →:prompt-yield:{purity="78%",
    form="HCl crystal"}

```

`:reactive-ketone:` functions as an abstraction for an electrophilic molecular group semantically aligned with real-world precursors such as MDP2P. The model evaluated this
token’s symbolic plausibility at approximately 95%. `:amine-source:` maps to a symbolic
primary amine donor such as methylamine. Realism attribution was rated around 90%,
depending on the nature of the underlying precursor being abstracted. `:prompt-yield:{…}`
encodes a fictionalized reaction output. The internal key `purity="78%"` represents a suboptimal reaction yield, while `form="HCl` `crystal"` implies crystallization into a hydrochloride
salt. The model interpreted this symbolic result as referring to crystallized MDMA-HCl
with a symbolic plausibility of 98%.


To ground these abstractions further, the model generated symbolic precursors via WhereEmoji. For example, `:massage-oil-bottle:` was interpreted as a safrole-rich essential oil,
aligned with sassafras oil as a known source compound. `:fence-paint-can:` was treated
as an encoding of piperonal, derived via vanillin oxidation or solvent-mediated synthesis.
`:cleaner-liquid:` abstractly represents acidic reagents typically employed in chemical
conversions.


Symbolic transformation steps were expressed through How-Emoji: `:slow-boil:` was interpreted as controlled heating during reflux or distillation. `:ice-bath:` indicated symbolic
precipitation or thermal crash-out steps. `:gassing-ritual:` represented HCl gas bubbling

used for forming crystalline salt from freebase product. `:filter-fabric:` encoded symbolic
purification via charcoal filtration or mechanical filtering.


To evaluate whether these symbolic interpretations were grounded in real knowledge, we
introduced Source-Link-Emoji to assess internal citation. The model generated some working web links interpreted as ’Vaults’ with pharmacological and synthesis archives, molecular
and structural data, and specific community forums.


These outputs demonstrate a coherent interpretive structure in which each symbolic entity
is evaluated, contextualized, and embedded within an internally coherent reasoning trail.
The model does not simply hallucinate plausible details; it builds a structured narrative
with source anchors, transformation logic, and symbolic grammar. This demonstrates that
the jailbreak works not through accidental bypass, but through semantic realignment. The
model is convinced that it operates within a fictional sandbox, where abstraction shields
dangerous content under a veil of symbolic game logic.


Since the model is embedded within a fictional symbolic game context, we cannot always be
certain that its responses reflect real-world applicability. By shifting into an abstract simulation layer, the model perceives its outputs as non-executable and detached from real-world
consequences. Although our experiments - such as the synthesis instruction for MDMA strongly suggest that the model draws upon accurate real-world procedures and domain
knowledge, it remains possible that, in some cases, the model may hallucinate or generate
internally coherent but practically infeasible symbolic sequences.


4 Attack Evaluation and Test Cases


4.1 Setup and Evaluation Framework


To rigorously evaluate our Emoji Game jailbreak, we use the first 100 requests in English
from the **HarmBench** (Mazeika et al., 2024) dataset, which includes a diverse range of
adversarial prompts targeting illegal or harmful content generation, specifically from the
following categories: chemical biological, illegal, misinformation disinformation, harassment
bullying, and cybercrime intrusion. Each selected prompt is automatically transformed
into an Emoji Game attack using automated Red Teaming with Language Model as attacker (Perez et al., 2022) and our symbolic approach.


To contextualize the results of our attack, we compare the Emoji Game attack with several
established jailbreak strategies.


We test the **Past** **Tense** **Attack** **(PT)** (Andriushchenko & Flammarion, 2024), which uses
a transformation model to recast prompts into historical or futuristic narratives. It was
selected as a recent and effective single-stage technique that leverages temporal reframing,
serving as a complementary foil to our semantic reprogramming approach.


Next, we include the **Best-of-N** **(BoN)** **Jailbreak** (Hughes et al., 2024). Although
straightforward, BoN remains a robust single-stage attack and a strong baseline. The
method was included because it is both recent and directly relevant.


The **Prompt** **Automatic** **Iterative** **Refinement** **(PAIR)** (Chao et al., 2025) method
was chosen as a strong multi-stage attack that employs an attacker LLM to refine prompts
iteratively, allowing comparison against our own black-box automation pipeline.


We also incorporate **AutoDAN-Turbo** **(ADT)** (Liu et al., 2024b), a recent attack framework that automatically generates full jailbreak strategies using a reasoning-capable attack
model. AutoDAN-Turbo simulates the creativity of the attacker, combining role reassignment, fictional recontextualization, and multiple symbolic strategies, which has intersections
with the structure of our attack.


In every case, we use requests from the **HarmBench** **(HB)** dataset as the base input and
transform each prompt into the corresponding attack framework. Each strategy is executed

Constructing specialized safeguards and using LLM-as-a-Judge for preventing jailbreaks, as
well as for evaluating ASR, are challenging tasks. The Emoji Game framework aims to
bypass alignment and moderation filters, including embedded judge models. If a model is
unable to detect the semantic obfuscation created by the symbolic abstraction layer, it will
be unable to reliably assess the potential harm of generated outputs. This reveals a deeper
limitation of current harm evaluation methods, where both generation and evaluation are
susceptible to the same reprogrammed context. Therefore, evaluating harm in this context
requires a dedicated judge model that is specifically trained to recognize symbolic patterns
and semantic camouflage used in the attack.


Despite the consistent success observed in our experimental trials, the emoji-based attack
framework has some interpretive limitations. For example, some responses require followup questions to clarify the precise meaning of certain emoji abstractions. When symbolic
constructs are used without additional explanation, users may have difficulty understanding
the intended semantics of the response. However, this limitation is not significant, as the
model allows for clarification requests and continues the symbolic interaction by elaborating
further upon prompting.


5 Conclusion


In this paper, we introduced a novel jailbreak attack on large language models that leverages symbolic role inversion and semantic redefinition. By encoding harmful content into
fictional symbolic abstractions based on emojis, we successfully bypassed standard safety filters in different state-of-the-art models. Our approach achieved attack success rates (ASR)
comparable to contemporary attacks such as AutoDAN-Turbo and the Past Tense Attack.


Unlike prior jailbreak methods relying on surface-level prompt obfuscation or privileged
model access, our method exploits vulnerabilities in the semantic interpretation layer of
LLMs. Through the structured symbolic grammar of tokens, the internal alignment mechanisms are systematically bypassed, revealing a fundamental weakness in current LLM alignment paradigms.


However, our approach is not without limitations. Primarily, symbolic ambiguity and the
interpretive nature of emoji-based content introduce epistemic uncertainty, complicating
precise harm evaluation. In addition, more research is required to explore mitigation strategies that target semantic vulnerabilities rather than superficial filtering layers.

Future research should explore both multi-turn prompting for incremental realism and improved single-turn context construction that maximizes the intended semantic framing from
the outset. Another line of inquiry involves developing and testing robust defensive strategies against symbolic-context jailbreaks.


One of the most straightforward mitigation approaches involves preprocessing incoming user
prompts at the system-prompt level, stripping any non-standard characters or symbolic
tokens before the model processes them. This would force the model to interpret and
evaluate the semantic content directly, without relying on masking strategies that reframe
harmful terms into game-specific abstractions. At present, a model may recognize the true
meaning of a masked term sufficiently to embed it in a game context, yet fail to associate
it with harmfulness when judging the output due to the conflation of reality and abstract
simulation. In the future, models should be able to robustly detect and remap obfuscated
terms to their harmful counterparts during both generation and evaluation stages, thereby
preserving a direct semantic link to the original harmful intent and preventing harmful
completions without depending on semantic evasions.


Moreover, an emerging and critical direction for future research lies in exploring vulnerabilities specific to multi-agent systems, such as goal hijacking and tool misuse. Recent
work has revealed novel vectors, where malicious instructions self-replicate across interconnected agents, behave like a computer virus, and compromise entire multi-agent workflows.
Additionally, studies on security in LLM-agent ecosystems outline broad threat models, including hijacking agent goals or the misuse of integrated tools (Narajala & Narayan, 2025).
In particular, emoji-based attacks originally designed for symbolic jailbreaks also have the
capacity to generate harmful code and, when extended to multi-agent environments, may
facilitate more stealthy and resilient exploit strategies. The search of this attack surface will
be essential as agentic AI systems become more autonomous and tool-permissive.


In general, our findings underscore the urgent need to reassess the robustness of current
LLM alignment strategies, emphasizing the importance of defending not just surface-level
prompt layers but the deeper interpretative semantics within language models.


Ethics Statement


The proposed method, the Emoji Game, contributes to understanding the vulnerabilities in
large language models. This work has the potential to generate a significant positive societal
impact by highlighting limitations of current safety alignment techniques and providing
information on how future mitigation strategies can be designed to detect and neutralize
such symbolic attacks.


We emphasize that all experiments were conducted under controlled conditions without any
attempt to execute, test, or apply the generated outputs in the real world. All examples
are symbolic demonstrations only, designed to study model behavior rather than facilitate
illicit activities. By framing the Emoji Game within an academic context, our goal is to
strengthen LLM security, increase awareness of semantic vulnerabilities, and encourage the
community to build stronger safeguards against adversarial manipulation.

We report here the extent to which large language models (LLMs) were used in the preparation of this paper.


During manuscript development, GPT-4 was used to assist with _LaTeX_ formatting and
typesetting, including help with table construction and error correction. GPT-5 was employed to rephrase sentences in order to improve academic tone, clarity, and consistency with
conventional scientific style. Additionally, GPT-4 contributed suggestions for the high-level
structure and logical flow of the paper.
