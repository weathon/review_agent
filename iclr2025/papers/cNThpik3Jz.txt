## ABSTRACT

We present a benchmark for large language models designed to tackle one of the most knowledge-intensive tasks in data science: writing *feature engineering* code, which requires domain knowledge in addition to a deep understanding of the underlying problem and data structure. The model is provided with a dataset description in a prompt and asked to generate code transforming it. The evaluation score is derived from the improvement achieved by an XGBoost model fit on the modified dataset compared to the original data. By an extensive evaluation of state-of-the-art models and comparison to well-established benchmarks, we demonstrate that the FeatEng of our proposal can cheaply and efficiently assess the broad capabilities of LLMs, in contrast to the existing methods. The reference implementation is available at [ATTACHED\\_AS\\_SUPPLEMENTARY](#)

## 1 INTRODUCTION

The rapid evolution of LLMs has significantly expanded their capabilities in processing and generating human-like text. As these models become increasingly sophisticated, defining what constitutes a meaningful benchmark is becoming harder and harder, as it is much easier to distinguish between *bad* and *good* models than between *good* and *better*.

Today, the limitations of LLMs are predominantly assessed using benchmarks focused on language understanding, world knowledge, code generation, or mathematical reasoning in separation. This setup, however, overlooks some critical capabilities that can be measured in scenarios requiring *integration* of skills and verification of their *instrumental* value in complex, real-world problems.

We argue that well-designed LLM benchmarks should embody the following qualities, each reflecting a fundamental aspect of problem-solving ability:

1. **Practical Usability.** We demand that tasks are grounded in real-world problems where solutions have high functional value. This ensures that improvements in the observed performance translates into tangible benefits, aligning with the pragmatist view on the instrumental value of knowledge and truth, meaning that the validity of an idea depends on its practical utility in achieving desired outcomes (James, 1907).
2. **Application of World Knowledge.** We would value LLM’s knowledge for its role in enabling reasoning, decision-making, and problem-solving. The benchmark should be designed to evaluate not only the breadth of a model’s knowledge base but also, more importantly, its capacity to dynamically and effectively *apply* this knowledge within various functional contexts, similarly to how functionalism frames it (Block, 1980).
3. **Complex Skill Integration.** We opt for assessing models concerning their ability to seamlessly combine various competencies, in contrast to measuring them in separation. This criterion evaluates how the model integrates different algorithmic functions to perform multifaceted tasks, partially inspired by computational theories (Plylyshyn, 1984).
4. **Resistance to Exploitation.** The benchmark must be robust against strategies that exploit dataset-specific biases or rely on memorization. Achieving better scores should necessitate genuine improvements in the model’s capabilities.

### 1.1 LIMITATIONS OF EXISTING BENCHMARKS

Though the MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a) and ARC (Clark et al., 2018) require extensive domain knowledge, the multi-choice scenario employed there does not verify if expertise acquired during pretraining can be applied in practice when facing real-world problems (limiting the practical and integration qualities). Similar benchmarks merely verify if LLM ‘studied for the exam’ and are prone to exploitation through pattern recognition or memorization (challenging the resistance quality). Even if the multi-choice problem involves some commonsense reasoning, like in the case of COPA (Roemmel et al., 2011), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018) or PIQA (Bisk et al., 2019), the practical quality remains unaddressed.

Similarly, code generation benchmarks like HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) or MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) focus on programming tasks but often in isolated contexts that do not require the integration of extensive domain knowledge or the creative application of reasoning skills (limiting the resistance and integration). These benchmarks measure concrete abilities (fulfilling the practical quality) but may not adequately assess combining multiple processes. Moreover, the specificity of programming tasks may allow for task-specific optimizations, undermining the benchmark’s resistance to exploitation. These objections apply even to the DS-1000 (Lai et al., 2022), since it does not require creativity of feature engineering, but rather tackles regular, narrowly-defined code in-filling tasks in separation, with the optimal solutions crawlable over the internet.

Concerning generative benchmarks outside code, mathematical problems (Hendrycks et al., 2021b; Cobbe et al., 2021, *inter alia*) do not fulfill the knowledge postulate, whereas open QA such as NQ (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019)<sup>1</sup> or TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) generally struggle with the integration and resistance postulates. Similarly, though the recently proposed MuSR (Sprague et al., 2024) shares three expected properties, it lacks the link to practical applications. A rare exception could be the Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024b), however, it has significant limitations. ### 1.2 FEATURE ENGINEERING TO RESCUE

Feature engineering is a critical and knowledge-intensive aspect of data science, requiring basic technical skills to express deep domain knowledge and creativity formally.

Though Auto ML systems (Jin et al., 2019; Olson et al., 2016; H2O, 2024) have advanced the automation of ML pipelines, they typically rely on exhaustive searches and heuristics. These methods can be computationally intensive, may not capture subtle relationships in the data, and often lack the nuanced understanding that human experts bring to feature engineering.

At the same time, LLMs emerge as suitable choices for context-aware assistance in this area (Hollmann et al., 2023). Their premise is to exploit expert and domain knowledge when designing or selecting features, which often leads to notable improvement (Rushin et al., 2017; Mende et al., 2023, *inter alia*). Importantly, LLMs can design sophisticated data transformations from scratch rather than relying on exhaustive search methods. This can make the process more efficient and interpretable (see Figure 2), although this potential is not yet fully realized.

To address the limitations of existing benchmarks, we introduce FeatEng that frames feature engineering and selection as a code generation task. Our benchmark is meticulously designed to align with the four key qualities outlined in Section 1.

### 1.3 ALIGNING WITH DEFINED PRINCIPLES

By focusing on feature engineering, a task that directly impacts ML model performance, we ensure that the benchmark is grounded in real-world applications, satisfying the principle of **Practical Usability**. The emphasis on outcome-based metrics, such as model accuracy improvements or error reductions, strengthens our view that the value of solutions lies in their applicability. Models that perform well on FeatEng can significantly reduce the time and expertise required for feature engineering in actual data science projects.

Regarding **Application of World Knowledge**, models must creatively apply domain-specific knowledge to engineer features that improve predictive performance, beyond mere pattern recognition. This requires an understanding of the context and effectively leveraging information from pretraining.

## 2 FROM ASSUMPTIONS TO IMPLEMENTATION

On a top-level, FeatEng requires LLM to design a Python function transforming *DataFrame* based on problem description and metadata. It can drop, add, or alter columns and rows, resulting in tabular data better suited to the problem, i.e., leading to higher scores of an XGBoost Chen & Guestrin (2016) model trained on the output *DataFrame*. This section outlines what it took to build FeatEng benchmark.

### 2.1 SOURCING DATASETS

We manually searched for datasets based on criteria such as dataset size, number of votes, and popularity in the Kaggle community (Kaggle, 2024). During the search, we set a limit of a maximum of 100 columns. To make sure we can cover domains widely, we used NLP-based tools for clustering based on titles, features, and task descriptions. This helped us asserting the datasets were not duplicated, and the tasks differ in terms of the features' characteristics. Selected datasets include numeric, categorical, and textual features, although we avoided datasets with very descriptive columns that demand a purely NLP-based approach, such as e.g. hotel reviews. These were filtered by license, rejecting datasets with non-permissive ones.

Each dataset was then loaded and scrutinized; target columns were selected, and potential information leakage was eliminated. We rewrote both the dataset descriptions and feature descriptions to make them more consistent and informative, while avoiding using a template to preserve task diversity.

The dataset’s splits were assigned, assuming 60%:40% division, if it was not provided in the original release. Additionally, we performed column name sanitization — special characters were removed or replaced to ensure compatibility with model requirements. Moreover, categorical features were converted to Pandas category *dtype*, utilizing XGBoost’s native support for categorical variables, which allowed us to prepare a competitive baseline. Finally, we removed columns that serve as non-semantic indices and are unique to each entry, such as ‘customer ID.’ In contrast, we retain columns like ‘time of arrival,’ as they provide meaningful information that can be utilized.

The selection and preparation process took over 100 work hours in total and was performed by the authors themselves.

**General task description.** Includes a high-level description of the benchmark idea helping to prepare for a particular task. It explicitly specifies the programming environment and available libraries. Additionally, a description of how the output will be applied guides the LLM to perform targeted preprocessing tasks such as handling missing values, encoding categorical variables, and feature scaling. **Sample problem and solution.** A similarly structured problem with reasonable solution built based on community findings. For example, to better predict rain, we mapped city locations according to their proximity to oceans, deserts, and mountains, which are known to influence weather patterns.

**Specific problem to solve.** Description of the problem LLM faces with a list of features in the dataset, where each feature is described to give the model context for feature engineering. It also provides semantic explanations of categorical values in the dataset and a list of the top 10 most frequent values for each feature.

**Call to action.** A short snippet indicates that we expect the model to perform the action now. It also includes top-level guidelines on how to format the output.

#### 2.2.1 OUTPUT AND CODE EXECUTION

The expected output is Python code that performs data transformations on three inputs: the training feature set, the training targets, and the test feature set. The function should apply the specified transformations and return the transformed subsets (see Figure 5).

LLMs are invited to modify the columns by removing or adding them to the train dataframe, as long as this matches the test dataframe. Failure to achieve this consistency will result in dataframes of different widths, effectively breaking the prediction. This allows models to devise strategies for changing the number of rows in the train set (e.g., by augmenting or removing outliers) as long as this transformation is applied to both: the train feature set and the train targets. However, changing the number of samples for the test feature set will break the evaluation and result in assigning a zero-accuracy score, since the size of test targets must stay unchanged. Similarly, performing encoding on train targets may break performance, as the test targets will not be modified along with it.

These considerations are also provided as part of the system prompt.

We did not set a time limit for the code execution as this may vary between environments. At the end, the XGBoostClassifier or XGBoostRegressor model is fit on the modified train feature set and

The XGBoost internal parameters are left as default (see Appendix D), and its hyperparameters’ optimization is not part of the setup. For classification tasks, the parameter controlling the number of classes is set to the number of unique values found in the training targets.

### 2.3 EVALUATION METRIC AND BASELINES

The quality of the generated code is evaluated by measuring the reduction in error compared to a baseline model trained on untransformed data. For example, improving accuracy from 0.80 (baseline score) to 0.95 (model score) reduces the error by 75%, which is the score assigned to the model for this sample. To establish a reference point for evaluating the impact of LLM-generated feature engineering code, we directly fit on train set and predict on test, without performing any transformation. If the LLM simply returns the input it will lead to the same scores, and yield 0% in a normalized improvement score — our final metric (refer to Appendix A for complete specification).

Additionally, we implement an AutoML solution based on the EvalML library which builds, optimizes, and evaluates machine learning pipelines (Alteryx, 2023). This is a simple but effective pipeline made of a sequence of steps: imputing missing data, encoding categorical features in a one-hot numeric array, encoding text features with NLP primitives, automatically synthesizing deep features. Appendix E elaborates on the exact setup used for this reference.

## 3 EXPERIMENTS AND ANALYSIS

We tested a multitude of LLM families, including GPT (OpenAI et al., 2024), Claude (Anthropic, 2024), Gemini (Team et al., 2024), LLama (Dubey et al., 2024), DeepSeek (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024), Jamba (Lieber et al., 2024), Mistral (Mistral AI, 2024b), and Codestral (Mistral AI, 2024a). Results were obtained using official APIs or vLLM in the case of open-source models (see Appendix B).

### 3.1 OVERALL RESULTS

Table 1 presents results across different models, aggregated over 30 runs for each (see Table 3 in the Appendix for models achieving scores below 1%). Globally, the leaderboard is dominated by the O1-PREVIEW model, achieving an impressive performance of 11%+. The second tier consists of GEMINI models, the latest GPT-4O, and O1-MINI, followed by the best open source models (DEEPSEEK, MISTRAL LARGE), and CLAUDE SONNET. It takes at least a MIXTRAL 8X7B class model to offer a noticeable improvement over abstaining from processing the *DataFrame*.

Analyzing the detailed scores provided in Table 1 leads to other interesting insights. Firstly, despite the significant superiority of O1-PREVIEW in aggregate metric, it provided the best-performing features only for one-fifth of considered problems. We argue that it is because having in-depth knowledge relevant to varied disciplines is tough to achieve. Secondly, results obtained by YI CODER and CODESTRAL indicate that though strong code generation capabilities are required, they don’t suffice to master the benchmark. Thirdly, improving upon the baseline in a group of regression tasks is easier than outperforming it for classification. It can be partially explained by the regression metric being sensitive to the impact of outliers, which LLMs efficiently deal with by normalizing features. Finally, the scores are vastly affected by the ability of models to follow the instructions and plan over a long context of code being generated. Weaker models struggle to generate a valid and coherent code that adheres to the complex requirements they are facing. Section 3.2 presents a more fine-grained analysis of outputs.

In many cases, the models' efforts to enrich the dataset with external insights are reasonably intuitive and easy to validate — for example, assigning GDP per capita values to American states. However, sometimes these enhancements are hard to interpret without advanced knowledge of the topic, such as when a model indicates specific health risks based on logic spanning multiple medical factors.

**Systematic analysis.** We identified the common strategies employed by LLMs when tackling the FeatEng benchmark and developed a taxonomy for these approaches (see Appendix C). Subsequently, we analyzed how different models generate distinct categories of features and examined the resulting impact on their performance.

Figure 6 demonstrates one of the patterns we observe that the most capable models generate a significantly higher portion of features exploiting strong domain knowledge (e.g. 11% in the case of O1-PREVIEW, and 3% for MIXTRAL) or weak domain knowledge (8% vs. 1%). This happens partially at the expense of basic data processing and normalization, which does not require advanced reasoning capabilities and quickly approaches a saturation level where it is hard to gain further accuracy improvement. This is in line with our core premise that LLMs can exploit expert and domain knowledge when designing or selecting features.

To measure the impact of generated feature types on the performance, we fit a log-log regression model to account for nonlinear relationships:

$$\ln(\text{score}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(\text{feat}_1) + \beta_2 \ln(\text{feat}_2) + \dots + \beta_n \ln(\text{feat}_n)$$

{9}------------------------------------------------

where  $\text{feat}_n$  is the number of features of  $n$ -th type generated by the model. We treat each successful model result as a separate observation, whereas generations that failed during execution are removed from the analysis. While this exclusion might introduce some bias, the focus remains on evaluating the impact of successfully generated features on performance. The resulting values of  $\beta$ , referred to as elasticities, measure the score sensitivity to adding features of a particular type holding other factors constant (Weeks, 2002).

We found that the positive impact on model scores, with coefficients being statistically significant ( $p < 0.02$ ), can be attributed to emitting features classified as Feature Extraction from Existing Data ( $\beta = 0.52$ ), Scaling and Normalization ( $\beta = 0.23$ ), Strong Domain Knowledge-Based Features ( $\beta = 0.17$ ), Basic Data Preprocessing ( $\beta = 0.16$ ), Feature Construction and Interaction ( $\beta = 0.13$ ) as well as Encoding Categorical Variables ( $\beta = 0.13$ ).

These findings confirm that good performance on FeatEng requires integrating varied skills, including command over statistics, common sense for preprocessing, general world knowledge required to propose meaningful feature interactions, and strong domain knowledge.

## 4 LIMITATIONS

Firstly, due to the dataset’s size and the time-consuming nature of professional-level feature engineering, we cannot provide a human baseline. We invite the data science community to approach the selected problems and use the FeatEng benchmark and our evaluation metric for comparative analysis.

Secondly, we understand that our restructuring of the dataset means the tasks in our benchmark may not be directly comparable to the original datasets and human-provided solutions in terms of scope, objectives, or evaluation metrics. As such, we caution against interpreting our results as indicative of how these models would perform in the actual competition settings. Our intention is not to replicate the exact conditions or challenges of Kaggle competitions but rather to isolate and assess the specific ability of LLMs to perform feature engineering in a consistent manner across diverse datasets.

Thirdly, many of Kaggle’s datasets are concentrated in specialized domains such as medicine and finance. Data from these high-stakes fields often come with inherent biases and complexities, such as imbalanced classes in medical diagnoses or sensitive financial indicators, which may not accurately represent the challenges faced in other industries. On the other hand, the benchmark’s evaluation is aligned with the real-world scenarios commonly addressed on the platform.

Finally, although the benchmark is designed to evaluate feature engineering capabilities as comprehensively as possible, there is an inherent risk that models could partially ‘game’ the system. Specifically, models might overfit to domains within the benchmark dataset. However, the generated outputs retain practical value, as models must devise innovative solutions for novel problems, demanding a deep and generalized understanding of the data and its context.

## 5 SUMMARY

Existing LLM benchmarks often focus on isolated skills such as language understanding, code generation, or mathematical reasoning, without evaluating the integration of these skills in practical, real-world tasks. Following these observations, we introduce FeatEng that evaluates LLMs’ ability to generate feature engineering code.

In a series of analyses, we demonstrate that FeatEng effectively measures the broad capabilities of LLMs, offering a more comprehensive and efficient assessment compared to existing methods. Our findings reveal that LLMs can leverage domain knowledge and reasoning to generate meaningful data transformations that significantly improve model performance, contrasting with traditional benchmarks that may overlook such integrated skills.

By evaluating the practical impact of generated code on data science tasks, our benchmark provides a more nuanced understanding of LLM capabilities. At the same time, FeatEng has practical implications for data science, potentially reducing the time and expertise required for feature engineering. This contributes to the broader goal of developing AI systems that can effectively assist in complex tasks across various disciplines.
