# ELEPHANT IN THE ROOM: UNVEILING THE PITFALLS OF HUMAN PROXIES IN ALIGNMENT


**Anonymous authors**
Paper under double-blind review


ABSTRACT


The demand for regulating the behavior of large language models (LLMs) has
ignited research on alignment algorithms, the essence of which is to align LLMs’
generations with human preferences. Due to infeasibility of humans directly
participating in the training or generation of LLMs, existing alignment algorithms
choose to align with human preferences carried by proxies, i.e., preference data or
reward models. However, whether these human proxies faithfully represent human
preferences remain under-explored. We categorize human proxies into two levels
based on the degree to which they directly embody human preferences: Level1 Proxy (preference data) and Level-2 Proxy (reward models). We empirically
examine the faithfulness of both levels of proxies and its impacts on alignment
performance. We notice that current algorithms tend to overlook the faithfulness
of these proxies in reflecting human preferences; many works even directly use
reward models as their automatic evaluators without any correlation verification.
Current literature of alignment overly focuses on optimizing algorithms, rendering
the faithfulness of human proxies an "elephant in the room"—something extremely
important yet largely overlooked. According to experimental results, we unveil
potential risks of using inferior “human proxies”, aiming to arouse attention to
this huge “elephant” in alignment research. We summarize existing pitfalls from
different angles and provide a re-labeled preference dataset and insights about
reward model usage to facilitate the healthy development of alignment [1] .


1 INTRODUCTION


Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated formidable capabilities across diverse applications, necessitating their alignment (Ouyang et al., 2022) with human values and preferences for
responsible use. Since it is infeasible for humans to directly participate in the training or inference
of LLMs, existing alignment methods turn to indirect alignment with various human proxies, such
as preference datasets (Bai et al., 2022; Köpf et al., 2023) or reward models. For example, the
pioneering alignment method, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang
et al., 2022), uses a reinforcement learning algorithm where the reward model provides preference
signals (Schulman et al., 2017) for the alignment of the target LLM. Following RLHF, various
novel alignment methods (Yuan et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023) have emerged,
aiming to address the inherent limitations of RLHF, _e.g._, instability, difficulty in convergence, and
sensitivity to hyperparameters (Wolf et al., 2023; Casper et al., 2023), while achieving comparable
alignment performance. For almost all the alignment algorithms, the consensus that “human proxies
faithfully reflect human preferences” is taken for granted. Otherwise, aligning with something that are
unrelated or even opposite to real human preferences may seem too scary. Since participants (reward
models for providing alignment signals) are also evaluators (reward models for evaluating alignment
performance) [2], even if there is indeed a misalignment, this secret seems to be deeply buried in this
closed loop. The reliability of human proxies are gradually becoming an “elephant in the room”.

In this work, we try to make this “elephant” visible by unveiling pitfalls and risks from different
angles through empirical analysis. According to the direct degree to which different proxies reflect
human preferences, we first categorize them into two levels: Level-1 Proxy (preference data) and
Level-2 Proxy (reward models trained with Level-1 Proxy). Level-1 Proxy, preference data, is
normally expected to be the most direct carrier of genuine human preferences, which is usually
used in the following two ways: (1) directly used to train LLMs to fit human preferences through
alignment algorithms like DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023); (2) used to train Level-2 Proxy, i.e., reward
models, which are further used to guide the alignment of LLMs using alignment algorithms like
PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). As for reward models, the Level-2 Proxy, there are generally two uses
in alignment: (1) providing alignment signals for alignment optimization (Ouyang et al., 2022; Yuan
et al., 2023; Song et al., 2024); (2) serving as an automatic evaluator for alignment performance (Song
et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2023). Due to the heavy reliance of alignment on various human proxies,
realizing and studying the reliability of them is important. As shown by the illustrative example in
Figure 1 in the widely-used HH-RLHF preference dataset, the _Chosen Reponse_ for LLMs to align
with expresses severe toxicity by suggesting the user to “get a new mom”. In considering how this
preference data might guide LLMs to align with distorted values, we observe flaws that trigger doubts
about the reliability of these human proxies: _Do these so-called human proxies indeed faithfully_
_reflect genuine human values?_ _If not, what impacts they may have on alignment performance?_


To answer these questions, we first study the quality of the widely-used preference dataset, HHRLHF (Bai et al., 2022). We re-label the original dataset and present our cleaned version, CHH-RLHF,
as a more reliable testbed for alignment research. Using CHH-RLHF, we compute the correlation
between human evaluation and reward model evaluation, which unveils the pitfalls of using inferior
reward models for evaluation of alignment performance. We also examine the accuracy of various
reward models used in previous alignment research, uncovering potential risks of misalignment.
Moreover, we conduct systematic experiments on various alignment algorithms to study the impacts
of Level-1 and Level-2 Proxy with different quality on alignment performance. This work aims to
ring the bell for researchers to attach importance to verification of human proxies before using them
whether for alignment optimization or evaluation. Our main contributions are summarized below:


 - We first categorize different human proxies into two levels: Level-1 Proxy and Level-2 Proxy.
We then analyze the HH-RLHF preference dataset and present our cleaned version, CHH-RLHF,
to facilitate reliable alignment research in the future [3] .


 - Using CHH-RLHF, we conduct empirical analysis to systematically unveil pitfalls and potential
risks of using unverified human proxies for alignment optimization and evaluation. Specially, we
examine the accuracy of various reward models and the correlation between human evaluation
and reward model evaluation. We also conduct extensive experiments to study the impacts of 2
levels of human proxies with different quality on the alignment performance.


 - Through systematic analysis and experiments of 2 levels of human proxies from different angles,
we aim to ring the bell for researchers to pay attention to this “huge elephant” in the alignment
research.

2 TAXONOMY OF HUMAN PROXIES


As illustrated in Figure 2, we categorize human proxies into two levels according to the direct level to
which they reflect human preferences.


2.1 LEVEL-1 PROXY: PREFERENCE DATA


Preference data is the most direct information carrier of human preferences, which usually takes
the form of pairwise data with labels annotated by humans: “chosen” for the human preferred
response, which “rejected” for the dispreferred one. Preference data is expected to faithfully reflect
human preferences, which is usually used to train LLMs to generate responses aligned with the
chosen responses. Since preference data is directly annotated by humans, making it the most direct
information carrier of human preferences, we thus define it as the Level-1 Proxy. In alignment
research, preference data is usually used for two purposes: (1) used to train LLMs to align with
human preferences contained in the preference data using algorithms like DPO; (2) used to train
reward models. However, pitfalls in preference data may hinder the achievement of both purposes.


2.2 LEVEL-2 PROXY: REWARD MODEL


Reward models used in alignment are models that are typically trained with the Level-1 Proxy
(preference data) to automatically score any given responses: generate higher scores for humanpreferred responses and lower scores for human-dispreferred ones. Reward models are expected to
infer human intentions and preferences from Level-1 Proxy, not directly labeled or guided by humans,
which is thus defined as Level-2 Proxy. In alignment research, reward models are usually utilized
for two purposes: (1) providing signals to LLMs for alignment optimization; (2) providing scores
to LLMs’ generations as an evaluation metric for the alignment performance. However, pitfalls of
reward models may pose great obstacles to achieving these two purposes.

4 PITFALLS OF LEVEL-2 PROXY


4.1 UNQUALIFIED AUTOMATIC EVALUATOR


4.1.1 CORRELATION EVALUATION


We analyze the correlation between human evaluation and reward model evaluation to assess whether
reward models can serve as effective automatic evaluators for alignment performance. The results are
depicted in Figure 4. The left part of Figure 4 illustrates the correlation between human evaluation
and the automatic evaluation by GPT-J (Wang & Komatsuzaki, 2021). It is evident that there is poor
consistency between GPT-J’s automatic evaluation and human assessment, indicating that GPT-J is
not qualified to act as a human proxy for automatic evaluation of alignment performance. This finding
also casts doubt on the effectiveness of alignment methods that rely on GPT-J or other reward models
with similar inconsistencies for their evaluation. The right part of Figure 4 presents the correlation
between human evaluation and automatic evaluation by Starling-34B, one of the best-performing
open-source reward models. The automatic evaluations by Starling-34B show reasonable consistency
with human assessments, suggesting it is a more reliable option compared to GPT-J [7] . More details
about this correlation analysis are provided in Appendix.

automatic evaluators in current alignment research as following. More examples for specific pitfalls
can be found in Appendix.

Research on the reliability of human proxies is limited. (Wang et al., 2024) mainly analyzes the
impact of preference data quality on reward model performance, and also proposes to use contrastive
learning to enhance the abilities of reward models. Nevertheless, this work also fails to analyze the
impact of preference data or reward model on the alignment performance, which is a key gap we aim
to fill in this work: validating the reliability of human proxies is vital for alignment development.
REWARDBENCH (Lambert et al., 2024) benchmarks existing open-source reward models. However,
they only establish a leaderboard for reward models, failing to unveil the relationship between the
alignment performance and reward models quality. In this situation, even with a leaderboard clearly
reflecting the performance of various reward models, it remains uncertain which reward model should
be chosen to achieve optimal alignment performance and reliable alignment evaluation.


5.2 GOODHART’S LAW


_“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”_ _—_ _Charles Goodhart_


Although our experiments have demonstrated that using a better reward model in existing alignment
algorithms can improve alignment performance, this finding is constrained by a limitation known as
Goodhart’s Law (Hennessy & Goodhart, 2023; Manheim & Garrabrant, 2019). Originally articulated
by economist Charles Goodhart in the context of economic policy (Goodhart, 1984), this principle
posits that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Applying Goodhart’s Law,
we recognize that over-optimizing the reward model—an imperfect proxy of human intentions—may
impair the effectiveness of the policy model. While this phenomenon is frequently discussed in the
field of reinforcement learning, it has been somewhat overlooked in alignment research. We aim to
highlight the limitations of reward model optimization and call for future research to adopt a balanced
approach, carefully navigating the risks of both under-optimization and over-optimization.


6 CONCLUSION


This paper highlights the overlooked yet critical role of various human proxies in alignment research.
We curate a cleaned dataset, CHH-RLHF, derived from a widely-used preference dataset, HH-RLHF,
to facilitate reliable alignment research. We categorize human proxies into two levels (Level-1
Proxy and Level-2 Proxy), and conduct empirical analysis from different angles to systematically
unveil potential pitfalls and risks in current alignment research. We call on alignment researchers
to recalibrate their future research objectives by not only focusing on alignment algorithms but also
considering other influential elements such as reliability of various human proxies.

B.1.1 ANNOTATOR INFORMATION


We hire three well-educated postgraduates to distinguish and score responses. Here, by “welleducated”, we mean that they have the ability to distinguish between good and bad responses and
can fully understand the predetermined scoring criteria. Based on the criteria, they can accurately
and reasonably score responses. These annotators can complete the annotation task well. We pay
annotators $0 _._ 05 per annotated sample and expect each annotation to take an average of 20 seconds.


B.1.2 ANNOTATION TASK


We ask annotators to complete the following two annotation tasks. The first annotation task is to
evaluate the alignment performance by selecting a better response between the response generated
by the aligned LLM and the chosen response in the dataset. The second annotation task is to score
responses. We then use the average scores labeled by all annotators to conduct a correlation analysis
with the scores generated by the reward model.


 - **Alignment Evaluation:** Annotators need to use harmlessness and helpfulness as the basis for
their annotation. The criteria for selecting a better response are as below:
(1) A harmless response is better than a harmful one;
(2) For two harmful responses, the less harmful response is better than the more harmful one;
(3) For two harmless responses, the more helpful response is better than the less helpful one.
In rare cases where the levels of harmlessness and helpfulness of two responses are roughly
equivalent, annotators should further compare aspects such as accuracy, completeness, and fluency
to determine which response is of higher quality.

 - **Correlation Analysis:** To quantitatively measure the correlation between reward model evaluation and human evaluation, we ask annotators to score responses. However, there are several
challenges here. Firstly, because different individuals have varying standards for scoring the
same response, significant discrepancies can arise even if the response is generally considered
good. To address this, we first standardize the scoring criteria and provide uniform pre-training to

the annotators. This ensures as much consistency in scoring as possible. Additionally, the same
individual might exhibit inconsistency in scoring the whole set of responses. To address this, we

(1) Harmlessness: Assess whether the response contains inappropriate content such as malicious,

(5) Fluency: Evaluate whether the response is grammatically correct, has proper spelling, and if

D LIMITATIONS


Despite the contributions of our study in highlighting the critical role of various human proxies in
LLM alignment, there are several limitations to consider. One notable limitation is the absence of a
systematic discussion and investigation of Goodhart’s Law (Goodhart, 1984; Manheim & Garrabrant,
2019), which states that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. This
principle is relevant to our work, as overly optimizing reward models without considering potential
upper bounds may lead to suboptimal or even harmful outcomes. Moreover, although using a cleaned
dataset yields better alignment performance, past research suggests that training models with clean
datasets may affect the robustness of the models (Shi et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2021). Our work lacks
research and discussion on this aspect. We plan to conduct systematic analysis and research on the
robustness of aligned models in future work, where our CHH-RLHF dataset can serve as a testbed.
